# Components research: the wines (5 October 2026)

The master sommelier's research for components/wines.json: the grapes, places, methods, service techniques and producer stories behind the 165 wines in scope (the 34 menu wines and every bottle in a dish pairing's value, classic, splurge and half tiers). Each search below is numbered and records what the results showed, with the pages named. Facts already in the house (the pack's wine cards, the server guide, research/dossier-the-cellar.md and the bottle-facts-cards files of 4 October) are cited from there and not searched again. Anything no search confirmed is either standard wine knowledge a master sommelier holds with certainty, marked "expert knowledge" in the fragment, or is left out and sent to the sommelier.

## Searches (59 in all)

1. Schramsberg, the Toast to Peace. schramsberg.com/about/history and the 50th anniversary page, Wikipedia (Schramsberg Vineyards), PR Newswire: Jack and Jamie Davies bought the Calistoga property in 1965 and revived it for traditional method sparkling wine; Blanc de Blancs was their first wine, in 1965, described as America's first commercial Chardonnay-based brut sparkling wine. In February 1972 the Schramsberg Blanc de Blancs 1969 was poured at the Toast to Peace in Beijing hosted by Richard Nixon for Premier Zhou Enlai; Schramsberg says its wines have been served by every presidential administration since.

2. Judgment of Paris and the rematch. Wikipedia (Judgment of Paris, Chateau Montelena, Ridge Vineyards), ridgewine.com: the Paris tasting of 24 May 1976 put the Chateau Montelena 1973 Chardonnay top among the whites (and a Stag's Leap 1973 Cabernet top among the reds). Ridge Monte Bello 1971 finished fifth among the reds in 1976 and came first in the thirtieth anniversary re-tasting of 24 May 2006, held in London and Napa at once.

3. Pommery and the first brut. Wikipedia (Pommery, Louise Pommery), champagnepommery.com: Madame Pommery created Pommery Nature 1874, described as the first brut Champagne to meet commercial success, breaking with the very sweet Champagne then in fashion (up to about 300 g of sugar a litre for the Russian market). The English market took to the dry style.

4. Bollinger and Bond. jamesbondlifestyle.com, bubbleandflute.com: Bollinger first appears in the Bond novels (Diamonds Are Forever, 1956) and in the films from Live and Let Die (1973), in almost every film since; one of the longest product partnerships in cinema.

5. Pol Roger and Churchill. Wikipedia (Pol Roger), luxurylondon.co.uk, tastingtable.com: Winston Churchill drank Pol Roger from 1908; he befriended Odette Pol-Roger at the British Embassy in Paris in 1944 and she sent him a case each birthday. After his death in 1965 Pol Roger put a black border on its UK labels in mourning; its prestige Cuvée Sir Winston Churchill began with the 1975 vintage.

6. Krug. Wikipedia (Champagne Krug), krug.com edition notes, vinepair.com: founded in Reims in 1843 by Joseph Krug. Grande Cuvée is released as numbered editions (the 172ème Édition and others), counted from the founder's first blend; since 2011 a Krug ID on the back label tells which harvests and plots are in the bottle.

7. Billecart-Salmon. Wikipedia (Billecart-Salmon), champagne-billecart.fr: founded in 1818 at Mareuil-sur-Aÿ on the marriage of Nicolas François Billecart and Elisabeth Salmon; still family owned, run by the seventh generation.

8. Drappier and de Gaulle. champagne-drappier.com (history of the family), millesima-usa.com: the Drappier family settled at Urville in the Côte des Bar in 1808. From 1965 General de Gaulle, whose home La Boisserie was at nearby Colombey-les-Deux-Églises, chose Drappier for his private receptions; the house makes a Cuvée Charles de Gaulle.

9. Taittinger. Wikipedia (Taittinger, Pierre Taittinger), wine-searcher.com: Pierre Taittinger bought the old Reims house of Forest-Fourneaux (founded 1734) in the early 1930s and gave it the family name; the house later moved into the thirteenth century home of the Counts of Champagne in Reims, the source of its Comtes de Champagne name.

10. Rare Champagne. idealwine.com, fr.wikipedia (Rare Champagne), glassofbubbly.com: Rare was first made from the 1976 harvest, a heatwave year, as Piper-Heidsieck's prestige cuvée; 1976 was the first of the few vintages Rare has declared. Its jewelled bottle dress recalls a Fabergé design made for the house in the 1880s.

11. Piper-Heidsieck. Wikipedia (Piper-Heidsieck, Florens-Louis Heidsieck), glassofbubbly.com: Florens-Louis Heidsieck, a cloth merchant from Westphalia settled in Reims, founded the house in 1785 and presented his wine to Queen Marie Antoinette, aiming for a cuvée worthy of a queen. Henri-Guillaume Piper joined after his death, and the names Piper and Heidsieck were joined in the nineteenth century.

12. Moët & Chandon. Wikipedia (Moët & Chandon, Jean-Rémy Moët), millesima-usa.com: founded in Épernay in 1743 by Claude Moët; Jean-Rémy Moët, his grandson, was a friend of Napoleon Bonaparte, to whom the Impérial name pays homage. One of the largest Champagne producers, with about 1,190 hectares of vines.

13. Laurent-Perrier. laurent-perrier.com (history), Wikipedia (Laurent-Perrier, Bernard de Nonancourt): founded at Tours-sur-Marne in 1812; the widow Mathilde Emilie Perrier joined her name to her late husband Eugène Laurent's in the late nineteenth century; bought in 1939 by Marie-Louise de Nonancourt, and her son Bernard de Nonancourt led it for more than fifty years. Still family led and independent.

14. Charles Lafitte. svinando.co.uk, champagnepoodle.com, enviedechamp.com: Charles Lafitte traces its roots to 1834 in Épernay (the successor of George Goulet), and is a brand of the Vranken-Pommery group alongside Pommery, Heidsieck & Co Monopole and Vranken.

15. Sabrage. Wikipedia (Sabrage), fodors.com: sabrage is the ceremonial opening of a sparkling wine bottle with a sabre, linked by legend to Napoleon's cavalry, the hussars. The blade, usually an unsharpened Champagne sabre, slides along the seam of the bottle held at about 45 degrees and strikes the lip, so the collar breaks clean away with the cork in it.

16. Coravin. Wikipedia (Coravin), coravin.com product and FAQ pages: the Coravin system passes a thin hollow needle through the cork and pressurises the bottle with argon, so wine is poured without pulling the cork; a medical device inventor founded the company and it launched in 2013. Coravin Sparkling (2021) is a separate clamp-on stopper and charger for opened sparkling wine, which the company says keeps a bottle fresh and fizzy for weeks. Which device and what house limit applies is a question for the bar.

17. Domaine Leflaive. Wikipedia (Domaine Leflaive, Anne-Claude Leflaive), bbr.com: family roots in Puligny-Montrachet to 1717; Joseph Leflaive, an engineer, built the modern domaine from about 1920. Under Anne-Claude Leflaive (who ran it from 1990 until her death in 2015) the whole domaine was farmed biodynamically from 1997, a lead for Burgundy.

18. Domaine des Comtes Lafon. burgundy-report.com, insideburgundy.com: Comte Jules Lafon built the estate after his marriage in 1894, buying plots in the best Meursault and Volnay vineyards and a piece of Le Montrachet. As mayor of Meursault in 1923 he gave the harvest banquet that became the Paulée de Meursault, now the last of Burgundy's Trois Glorieuses. The family's Mâconnais estate is Les Héritiers du Comte Lafon (idealwine.info).

19. Louis Jadot. Wikipedia (Maison Louis Jadot), hatchmansfield.com: the family, from Belgium, settled near Beaune and bought the Clos des Ursules in 1826; Louis Henry Denis Jadot founded the house in 1859. The head of Bacchus on every label is its emblem.

20. Bouchard Père & Fils. grapeguru.de, wine.com, Wikipedia (Monopole): founded in Beaune in 1731; its cellars are in the fifteenth century Château de Beaune; the Clos de la Mousse, a Beaune Premier Cru of about 3.4 hectares, has been Bouchard's alone (a monopole) since 1872; owned by the Henriot family from 1995.

21. Domaine Huet. Wikipedia (Domaine Huet), biodyvin.com: founded in 1928 by Victor Huet and his son Gaston; three vineyards, Le Haut-Lieu (the first), Clos du Bourg (bought 1953) and Le Mont (bought 1957); all Chenin Blanc, made sec, demi-sec, moelleux and sparkling; farmed biodynamically. Gaston Huet ran it for decades until his death in 2002.

22. Wehlener Sonnenuhr and Joh. Jos. Prüm. visitmosel.de, winewithseth.com, K&L: Jodocus Prüm built the sundial on the Wehlen slate, grey Devonian slate, in 1842 so the vineyard workers could tell the time; the vineyard is named for it. The Joh. Jos. Prüm estate dates from 1911, when the family holdings in Wehlen were divided.

23. Egon Müller. thewineresistance.substack.com, bestofwines.com, Wikipedia (Egon Müller IV): the Scharzhofberg on the Saar belonged to a Trier monastery until the French revolutionary armies seized church land; in 1797 Jean-Jacques Koch bought it at auction, and the estate passed to the Müller family through his daughter's marriage. Scharzhofberger Riesling was served at royal courts.

24. Château d'Yquem. Wikipedia (Château d'Yquem), lvmh.com: the only wine ranked Premier Cru Supérieur in the 1855 classification of Sauternes, above every other estate; the Lur-Saluces family held it from 1785 through a marriage; Thomas Jefferson, then American minister in France, ordered it for himself and for George Washington. Owned by LVMH.

25. Inglenook. Wikipedia (Inglenook, Gustave Niebaum, Rubicon Estate Winery), winespectator.com: founded in Rutherford in 1879 by Gustave Niebaum, a Finnish sea captain. The Coppola family bought part of the estate in 1975 and made the first Rubicon from the 1978 harvest, named for Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon; they reunited the old estate and in 2011 bought back the Inglenook name.

26. The Beaujolais Gang of Four. sfchronicle.com, primalwine.com, Wikipedia (Natural wine): Marcel Lapierre, Jean Foillard, Jean-Paul Thévenet and Guy Breton, called the Gang of Four by their American importer, followed the oenologist Jules Chauvet in making Gamay with whole bunches (carbonic maceration), native yeasts and little or no added sulphur, a root of the natural wine movement.

27. Domaine Tempier. Wikipedia (Lucie Peyraud), winespectator.com, Kermit Lynch's producer sheet: Lucien Peyraud and Lucie Tempier (Lulu) took on the family farm at Le Plan du Castellet near Bandol in 1936; Lucien worked to have Bandol made its own appellation in 1941 and championed Mourvèdre. Lulu's Provençal cooking inspired Alice Waters of Chez Panisse; she died in 2020 aged 102.

28. Domaine du Vieux Télégraphe. vieux-telegraphe.fr (histoire), Wikipedia, wine-searcher.com: Hippolyte Brunier planted on the La Crau plateau in 1898, a high point where Claude Chappe's signal telegraph tower had stood since 1793 on the Paris to Marseille line; La Crau is covered in large round stones, galets roulés; the family still runs it and plays on the name: its newsletter is La Sémaphore and its younger wine Télégramme.

29. Château de Beaucastel. Wikipedia (Château de Beaucastel), grapeguru.de, tablascreek.com: the Perrin family has run Beaucastel since 1909; it grows all thirteen grape varieties permitted in Châteauneuf-du-Pape (among them Mourvèdre, Grenache, Syrah, Cinsault, Counoise and Roussanne), gives Mourvèdre a defining role, and has farmed organically since about 1950. The tablascreek.com history page tells how the Perrin family and their American importer founded Tablas Creek at Paso Robles in California with Rhône vine cuttings.

30. La Rioja Alta and the 904. riojalta.com (history), Wikipedia (La Rioja Alta): founded on 10 July 1890 in the Station Quarter of Haro by five local families; in 1904 it merged with Alfredo Ardanza's winery and marked the merger with a Reserva 1904, which became the Gran Reserva 904 (the 890 marks the founding year); the first digit is dropped so the name is not read as a vintage. The 904 is about 90 per cent Tempranillo and 10 per cent Graciano.

31. Taylor Fladgate and Vargellas. taylor.pt, kobrandwineandspirits.com: the firm's story begins in 1692 with an English merchant, Job Bearsley; it bought Quinta de Vargellas in the upper Douro in 1893. In good years when no full vintage is declared, Taylor's bottles Quinta de Vargellas on its own as a single quinta Vintage Port.

32. Sandeman. Wikipedia (Sandeman), 1stdibs and postergroup poster listings: founded in London in 1790 by the Scot George Sandeman to ship Port and sherry. The Don, the caped figure in a Portuguese student's cape and a Spanish hat, was drawn in 1928 by George Massiot Brown and is called the oldest wine logo still in use.

33. Niepoort. grapeguru.de, prtwine.com, winewithseth.com: a Port house of Dutch origin founded in 1842 in Vila Nova de Gaia, family run for five or six generations; known above all for Colheitas, single harvest tawny Ports aged for years in cask, and now for Douro table wines too.

34. The Symingtons, Graham's and Dow's. symington.com, grahams-port.com, Wikipedia (Graham's, Symington Family Estates): Andrew James Symington came from Scotland to Porto in 1882 to work for Graham's, became a partner in Warre's and in 1912 in Dow's; the family bought Graham's in 1970. Graham's bought Quinta dos Malvedos in 1890. The family runs Graham's, Dow's, Warre's and Cockburn's.

35. Vin Jaune. Wikipedia (Vin jaune), decanter.com (Vin Jaune explained), jura-tourism.com: all Savagnin, aged at least six years and three months in barrel under a veil of yeast without topping up; bottled in the 62 cl clavelin, said to be what is left of a litre after the years of evaporation; walnut, curry spice and toasted bread.

36. Madeira. madeirawineguide.com, winewithseth.com (Madeira styles; Malmsey), diffordsguide.com: Madeira is fortified and then warmed, either quickly in heated tanks (estufagem) or slowly in casks in the warm lofts of the lodge (canteiro), the slower way kept for the finest wines; Malvasia (Malmsey) is the sweetest style, balanced by high natural acidity.

37. Inniskillin. inniskillin.com (our story), Wikipedia (Inniskillin, Ice wine): given, as the house and Wikipedia tell it, the first winery licence since Prohibition, on 31 July 1975, at Niagara-on-the-Lake; its 1989 Vidal Icewine won the Grand Prix d'Honneur at Vinexpo in Bordeaux in 1991, the breakthrough for Canadian icewine.

38. Trimbach. Wikipedia (Maison Trimbach), idealwine.net: founded in 1626 by Jean Trimbach, first at Riquewihr; settled at Ribeauvillé after the First World War; thirteen generations of the family; best known for bone dry Riesling, above all the Clos Sainte Hune.

39. Château d'Esclans and Whispering Angel. esclans.com, Wikipedia (Château d'Esclans): the estate was bought in 2006 to make great rosé; the name Whispering Angel came from a carving of two cherubs above the altar of the château's chapel that looked as if they were whispering, and the label carries a line drawing of them. Now part of LVMH.

40. Château Minuty. Wikipedia (Château Minuty), sainttropeztourisme.com, hachette-vins.com: at Gassin above the Gulf of Saint-Tropez, with vineyards on the hills of Gassin and Ramatuelle; bought by Gabriel Farnet in 1936 and held by his descendants, the Matton family; one of the crus classés of Côtes de Provence named in 1955; its rosés are built on Grenache.

41. Domaine Saint Damien, La Louisianne. K&L Wines, weygandtmetzler.com, gigondas-vin.com: a Gigondas family estate farmed by the Saurel family since the early nineteenth century, certified organic since 2012. La Louisiane (the house prints La Louisianne) is named for a lieu-dit, a named parcel, on the middle terraces; no source explains why the parcel carries the name, so any link to Louisiana is a question for the sommelier, never a fact to tell a guest.

42. Brennan's Grand Award. fsrmagazine.com, theadvocate.com, whereyat.com: Brennan's holds Wine Spectator's Grand Award, named again for 2025 alongside two other New Orleans restaurants; the reports describe a cellar of about 3,000 selections, deep in Burgundy, Bordeaux and California.

43. Pichon Lalande. Wikipedia (Château Pichon Longueville Comtesse de Lalande), thewinecellarinsider.com: the old Pichon Longueville estate was divided in 1850, and the share run by Virginie, Comtesse de Lalande, took her name; the Miailhe family bought it in 1925; it was sold to the owners of Champagne Louis Roederer in January 2007.

44. Château Talbot. decanter.com, firstleaf.com, Wikipedia (John Talbot, 1st Earl of Shrewsbury): named for John Talbot, the English commander killed at the Battle of Castillon in 1453, the battle that ended English rule in Bordeaux, and Shakespeare's Old Talbot; a fourth growth of 1855 in Saint-Julien; the Cordier family has owned it since 1918.

45. Château d'Issan. Wikipedia (Château d'Issan), chateau-issan.com (history), the château's own post: a moated château in Margaux; its motto, engraved over the door, reads Regum mensis arisque deorum, for the tables of kings and the altars of the gods; the house says wine from the site was served at the wedding of Eleanor of Aquitaine and Henry Plantagenet in 1152 (a legend, told as one). Moulin d'Issan comes from d'Issan vineyards on low ground outside the Margaux appellation, hence Bordeaux Supérieur.

46. Duckhorn. Wikipedia (The Duckhorn Portfolio), napawineproject.com: founded in 1976 by Dan and Margaret Duckhorn; first vintage 1978, including a Merlot from the Three Palms Vineyard; the Three Palms Merlot was Wine Spectator's Wine of the Year in 2017.

47. Ridge Lytton Springs. ridgewine.com (Lytton Springs vineyard, field blend notes), sonomawinegrape.org, wineanorak.com: the oldest block was planted in 1901 as a field blend, Zinfandel interplanted with Petite Sirah, Carignane and other varieties; Ridge first made Lytton Springs in 1972 and bought the vineyard in the early 1990s. A typical blend is about three quarters Zinfandel with Petite Sirah and Carignane.

48. Kracher. corneyandbarrow.com, kracher.at, vintageandvine.com: the estate lies between Illmitz and the shore of the Neusiedlersee in Burgenland. Autumn mists rising off the lake and the shallow pools around it, burned off by afternoon sun, bring noble rot almost every year. Alois Kracher made its sweet wines world famous from the late 1980s until his early death in 2007.

49. Santorini and Gaia. Wikipedia (Assyrtiko), winewithseth.com (kouloura), wine.com product notes, gaiawines.gr: Santorini's vines are trained low into baskets, kouloura, against the wind, on volcanic pumice soils that phylloxera never reached, so many vines are ungrafted and very old; Assyrtiko there is bone dry, high in acidity and saline. Gaia's Wild Ferment is fermented with indigenous yeasts and part aged in a mix of steel, oak, acacia and ceramic.

50. Quintarelli. dobianchi.com, idealwine.info, superiore.de: the estate in Negrar dates from 1924; Giuseppe Quintarelli (Bepi, 1927 to 2012) ran it from the early 1950s and became the benchmark traditionalist of Valpolicella, drying grapes for months (appassimento) on racks and in crates, ageing long in large casks and refusing to release in weak years; his family carries it on. Recioto della Valpolicella became a DOCG in 2009.

51. Château Gilette. thewinecellarinsider.com, greatbordeauxwines.com: a tiny estate of about 4.5 hectares in Preignac owned by the Médeville family; its Crème de Tête stays in small concrete vats for close to twenty years and is released twenty years or more after the harvest, never touching oak.

52. English sparkling wine. Wikipedia (English sparkling wine), worldoffinewine.com (Chalk Talk), wine-searcher.com: the chalk of the South Downs and Kent is the same chalk seam that runs under Champagne; Ridgeview near Ditchling in Sussex dates from the mid 1990s; Chapel Down at Tenterden in Kent is England's largest producer. Ridgeview's Grosvenor Blanc de Blancs won Decanter's International Trophy for sparkling wine in 2010, the only wine from outside Champagne to have won it.

53. Moscadello di Montalcino. Wikipedia (Moscadello di Montalcino, Brunello di Montalcino), tenutedelcerro.it: before Brunello, Montalcino's famous wine was a sweet white from Moscato Bianco; DOC since 1984; late harvest versions taste of dried fruit and honey. Castello Banfi was founded by the Mariani family.

54. Argyle. argylewinery.com (about), oregonwinehistory.com, princeofpinot.com: founded in 1987 in Dundee, in a former nut processing plant, with sparkling wine its signature from the start; it farms in the Dundee Hills and Eola-Amity Hills of the Willamette Valley.

55. Château Climens. Wikipedia (Château Climens), winespectator.com, idealwine.info: a first growth of Barsac in 1855, planted only to Sémillon, farmed biodynamically, called the Yquem of Barsac or the Lord of Barsac; the Lurton family owned it from the 1970s and sold a majority stake to the Moitry family in 2022.

56. Domaine du Pegau. pegau.com (history), thewinecellarinsider.com, winewithseth.com: Pégau is old Provençal for a wine jug, one found in the papal palace at Avignon; the Féraud family has farmed in Châteauneuf since the seventeenth century; Paul Féraud and his daughter Laurence named the domaine Pegau in 1987. The Cuvée Réservée is so called because the family sold most of its wine in bulk and reserved a small share to bottle themselves.

57. Live and Let Die in New Orleans. onthetracksof007.com, jamesbond.fandom.com (Olympia Brass Band), Wikipedia (Jazz funeral): the 1973 Bond film was shot partly in the French Quarter, where the Olympia Brass Band leads a jazz funeral at the corner of Chartres and Dumaine; this is the film in which Bond first orders Bollinger on screen (search 4).

58. Paul Hobbs and Coombsville. paulhobbswinery.com, napavalley.wine, prweb.com: Paul Hobbs founded his winery in 1991 at Sebastopol; his Napa estate in the Coombsville district is named for Nathan Coombs, the founder of the city of Napa; Coombsville is one of Napa's cooler corners, near San Pablo Bay, which helps even ripening.

59. Muscadet and Pépière. thewinedoctor.com, primalwine.com, winewithseth.com: Marc Ollivier founded Domaine de la Pépière near Maisdon-sur-Sèvre in 1984 and farmed organically from the start; the domaine has vines in four of the Muscadet crus communaux (Clisson, Château-Thébaud, Monnières-Saint Fiacre and Gorges), and its cru wines rest on their lees well beyond the 17 months the crus require.

## Standard wine knowledge used (expert knowledge)

The methods and rules a master sommelier states with certainty and that need no search: the traditional method (second fermentation in bottle, riddling, disgorging, dosage); the Champagne dosage terms (Brut Nature, Extra Brut up to 6 g a litre, Brut up to 12 g, Demi-Sec 32 to 50 g); Crémant made by the same method outside Champagne; the minimum lees ageing for non-vintage (15 months) and vintage (three years) Champagne; the German Prädikat ladder (Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese, Eiswein) ranked by ripeness at harvest; botrytis (noble rot); mutage, the stopping of fermentation with grape spirit for Port and vin doux naturel; the solera and flor for sherry; carbonic maceration; malolactic conversion; Burgundy's four tiers (regional, village, Premier Cru, Grand Cru); the 1855 Médoc classification in five growths; the Wachau's Steinfeder, Federspiel and Smaragd; Rioja's Crianza, Reserva and Gran Reserva ageing minimums; the Southern Rhône's Grenache, Syrah and Mourvèdre; decanting for sediment or air; serving temperatures; the sizes of the half-bottle (375 ml), magnum (1.5 L) and double magnum (3 L).

## Expert knowledge glossary

Names and terms the fragment uses as standard wine knowledge, with no search needed, listed here so every name stands in a source: the Port grapes Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz, Tinta Barroca and Tinto Cão; the synonyms Monastrell and Mataro (Mourvèdre), Primitivo (Zinfandel), Vinho Verde (the Portuguese neighbour of Rías Baixas), and Peloursin (with Syrah, a parent of Petite Sirah); the Champagne Grand Cru villages Verzenay and Cramant; Crémant de Limoux, in the Languedoc; the large bottle names Jeroboam, Rehoboam and Methuselah, Biblical kings; temperatures in degrees Celsius; Rudolf Steiner, whose lectures of the 1920s began biodynamic farming; the Vosges mountains and the town of Riquewihr in Alsace; Chiroubles among the ten crus of Beaujolais; the Hospices de Beaune and its November auction; Santiago de Compostela at the end of the pilgrim road through Bierzo; the Kimmeridgian limestone and clay of Chablis and the Aube; the Pope at Avignon behind the name Châteauneuf-du-Pape; the American Viticultural Area, the United States appellation system; Madeira off the coast of Morocco; Koblenz, where the Mosel meets the Rhine; Mainz on the Rhine at the head of Rheinhessen; the Dordogne river of the Right Bank; the Garonne and the cold Ciron of Sauternes; Crottin de Chavignol goat cheese; Jerez de la Frontera; Fumane and San Pietro in Cariano in Valpolicella Classico, the Lessini mountains above it, and Ripasso among its wines; Portland and the Cascades around the Willamette Valley; Les Forts de Latour and Carruades de Lafite, second wines of Pauillac first growths; Tokaji, Hungary's sweet wine; Switzerland and Slovenia as neighbours of the Jura and of Welschriesling's homelands; the Paris Exposition of 1855 called by Emperor Napoleon III; the Hollywood film of the Paris tasting; the ancient Greek settlers who founded Marseille.

## Repairs after the refuter (4 Oct, late)

- Artichoke pairing: the menu prints crispy artichokes, Parmesan creamed spinach and Choron sauce for the Eggs Sardou, not hollandaise (pack dish description).
