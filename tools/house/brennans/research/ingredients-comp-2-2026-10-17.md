# Ingredient components, chunk comp-2 (the first seven Entrées), 17 October 2026

The staple ingredients and techniques behind Eggs Hussarde, Eggs Sardou, Eggs Owen, the Duck Confit Waffle, New Orleans Shrimp & Grits, Pecan Gulf Fish and the Crab & Corn Omelette: eggs, butter, shallots, garlic, parsley, tarragon, red wine, mushrooms, spinach, tomatoes, potatoes, chives, wheat flour, mustard, basil, beef short rib, vinegar and mayonnaise, with curing, compound butter and stock as techniques. Written to components/_staging/comp-2.json. The general culinary facts in those cards (what a shallot is, how a cure works, what a stock is) are a cook's common knowledge and name no maker; the one producer profile, the origin of Dijon mustard, rests on the facts below. The menu prints mustard twice (Duck Confit Waffle, Poussin à la Moutarde) and names no kind or maker.

## 1. Dijon mustard, the origin behind the mustard component

Facts, as the sources say them, with a reading date of 17 October 2026:

- The modern style of Dijon mustard dates from 1856, when Jean Naigeon of Dijon replaced the vinegar in his mustard with verjuice, the sour juice of unripe grapes. Today's Dijon is brown mustard seed (Brassica juncea) ground with white wine, vinegar, water and salt in place of that verjuice. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dijon_mustard ; https://www.cooksinfo.com/dijon-mustard ; https://tastingtable.com/1192664/the-french-origins-of-dijon-mustard
- The name is not protected. The decree of 10 September 1937 defines Dijon mustard by its production method (brown or black seed, the liquid), not as an appellation, so it may be made anywhere the method is followed; the EU holds the term generic, and neither a PDO nor a PGI covers it. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dijon_mustard ; https://www.cooksinfo.com/dijon-mustard (the 1937 decree, amended 2000) ; https://www.chowhound.com/1488620/why-called-dijon-mustard/ . Checked again on 17 October 2026: the card says the decree defined the mustard by its method, not its town.
- France needs about 35,000 tonnes of mustard seed a year and imports about 80 per cent of it from Canada, from the prairies of Alberta and Saskatchewan. Drought there halved the crop in the 2021 season, and French shelves ran short of Dijon mustard in 2022, made worse by stockpiling. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dijon_mustard ; https://afriendlyletter.com/cant-cut-it-the-french-mustard-crisis-in-france/ ; https://kitchensense.substack.com/p/issue-68-mustard-shortage-solved . Checked again on 17 October 2026: the 35,000 tonnes and the 80 per cent from Canada are repeated by the French press (https://www.lesoleil.com/2022/09/10/moutarde-de-dijon-la-relocalisation-comme-remede-a-la-penurie-481fd653007a6f97c968b44c88305213 , AFP; https://www.linfodurable.fr/changement-climatique-inflation-et-penuries-la-moutarde-de-dijon-en-peril-30030 ), and the halved 2021 Canadian harvest by https://www.thelocal.fr/20220914/confident-for-christmas-how-france-plans-to-tackle-mustard-shortages ; Canada shipped 157 tonnes of seed to France in 2021, down 80 per cent on 2020 (CNN, as carried by https://kvia.com/?p=1020928 ). Burgundy's own seed fell from 12,000 tonnes in 2017 to 4,000 in 2021 (AFP, Le Soleil); not on the card.
- The protected name is Moutarde de Bourgogne, an IGP (PGI) recognised by the INAO and registered with the EU on 24 November 2009. It must be made with mustard seed grown and stored in Burgundy (Côte-d'Or, Nièvre, Saône-et-Loire and Yonne) and with at least 25 per cent Bourgogne PDO dry white wine, from Aligoté and Chardonnay. Edmond Fallot of Beaune makes an IGP Moutarde de Bourgogne with Bourgogne Aligoté. https://www.tastefrance.com/essentials/sauces-condiments/pgi-burgundy-mustard ; https://extranet.inao.gouv.fr/fichier/CDCMoutardeDeBourgogne-210308.pdf ; https://igourmet.com/products/burgundy-mustard-by-edmond-fallot . Checked again on 17 October 2026: the INAO sheet (https://www.inao.gouv.fr/node/5984/printable/pdf ) gives the 2009 recognition, seed grown only in Burgundy (Côte-d'Or, Nièvre, Saône-et-Loire, Yonne) and AOP Bourgogne white wine, but the 25 per cent share could not be read from it, and a French listing of the Fallot jar gives Bourgogne Aligoté at 16.3 per cent of the finished mustard (https://www.achatmoinscher.com/comparateur-de-prix-Epicerie/moutarde-de-bourgogne-igp-edmond-fallot-le-pot-des-210-g.html ), so the share is not on the card; it says seed grown in Burgundy and Bourgogne white wine, Aligoté at Fallot. The current specification is the one under BO-Agri notice 2023-26 (https://info.agriculture.gouv.fr/boagri/document_administratif-a7264c06-0a0b-47ee-8f17-4114f5fd0f51/telechargement ). Gault&Millau calls the Fallot mustard an AOP; the INAO says IGP, and the card follows the INAO.
- Maille, the oldest mustard name, was founded in Paris in 1747 by Antoine-Claude Maille, a supplier to the court of Louis XV; the company passed to Maurice Grey of Grey Poupon in 1885. The founding date of Grey Poupon itself is given as 1777 by one source and 1866 by another, so no Grey Poupon date is printed on the card. Checked again on 17 October 2026: the Paris boutique on the rue Saint-André des Arts and the Louis XV supply are repeated by Maille's own history page (https://maille.com/ca/en/history ); Wikipedia gives 1885 for Maurice Grey's purchase and Chip Paper gives 1880, so the year is not settled and the card keeps 1885, Wikipedia's; Maille was bought by Baron Philippe de Rothschild in 1930 and sold in 1952 to André Ricard and Joseph Poupon, and Unilever owned it by 2000 (not on the card). The card no longer calls Maille the oldest mustard name, since Meaux and other houses claim older dates; it says the best-known old name. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maille_(company) ; https://artofeating.substack.com/p/french-mustard ; https://chippaper.substack.com/p/condiment-curiosity-maille-dijon
- Brennan's: the guide prints mustard on the Duck Confit Waffle (mustard maple syrup, pdf p20) and the Poussin à la Moutarde (mustard cream sauce, pdf p27), and its step-up line on the poussin calls Burgundy "Dijon mustard's home" (guide.txt, the Poussin card). The kitchen dossier reads the mustard maple syrup as maple sharpened with Dijon or whole-grain (dossier-the-kitchen.md, section 6). Neither says which mustard the kitchen uses, so the card says mustard, as printed, and asks the chef.

## 2. Respellings used in the chunk

- Glace de viande: GLAHS duh vee-AHND (the stock and glace technique card). French final consonants silent, the stress on the last word, in the style of the pack's burr BLAHN and mar-SHAHN duh VAN.

## 3. What the house itself says, used as the ground of the staple cards

- Marchand de vin: the guide calls it "a red wine and mushroom sauce" (pdf p2) and "a savory red wine reduction" (pdf p17); Brennan's own 1994 cookbook builds it as a roux-based brown sauce of butter, ham, mushrooms, onion, shallot and garlic, flour, beef stock and red wine (dossier-the-kitchen.md, section 6, Eggs Hussarde). The current kitchen's build is not public, so the shallot, garlic, mushroom and stock cards each send the server to the chef.
- Choron: "a béarnaise, butter, egg yolk, shallot and tarragon, with tomato folded in" and "the vinegar in the sauce" (guide.txt, Eggs Sardou card). Béarnaise on the breakfast hanger: "butter, egg yolk, shallot and tarragon" (guide.txt, Creole-Spiced Hanger Steak card).
- Pecan bordelaise: the guide says the classic French sauce is red wine based and in New Orleans the word often means garlic butter (pdf p22); the dossier reads the Creole garlic butter as butter, olive oil, garlic and parsley (section 6, Pecan Gulf Fish). The garlic, parsley and compound butter cards keep that as a reading to confirm.
- Brabant potatoes: "tossed with garlic and parsley", "garlic, butter and parsley" (guide.txt, Brabant Potatoes card and service note).
- Creole sauce: the trinity with garlic, tomatoes, stock, bay, thyme and cayenne (components/dishes.json, creole-sauce; dossier-the-kitchen.md, Shrimp & Grits).
- Eggs: Vital Farms is printed on the Creole-Spiced Hanger Steak, and the fall training sheet prints a Vital Farms Egg Yolk on the Steak Tartare with Rye (pages/brennans-fall-2026-training-sheets-2026-10-15.txt); whose eggs go into the other egg dishes is an open lineup question (producers-food-beef-eggs-duck-2026-10-06.md, section 5).
- Mustard: the chive rémoulade on the Crawfish Meat Pies carries mustard in either family, the French one being mayonnaise with mustard and the Creole one built on Creole mustard (components/dishes.json, remoulade-chive), so the mustard card reaches the pies too.
- Wheat flour: the house's Roux card reaches Eggs Owen and the South Louisiana Rice Dressing (components/dishes.json, roux: New Orleans marchand de vin is often roux-bound; rice dressing often carries a little roux), and the Popcorn shrimp card says the shrimp are dredged in seasoned flour or a light batter (components/dishes.json, popcorn-shrimp), so the wheat flour card reaches those five dishes as likely, with the guide's standing instruction to ask the kitchen.
- Short rib: the Roost Burger's "2 Beef Short Rib Patties" and the Eggs Owen's débris name no producer; Creekstone is printed on the two hangers and the Chateaubriand only (producers-food-beef-eggs-duck-2026-10-06.md, sections 1 and 5).

## 4. Fact check of the chunk, 17 October 2026

Every component of comp-2.json was read against the menu lines, the guide, the kitchen dossier and the producer research; the Dijon origin facts were checked again on the web the same day. What changed, and the sources read:

- Naigeon and 1856: as the encyclopaedia page says it (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dijon_mustard ); the attribution is secondary, so the card keeps the source's own wording, "dates from 1856, when Jean Naigeon of Dijon replaced the vinegar with verjuice".
- The 1937 decision: the designation was held to be a recipe, not a place, so the mustard may be made anywhere the method is followed (https://www.cooksinfo.com/dijon-mustard ; https://www.connexionfrance.com/article/Archive/Just-don-t-call-it-French-mustard ). The card's clause "and most of it is" (made away from Dijon) stood in no source and is struck; the card now says only that it can be made anywhere.
- 35,000 tonnes of seed a year, about 80 per cent from Canada, the Alberta and Saskatchewan drought that halved the 2021 Canadian harvest, and the 2022 shortage: AFP as carried by The Financial Express, 21 May 2022 (https://thefinancialexpress.com.bd/world/france-faces-shortage-of-mustard-price-increases-by-10pc-1653146317 ); NPR, 23 August 2022 (https://www.npr.org/2022/08/23/1119103549/french-supermarkets-have-been-low-on-mustard-the-main-cause-stems-from-canada ); https://afriendlyletter.com/cant-cut-it-the-french-mustard-crisis-in-france/ . One account gives Canada's 2021 seed shipments to France as 157 tonnes, down 80 per cent on 2020; that is an export figure, not the harvest, and is not on the card.
- Moutarde de Bourgogne, an IGP since 2009, seed grown in Burgundy, and Edmond Fallot of Beaune using Aligoté: https://www.connexionfrance.com/article/Mag/Food-and-Drink/Sowing-the-seeds-of-a-French-mustard-boom ; https://igourmet.com/products/burgundy-mustard-by-edmond-fallot . One retailer calls the Fallot jar AOC; the INAO and the rest say IGP, and the card keeps IGP.
- Maille: the 1747 Paris boutique on the rue Saint-André des Arts and the supply to the court of Louis XV belong to the son, Antoine-Claude Maille, whose father of the same name sold a plague vinegar in the 1720s; Maurice Grey of Grey Poupon bought the house in 1885 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maille_(company) ; https://maille.com/ca/en/history ). The card names only the 1747 founder, so no change.
- House corrections made in the same pass: the Eggs Owen's Creole-spiced potato sits with the short rib, as the guide says (pdf p19), not under it; the chives card no longer says they are stirred in fresh, which no menu prints; the stock card says the lobster's white bean stew likely carries stock, since no house source says so, and the chives and mayonnaise cards cite the pasted menus page for the Roost bar snacks (the Roost cocktails page prints only the Spoonbill's chive oil).
