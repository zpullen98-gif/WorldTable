# The bar: Brennan's as a master bartender reads it

Brennan's, 417 Royal Street, New Orleans. Prices as printed in the house server guide, menus read 26 September 2026. Read this on the walk in. Drill the section you will sell today.

## 0. How to read this

Three labels, every time.

- (pdf): the house server guide, the only Brennan's-specific source. Pages read for this dossier: 1 to 3, 8 to 10, 33 to 35, 38 to 42, 47 to 51.
- (search): a web snippet, naming the site. This session's search budget was already spent (200 of 200 calls) before this dossier ran, and the restaurant's site is blocked at the proxy, so no live search ran here. The only (search) lines below are snippets cached earlier in the same research session and quoted in the verification record. Each is marked "cached" and names its site. There are five of them.
- (expert): what a master bartender knows of the drink in general. Never a fact about Brennan's. The guide's own rule (pdf, p2): describe what the menu says plus what is true of the technique in general; do not invent house details.

Verdict marks. Where the verification record refuted a claim, only the correction appears. Where a claim could not be verified, the line says "unverifiable". Where two sources disagree, both are given and neither is picked.

What the PDF never gives for any drink: measures, glassware, ice, garnish, or the brand of the base spirit unless the menu names one. Every build below marked (expert) is the classic. It is to be checked against the bar's own spec before it is said at a table or stored in the app as Brennan's.

## 2. Brandy Milk Punch, $12

One breath: "Brandy, cream, vanilla and nutmeg, shaken cold: New Orleans' breakfast cocktail, like a lighter eggnog with no egg."

What the house says
- (pdf, p38) Menu line: brandy, heavy cream, vanilla bean, nutmeg. Breakfast and lunch $12. No dinner listing.
- (pdf, p38) Technique box: shaken with heavy cream and vanilla bean; topped with nutmeg. "Shaken" is the guide's reading of the menu, and (expert) the standard one.
- (pdf, p38) Service note: dairy, alcohol. It opens the Traditional Breakfast tasting. Offer it at the greeting; it is the first sale of the morning.
- (pdf, p38) The 45-second line: served cold with fresh nutmeg on top; a lighter, boozier eggnog without the egg; creamy, smooth, a little sweet.
- (pdf, p50) Menu words: milk punch is the house's classic breakfast cocktail, brandy, cream, vanilla and nutmeg.
- (search, cached, summary of the brennansneworleans.com breakfast tasting page) The tasting "begins with an Eye Opener Cocktail, followed by their classic Brandy Milk Punch, crafted with brandy, vanilla bean, cream, and freshly grated nutmeg", $80 with all paired beverages. Matches the PDF.

The classic build (expert, high on the pattern; measures vary by house)
- 2 oz brandy, 1 oz heavy cream or 2 oz half-and-half, 1/2 oz simple syrup or 1 to 2 teaspoons powdered sugar, a scrape of vanilla bean or 1/4 teaspoon extract. Shake hard with ice for a proper froth. Serve over fresh ice in a rocks glass, or up in a chilled coupe. Grate the nutmeg fresh at the pass; pre-ground nutmeg is the tell of a lazy milk punch. No other garnish.
- The menu line prints no sugar. (expert) A milk punch almost always carries one. Quote the four printed ingredients and do not add a fifth until the bar confirms.
- "Vanilla bean" rather than extract usually means a bean-infused cream or syrup made ahead. Ask.
- Variants to know: bourbon milk punch, the brandy-and-bourbon split, and the blended frozen style some Quarter houses pour.
- (repo, ledger/js/data-core.js line 382) The Ledger's entry, tier 8: 2 oz cognac, 1/2 oz rich simple, 3 oz whole milk, dash vanilla extract, shake hard, strain over ice, highball or rocks, grated nutmeg. The house differs on heavy cream, vanilla bean and possibly ice; the Ledger narrows brandy to cognac where the menu says only brandy.

The story (expert)
- (high) Milk punch is English and old. Aphra Behn mentions it in 1688. The earliest written recipe is Mary Rockett's of 1711, a clarified punch: spirit, citrus and sugar curdle hot milk, which is strained off clear. Benjamin Franklin wrote one out in 1763.
- (high) The New Orleans drink is the unclarified, cold, shaken kind. Christmas, New Year and Carnival mornings are well attested. "After Mass" is oral tradition; tell it as lore.
- (high) Brennan's and Galatoire's are the houses most tied to the brandy version; Bourbon House to the bourbon one. (medium) Brennan's has poured it as the breakfast eye opener since the Owen Brennan era.

Confirmed: ingredients, price, placement on the tasting, service note, the instruction to offer it at the greeting.
Ask the bar: brandy brand; cream throughout or a cream and milk blend; whether the base is batched; how the vanilla bean is used; sugar; glass; up or on the rocks; whether a frozen version exists.

## 3. Bloody Bull, $13

One breath: "Our Bloody Mary with beef bouillon stirred in: savoury and rich, like a spicy chilled consommé with vodka. Not for vegetarians; our Bloody Mary with pickled okra is."

What the house says
- (pdf, p38) Menu line: housemade Bloody Mary mix, beef bouillon, vodka. Breakfast and lunch $13. Poured with the soup course on the Traditional Breakfast.
- (pdf, p39) The 45-second line: the bouillon is a clear, savoury beef broth stirred in; almost like a spicy, chilled consommé; wonderful with the turtle soup. The 10-second line calls it "a Brennan's breakfast classic", not an invention.
- (pdf, p39) Service note: contains beef, not vegetarian; offer the Brennan's Bloody Mary, $11, and ask the bar about the mix. Spice level from the mix. The guide adds that Bloody Mary mixes usually include Worcestershire (anchovy); that is a general warning, not a confirmed fact about this mix. Alcohol.
- (pdf, p47) The vegetarian script lists "bouillon in the Bloody Bull" among hidden animal products.
- (pdf, p50) Menu words: a Bloody Bull is a Bloody Mary with beef broth, savoury and rich.
- (search, cached, brennansneworleans.com breakfast tasting page) "Second Course choice of Turtle Soup or Seafood Gumbo, paired with a Bloody Bull Cocktail." Matches.

The classic build (expert, high on the pattern)
- 1.5 to 2 oz vodka, 2 oz tomato juice or mix, 2 oz chilled beef bouillon or consommé, 1/2 oz lemon, 3 dashes Worcestershire, 2 to 3 dashes hot sauce, a pinch of celery salt, cracked black pepper, a teaspoon of horseradish if liked. Build in a highball over ice. Roll between two tins; never shake, because tomato foams and loses its texture. Lemon or lime wedge and a pickled vegetable; okra is the New Orleans choice.
- A Bull can also be served warm as a beef toddy. The PDF's "chilled consommé" says Brennan's pours it cold. Confirm.
- (repo) The Ledger carries a Bloody Mary (roll, never shake) and no Bloody Bull.

The story (expert)
- (high) The Bloody Mary is credited to Fernand Petiot at Harry's New York Bar, Paris, in the early 1920s; from 1934 at the St. Regis King Cole Bar in New York as the Red Snapper, where he added the seasonings. George Jessel also claimed it, so say "credited".
- (medium) The Bull Shot, vodka and cold beef bouillon, is credited to Lester Gruber's Caucus Club in Detroit, about 1952. The Bloody Bull is the cross of the two and sits in the standard bar books from the 1960s.
- Unverifiable: whether Brennan's originated the Bloody Bull as a breakfast drink. No primary source was found and the PDF stops at "a Brennan's breakfast classic". Lizzy and servers say "a Brennan's classic", never "invented here", until the house confirms.

Confirmed: the three printed components, price, placement, the beef flag, the $11 alternative.
Ask the bar: house stock or a commercial bouillon base, and whether it carries gluten or soy; cold or warm; the mix recipe and heat; whether the mix has Worcestershire; the garnish set; whether the house claims the drink as its own.

## 5. Classic Sazerac, $13

One breath: "Sazerac rye and Peychaud's, stirred, into a Herbsaint-rinsed glass with no ice: New Orleans' official cocktail, spirit-forward and aromatic."

What the house says
- (pdf, p39) Menu line: Sazerac rye whiskey, Herbsaint rinse, Peychaud's bitters. $13 breakfast and lunch, $13 dinner.
- (pdf, p39) Technique: stirred with Peychaud's; served in a glass rinsed with Herbsaint. The 45-second line: Peychaud's is a bright red New Orleans bitters with anise and cherry notes; Herbsaint is a local anise liqueur, rinsed so you get the aroma without much of the flavour; spirit-forward, spicy and aromatic.
- (pdf, p39) Service note: spirit-forward and strong, classically served up, without ice, confirm the bar's build. Upsells: Thompson's Dream $20 and Origin Story $40. Herbsaint is ERB-saint.
- (pdf, p51) Menu words: Herbsaint is the house's local anise liqueur, just a rinse in the Sazerac glass for aroma.
- Absent from every line: sugar and lemon peel. A menu line is an ingredient list, not a spec. (expert) Every classic build carries both, so ask before telling a guest the drink is rye, bitters and a rinse only.
- (search, cached, a diner review surfaced via Tripadvisor, theroamingboomers.com and listenjourneysavor.com) "They also pour great cocktails, including a perfect Sazerac." Confirms it is poured; nothing on the build or the price.

Vocabulary, because "up" trips people (expert)
- Neat: unchilled, straight from the bottle. Up: stirred or shaken cold, strained, no ice, usually stemware. Down: chilled and strained into a rocks glass with no ice. A Sazerac is stirred over ice and strained into a chilled rocks glass with no ice: "down", or "up with no ice in a rocks glass". Never a coupe. The PDF's "served up, without ice" is shorthand for exactly that, and it already says confirm. Do not read "neat" as skip the stir.
- Suggested wording for the house layer: "stirred cold, strained into a chilled rocks glass, no ice; confirm the bar's build".
- (repo, data-core.js lines 55 to 58) The Ledger's Sazerac: 2 oz rye, 1 sugar cube, 3 dashes Peychaud's, absinthe rinse, stir, strain into the rinsed glass, no ice, chilled rocks glass, lemon peel expressed and traditionally discarded. The house swaps Herbsaint for absinthe and names Sazerac Rye.

The canon builds (expert, high; corrected in verification)
- Sazerac Company house recipe: 1 sugar cube, 1 1/2 oz Sazerac Rye, 1/4 oz Herbsaint, 3 dashes Peychaud's, lemon peel. Two glasses. Pack one old fashioned glass with ice to chill it. In the second, crush the sugar with the bitters and add the rye; the Sazerac House bar adds ice and stirs. Empty the first glass, coat it with Herbsaint, discard the excess. Pour the drink across. Peel. No ice in the serving glass.
- IBA: 50 ml cognac, 10 ml absinthe, 1 sugar cube, 2 dashes Peychaud's, lemon peel. Rinse a chilled old fashioned glass with absinthe and discard; stir the rest over ice; strain in. The IBA names cognac as the base and notes only that rye replaced cognac after the American Civil War. It does not print "cognac or rye".
- First printed recipe: William Boothby, 1908, credited to Tom Handy's Sazerac House. Frappé an old fashioned glass. Muddle half a sugar cube with a little water, add ice, a jigger of "good whiskey" (not rye by name), two dashes of Peychaud's and a twist of lemon peel, stir cold. Empty the ice from the first glass, dash in absinthe, twirl to coat. Strain in; ice water on the side. The name is in print by 1899 without a recipe. (repo) The Ledger's lore says "in print from 1899 it is a rye drink"; reconcile 1899 (name) with 1908 (recipe) before anything ships.
- Why two glasses: the rinse must cling to a cold, dry glass and must not be diluted by the stir. Stir, never shake: a spirit-only drink wants silk, not air.

The timeline (expert)
- (high) 1806: "cocktail" defined in The Balance and Columbian Repository, Hudson, New York, 13 May: spirits, sugar, water and bitters. The word appears in a London paper in 1798 and a New Hampshire paper in 1803. Antoine Peychaud was born about 1803, so the story that he coined "cocktail" from the coquetier egg cup is folklore, popularised by Stanley Clisby Arthur in 1937. The PDF does not repeat it, which is right. Be kind about it when a guest does.
- (medium) 1830s: Peychaud's pharmacy on Royal Street, usually placed at 437 Royal, the 400 block between Conti and St Louis, the same block as Brennan's at 417. Caveat: the city renumbered its streets in 1894 and period directories give 123 Royal; 437 is the modern equivalent. A good table line if the house confirms it; the PDF says nothing of it.
- (medium) About 1850: Sewell Taylor sells his bar to import Sazerac de Forge et Fils cognac from the Charente; Aaron Bird renames it the Sazerac Coffee House at 13 Exchange Alley; the house pour of that cognac with Peychaud's takes the bar's name. Thomas H. Handy buys it about 1869 to 1870; his firm becomes the Sazerac Company. Caveat: David Wondrich finds no printed "Sazerac" cocktail before the 1890s, so tell the 1850s cognac stage as tradition.
- (medium) 1870s: phylloxera reaches the Charente in 1872; cognac grows scarce and rye takes over, though the real squeeze on stocks came in the 1880s. The absinthe dash is dated 1873 and credited to Leon Lamothe by the Sazerac Company, a credit that traces to Arthur's 1937 book. Folklore-grade dates.
- (high) 1912: absinthe banned in the United States. 1920 to 1933: Prohibition.
- (high) 1934: Herbsaint, below.
- (high) 1949: the Sazerac Bar opens at the Roosevelt; 26 September 1949, "Stormin' the Sazerac", women admitted.
- (high) 2007: absinthe returns. Lucid (Ted Breaux, made in France) first on the US market in 2007; St. George Absinthe Verte on 5 December 2007, the first American-made absinthe since the ban.
- (medium) 2008: Senator Edwin R. Murray's Senate Bill 6 sought to make the Sazerac Louisiana's state cocktail; scaled back to the city of New Orleans; passed by the Legislature on 23 June 2008. Say "passed", not "signed", for that date. Louisiana has no state cocktail.
- (high) 2019: the Sazerac House, Canal and Magazine, museum and small distillery.
- Never say "America's first cocktail". The 1806 definition predates it. "New Orleans' official cocktail" is true and sells better.

Sourcing (expert)
- (high) Sazerac Rye: Sazerac Company, distilled at Buffalo Trace, Frankfort, Kentucky; 90 proof; the low-rye Kentucky style, soft and spicy with a little caramel.
- (high on the brand, medium on production) Peychaud's: Sazerac Company; gentian base with anise, clove and cherry; its red tint is the drink's signature. Made at Buffalo Trace since the 2000s. The Sazerac House on Canal Street bottles some and shows small-batch production, so "it has a home on Canal Street again" is safe and "made here" is not.
- (high) Herbsaint: created in New Orleans in 1934, just after Repeal, by J. Marion Legendre with Reginald Parker; Legendre had learnt absinthe-making in France during the First World War. First sold as Legendre Absinthe, renamed when the federal authorities objected. The name is from "herbe sainte", the Creole name for wormwood, and an anagram of absinthe. It contains no wormwood, hence legal after 1912. The Sazerac Company bought the firm in 1949. Standard bottling 90 proof; Herbsaint Original, 100 proof, the 1934 recipe, since 2009. Distilled in Kentucky today. The H is silent: ERB-sant, as the PDF has it.
- On "our local anise liqueur" (pdf, p39, p51): true of origin and ownership, not of the still. Suggested wording: "New Orleans' own anise liqueur, created here in 1934".
- Three bottles, one company: Sazerac Rye, Peychaud's and Herbsaint are all Sazerac Company brands. A strong line for the guest who asks.

Confirmed: the three named components, the price at both meals, no ice, the two upsells, the pronunciation.
Ask the bar: sugar cube or syrup; number of dashes; any Angostura; peel dropped in or discarded; Herbsaint standard or Original; the glass.

## 6. Thompson's Dream, $20

One breath: "Our Sazerac stepped up: Willett four-year rye, Creole bitters and a St. George absinthe rinse, $20."

What the house says
- (pdf, p39) Exists only inside the Sazerac service note: Willett 4-year rye, Bitter Truth Creole Bitters, St. George absinthe rinse, $20. The 45-second hand-off: "two luxury versions: Thompson's Dream with Willett rye, and Origin Story with Cognac and absinthe." No entry of its own, so no 10/20/45 lines; do not invent them. No measures, glass or garnish; the build pattern follows the Classic.
- (search, cached, two result link lists) A page titled "Luxury Roost Bar Cocktails" exists on brennansneworleans.com. Nothing on its contents. The PDF never cites that page, so placing these two drinks on it is an inference.

Sourcing (expert)
- (high on the house, medium on the proof range) Willett: Bardstown, Kentucky. Founded 1936 by Thompson Willett with his father A. Lambert Willett; first barrels filled March 1937. Even Kulsveen bought it in 1984 and ran it as Kentucky Bourbon Distillers; the family resumed distilling on site in 2012. The Willett Family Estate 4-year rye is their own distillate, bottled at barrel proof, typically around 108 to 115, so a Sazerac on it runs hotter and spicier than one on 90-proof Sazerac Rye.
- (medium) The name: no Willett product is called Thompson's Dream, so the cocktail name is Brennan's own and the link to Thompson Willett is an inference. Lizzy may say "probably named for", never "named for".
- (high) Bitter Truth Creole Bitters: The Bitter Truth, Munich, founded 2006 by the bartenders Stephan Berg and Alexander Hauck. Creole Bitters added around 2010; bright red; the maker's descriptor is anise, caraway and clove; called "Creole style" because Peychaud's is a trademark; recommended for the Sazerac. Guest answer: a Munich bartender's tribute to the Creole style, built for exactly this drink.
- (high) St. George Absinthe Verte: St. George Spirits, Alameda, California, founded 1982 by Jörg Rupf, master distiller Lance Winters. Released 5 December 2007, Repeal Day, the first legal American-made absinthe since 1912. 60 percent ABV, brandy base: wormwood, star anise and fennel macerated and redistilled, then a second maceration of lemon balm, hyssop, meadowsweet, stinging nettle, tarragon, basil and mint for the green. Herbaceous, not candied.
- Next to the Classic (expert): louder rye spice, more heat, a greener, more herbal rinse. For the guest who already loves Sazeracs.

Ask the bar: measures; the bottling proof on the bar; the name story.

## 7. Origin Story, $40

One breath: "The Sazerac the way it began, on cognac: Dudognon, barrel-aged Peychaud's and Jade Nouvelle-Orléans absinthe, $40."

What the house says
- (pdf, p39) Dudognon Cognac, barrel-aged Peychaud's, Jade Nouvelle-Orléans absinthe, $40. One line only; no entry of its own. It is the highest cocktail price in the guide: bubbly cocktails top out at $20 (p49) and the Affogato at $16 (p40).

Sourcing (expert)
- (high on cru and style, medium on village and range) Dudognon: a family estate at Lignières-Sonneville, inside Grande Champagne, Cognac's first cru. About 20 hectares of Ugni Blanc, distillation on the lees, long ageing in old Limousin casks, slow reduction with distilled water, no caramel, boisé or added sugar. Range from memory: Sélection (about 5 years), Réserve (about 10), Vieille Réserve (about 20), Héritage (about 50), occasional single casks. The PDF names no expression; the $40 could rest on any of them.
- (high) Jade Nouvelle-Orléans: Jade Liqueurs, the brand of T.A. (Ted) Breaux, a New Orleans native and chemist who analysed sealed pre-ban bottles. Distilled at Combier in Saumur, Loire; 68 percent ABV; a tribute to nineteenth-century New Orleans absinthe culture. Breaux also formulated Lucid, the first genuine grande-wormwood absinthe back on the US market in 2007; the Swiss brand Kübler had a label approval in March 2007, so "first approved" is contested and "first genuine absinthe back on the market" is the safe wording.
- (high on the technique) Barrel-aged Peychaud's: bitters rested in a used spirit cask, which softens the anise edge and adds vanilla and oak. Whether Brennan's ages it in house or buys a limited release is unknown. Print it as written; do not attribute it to a Sazerac Company release.
- (high on the logic) Why the name: the first Sazeracs were cognac and Peychaud's. Absinthe probably joined the drink after cognac was already on the way out, so "the way it began, cognac and Peychaud's" is safer than "the way it was first made".
- Next to the Classic (expert): rounder, grape and dried fruit, oak from the cognac, a deeper and softer bitters note, a floral wormwood aroma from the Jade. Offer it at the Chateaubriand table or with the dinner tasting.

Ask the bar: which Dudognon; who ages the bitters and in what cask; measures; glass.

## 8. Brennan's Irish Coffee, $12

One breath: "Chicory coffee, brown sugar, Tullamore Dew and cold cream on top: sip the hot coffee through the cream. Lovely with the Bananas Foster."

What the house says
- (pdf, p39) Menu line: dark roast chicory coffee, brown sugar, Tullamore Dew, whipped cream. $12 breakfast and lunch, $12 dinner. On the breakfast and lunch menu and on the dessert menu (p49: one dessert menu, linked from both meals).
- (pdf, p39) Service note: dairy, caffeine, alcohol. Pair with dessert as an upsell. The 45-second line: chicory root adds a roasty, almost chocolatey depth; you sip the hot coffee through the cool cream; perfect with Bananas Foster or bread pudding. The 20-second line says "a cap of whipped cream".
- (search, cached, Wikipedia and retailer pages) Tullamore D.E.W. is a triple-blend Irish whiskey from Tullamore, County Offaly, established 1829. Nothing on Brennan's.

The classic build (expert, high)
- 4 oz hot, strong coffee; 1.5 oz Irish whiskey; 2 sugar cubes or 2 teaspoons brown sugar; cream whipped only to a pourable ribbon and floated over the back of a spoon. Pre-heat a stemmed heat-proof glass. Dissolve the sugar in the coffee first, then the whiskey, then the cream. Never stir after the cream goes on. Sugar is structural: it raises the coffee's density so the cream floats. A stiff canister cap is a different texture; the PDF says "cap", so ask which the bar does.
- (repo, data-core.js lines 131 to 134) The Ledger's Irish Coffee already has brown sugar and a lightly whipped float in a pre-heated glass. The house edits are the chicory coffee and the named whiskey.

The story (expert)
- (high) Joe Sheridan, chef at the Foynes flying-boat terminal in County Limerick, winter 1942 to 1943 (the museum's date; often simply 1943), for passengers off a flight turned back by weather. Stanton Delaplane of the San Francisco Chronicle and Jack Koeppler recreated it at the Buena Vista Café on 10 November 1952; the float was reportedly solved with help from the mayor, who owned a dairy. The Buena Vista pours Tullamore D.E.W., which supports the house choice.
- (high) Tullamore D.E.W.: the initials of Daniel Edmund Williams, who rose from stable boy to run and own the distillery; a triple-distilled blend of pot still, malt and grain. The original distillery closed in 1954; the whiskey was long made at Midleton; a new Tullamore distillery opened in 2014; William Grant and Sons since 2010. Write it D.E.W. when a guest asks what it means.
- (high) Chicory: the roasted root of Cichorium intybus. The New Orleans habit is usually traced to Civil War blockade shortages, with a French precedent. It adds body and a cocoa-like bitterness and no caffeine.
- (expert, medium) Congregation Coffee, named on p49 and p34, is a New Orleans roaster in Algiers Point. The PDF does not say the Irish Coffee uses it.

Ask the bar: pourable float or stiff cap; the glass; whether the coffee is Congregation; coffee strength.

## 9. Dulce de Leche, $14, and the other dessert cocktails

One breath: "Bananas Foster in a glass: Don Q rum, crème de cacao, our banana rum butter sauce and cream. Rich and sweet, a dessert on its own."

What the house says
- (pdf, p40) Menu line: Brennan's Blender's Reserve Don Q Rum, crème de cacao, banana rum butter sauce, cream. $14 at both meals. Framed as the cocktail cousin of Bananas Foster: buttery, caramel and banana; works as dessert on its own or with a spoonful of something warm.
- (pdf, p40) Service note: dairy, alcohol. Confirm the Blender's Reserve story at lineup before telling it; the menu names it only as Brennan's Blender's Reserve Don Q Rum. Other dessert cocktails: Tiramisu $11, Affogato $16, copied as printed (the Tiramisu is cheaper than the Affogato).
- Internal tension (pdf, p40): the 10-second line says "Brennan's own Don Q rum" and the 45-second line "a Don Q rum that carries our name", while the service note says confirm first. Until confirmed, say "Don Q rum" or "our Don Q selection".
- (search, cached, rum-x.com and excellencerhum.com) A "Don Q Blender's Reserve" exists as a Destilería Serrallés limited release: "an exceptional blend aged for up to 22 years, produced in a limited edition of 3,800 bottles for Europe". That is a different claim from a bottle named for Brennan's. Whether the bar pours that release, a house-named blend or a private barrel is unknown. The PDF does not disagree; it simply says confirm. This snippet corrects the earlier professional memory that no such Don Q line exists.

The build (expert; there is no public canon for a house drink)
- A dessert flip without egg: aged rum, crème de cacao (dark matches the caramel; white keeps the colour pale), the kitchen's sauce as the sweetener, cream. A working ratio for a drink of this kind is 1.5 oz rum, 1/2 oz crème de cacao, 3/4 oz sauce, 1 oz cream, shaken hard, served up, nutmeg or a banana chip. That ratio is mine, not Brennan's. Nothing goes in the app until the bar gives numbers.
- The 45-second line's "with a spoonful of something warm" implies the drink is cold. The sauce is a kitchen component: know who makes it and how it is held.

Sourcing (expert, high)
- Don Q: Destilería Serrallés at Mercedita, Ponce, Puerto Rico; distilling since 1865; family owned; the brand launched in 1932 and named for Don Quixote. Aged expressions: Añejo, Reserva 7, Gran Añejo, Signature Release single barrels, Double Aged cask finishes.

Ask the bar: the Blender's Reserve story (the European release, a private barrel or a bespoke blend; when; by whom); measures; cold or warm; dark or white crème de cacao; who makes the sauce; whether the Affogato is a cocktail or a dessert.

## 10. Brennan's Champagne Cocktail, $20, and Bubbles at Brennan's

Neither is picked here; the pour may have changed between readings. Ask.

The classic build (expert, high)
- A sugar cube soaked with 2 to 3 dashes of Angostura in a chilled flute, 4 to 5 oz of dry Champagne, lemon twist. The cube keeps the drink fizzing from the bottom. The "with cognac" version adds 1/2 oz before the wine. Jerry Thomas printed the no-brandy version in 1862. New Orleans houses often swap Peychaud's for Angostura, which turns it pink and anise-scented.
- (repo) The Ledger's Champagne Cocktail, tier 3: Angostura-soaked cube, 5 oz Champagne, optional 1/2 oz cognac, lemon twist. Whether Brennan's uses Peychaud's, cognac, the house Piper or something else is unknown.
- (expert) A $12 to $20 bubbly list usually means a Mimosa, a Kir Royale, a French 75, a Champagne Cocktail, perhaps a Bellini or a spritz. A guess, not a fact; the PDF confirms only the Champagne Cocktail.

Mirabelle to Pommery (expert, high)
- Mirabelle is Schramsberg's second label, North Coast California, traditional method. Pommery, founded 1836 and now Vranken-Pommery, is a Reims grande marque; its rosé is Brut Rosé Royal. So the range runs from California sparkling to true Champagne, and these are sparkling rosés, not still ones. A guest who asks "is it real Champagne?" gets that distinction.

Sabrage (expert, high), for whoever is asked to do it
- A proper sparkling bottle only, at about 5 to 6 bar. Chill it well, ideally with the neck in ice; never a shaken or warm bottle. Strip the foil, remove the cage, keep a thumb on the cork until the stroke. Find a side seam and turn it upward. Hold the bottle by the punt at 30 to 45 degrees, aimed away from every person, light and pane of glass. Run the blunt back edge of the blade along the seam in one firm stroke that follows through past the lip; the cork leaves inside its glass collar. Inspect the neck and discard the first pour. Brief the courtyard before you swing. The Napoleon's hussars story is a legend.
- The PDF gives no procedure and does not say who performs it. Its flambé note (p34) says learn the house procedure at training; the same applies here.

Ask the bar: the Champagne Cocktail build (bitters, sugar, cognac or not, which wine, glass); the full bubbly cocktail roster; who sabers and which bottle; the rosé bottles between Mirabelle and Pommery; whether the snack menu runs outside 2 to 6.

Product facts, corrected in verification (expert)
- Seedlip Grove 42 is a citrus-led 0.0 percent ABV distilled non-alcoholic spirit from the British brand Seedlip, launched 2015 by Ben Branson; Grove 42 followed in 2018 (bitter orange, blood orange, mandarin, lemon peel, ginger, lemongrass); Diageo took a majority stake in 2019.
- Lyre's is Australian, launched 2019 by Mark Livings and Carl Hartmann. The PDF names only "Lyre's N/A agave"; Lyre's makes both Agave Blanco and Agave Reserva, so confirm which. Lyre's labels its spirits as under 0.5 percent ABV, the legal non-alcoholic threshold, not 0.0. Describe the Black Hills as non-alcoholic, not zero, and read the ABV off the bottle at lineup before promising a guest zero. Lyre's products are blends of extracts and essences in water, so "distillate" is wrong for them.
- Both drinks carry fresh components (watermelon, basil purée) that need a daily prep check.

## 12. Coffee, the Café Glacé and the Café Brûlot question

- (pdf, p49) Coffee $11: New Orleans style with chicory; a single-origin Papua New Guinea from Congregation Coffee; Café Glacé de la Maison (chicory cold brew, cardamom-clove syrup, orange flower foam). - (pdf) The guide's zero-proof pairings lean on chicory coffee for the Snickers, the coffee cake and the bread pudding, and on the Café Glacé when the guest wants it cold.
- (expert) Café glacé is French for iced coffee; "de la Maison" marks the house version; the cardamom-clove syrup nods to the brûlot spice set. It is the bar's one coffee drink with real craft content. Learn it and be able to describe it.

Café Brûlot
- (pdf, p9 to 10, p50) The only Brûlot item is the Café Brûlot Coffee Cake, $14, breakfast and lunch: chicory coffee and bittersweet chocolate cake, pecans, spiced citrus glaze, whipped crème fraîche. The 45-second line describes the drink generically, a classic New Orleans after-dinner coffee flamed with brandy, orange and lemon peel, cinnamon and clove, and says the cake borrows those flavours. Say broo-LOH. Across all 51 pages the drink is never listed on any menu, and p49 says only two things are flamed at the table, both desserts. Lizzy should not offer Café Brûlot as a drink until the house confirms it.
- (expert, high) Café Brûlot Diabolique was devised at Antoine's by Jules Alciatore, usually dated about 1890; the Prohibition-disguise story is a legend, since the drink predates 1920. The build: brandy, orange and lemon peel in strips, cinnamon sticks, whole cloves and sugar cubes warmed in a silver brûlot bowl over a flame; ignite; ladle the burning brandy down a clove-studded orange-peel spiral held on a fork; pour in about 3 cups of hot, strong coffee (chicory-blended in New Orleans practice) to tame the flame; ladle into demitasse cups. Some houses add orange liqueur; Antoine's classic does not. Lights down for the show.
- (expert, medium) Brennan's served it in the family era and the house cookbook carries a recipe. History until confirmed.
- The flambé safety rules the PDF gives for Bananas Foster (p34) apply to any flamed drink: nothing overhead, guests back, measure the spirit away from the flame, never add spirit to a lit pan, lid within reach, know the extinguisher, let the flame burn out, and tell guests who avoid alcohol that flaming does not remove all of it.
