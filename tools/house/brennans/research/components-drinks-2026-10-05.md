# Components of the drinks: the master bartender's searches, 5 October 2026

What each WebSearch showed, in the order run, for the component fragment tools/house/brennans/components/drinks.json. Sixty searches, the full budget. A search summary is a reading of the result snippets, not a fetch of each page; where the snippets disagree or stop short, the line says so and the component leaves the point to the bar. Nothing here is a fact about Brennan's unless the guide, the menu paste or the house pages say it; those remain the only house sources. Living people named in these results are recorded here as research and never appear in a drink component.

## Searches

1. Sazerac official cocktail of New Orleans, 2008. Wikipedia, the Sazerac House and the Louisiana Legislature site: Senator Edwin R. Murray filed Senate Bill 6 to make the Sazerac the state cocktail; defeated on 8 April 2008; on 23 June 2008 the Legislature proclaimed it the official cocktail of the city of New Orleans instead.
2. Antoine Peychaud and 437 Royal Street. The Sazerac Company story page and Wikipedia: Antoine Amédée Peychaud (1803 to 1883), a Creole apothecary from Saint-Domingue (now Haiti), kept his apothecary at 123 Royal Street, known as 437 Royal Street today, selling bitters to the coffee houses. The bitters date between 1849 and 1857; in 1857 he advertised his American Aromatic Bitter Cordial and its use at the Sazerac House; he sold the Royal Street building in 1868.
3. Herbsaint. Wikipedia and retailers: an anise liqueur created in New Orleans in 1934 by J. Marion Legendre and Reginald Parker, who had learned absinthe making in France during the First World War; first sold as Legendre Absinthe though it never held grand wormwood; the Federal Alcohol Control Administration objected, so it became Legendre Herbsaint, from herbe sainte, sacred herb, the Creole name for wormwood; first sold after the repeal of Prohibition; the Sazerac Company bought J.M. Legendre and Co. in June 1949.
4. Absinthe ban and return. Wormwood Society, Difford's Guide, study.com: in 1912 the Department of Agriculture banned absinthe, treating wormwood and thujone drinks as adulterated; on 5 March 2007 the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau confirmed that wormwood spirits could be sold if thujone free, under 10 parts per million.
5. The Sazerac Coffee House and the move from cognac to rye. Wikipedia, VinePair, Country Roads: Sewell Taylor sold the Merchants Exchange Coffee House to import Sazerac de Forge et Fils cognac; Aaron Bird renamed it the Sazerac Coffee House and served the Sazerac Cocktail on that cognac, allegedly with Peychaud's bitters; Thomas Handy bought it about 1870 as phylloxera ruined the European vineyards; rye replaced cognac and stayed.
6. St. George Absinthe Verte. St. George Spirits and reviewers: the first legal American absinthe released after the ban was lifted in 2007; a brandy base with star anise, mint, wormwood, lemon balm, hyssop, meadowsweet, basil, fennel, tarragon and stinging nettle; 60 percent alcohol; distilled in Alameda, California.
7. Jade Nouvelle-Orléans. Retailers and K&L: an absinthe supérieure at 68 percent, made at the Combier distillery in Saumur, France, with wormwood and other plants macerated in wine alcohol and distilled in old alembics; a tribute by a native New Orleanian to the city's nineteenth-century absinthe culture. The creator is living and is not named in any component.
8. The Bitter Truth Creole Bitters. Astor Wines, the maker: from Munich; bright red; anise, caraway and fennel, with cinnamon and dried fruit; sold as a natural choice for a Sazerac and an alternative to Peychaud's; 39 percent.
9. Willett. Wikipedia, whisky.com, Skurnik: the Willett Distilling Company was founded in 1936 in Bardstown, Kentucky, on the family farm, Thompson Willett its founder and president, his brother Johnny beside him; first whiskey on 17 March 1937; the old distillery closed in the 1980s and distilling restarted on the site in 2012; bourbon and rye are made.
10. Dudognon. Skurnik, Cognac Expert, the house: the Dudognon family has grown grapes at Lignières-Sonneville since 1776, in the heart of Grande Champagne, Cognac's first cru; chalk soils; a traditional family estate distilling only its own grapes, Ugni Blanc among them; a gold medal for Paulin Dudognon in 1898; bottlings by age such as the 10-year Réserve and 20-year Vieille Réserve.
11. Milk punch. VinePair, Atlas Obscura, Wikipedia: in 1711 Mary Rockett recorded the earliest known clarified milk punch, hot milk added to brandy, lemon, sugar and water, rested and strained through flannel; Benjamin Franklin sent his own recipe to the governor of Massachusetts in 1763; the New Orleans brandy or bourbon milk punch is the unclarified, milk or cream kind.
12. The Bull Shot. Punch, VinePair, Michigan Public: invented at the Caucus Club in Detroit in 1952, opened by Lester Gruber, with an advertising team selling Campbell's canned beef bouillon; vodka and bouillon with Worcestershire and Tabasco; the Detroit Free Press called it a luncheon fad.
13. The Bloody Mary. Difford's Guide, Wikipedia: often credited to Fernand Petiot at Harry's New York Bar in Paris about 1921, vodka and tomato juice; in 1934 he took it to the King Cole Bar at the St. Regis in New York, adding Worcestershire, lemon, salt, pepper and Tabasco; the hotel tried the name Red Snapper.
14. Irish coffee. The Foynes Flying Boat Museum, Punch, Wikipedia: Joe Sheridan, chef at Foynes in County Limerick, served whiskey coffee to passengers of a turned-back flight in 1943; on 10 November 1952 Jack Koeppler of the Buena Vista Cafe in San Francisco and the travel writer Stanton Delaplane recreated it; Sheridan later moved to the Buena Vista.
15. Tullamore D.E.W. Wikipedia, the brand: established in 1829 at Tullamore, County Offaly; the name is the initials of Daniel Edmund Williams (1848 to 1921), general manager and later owner; a triple-distilled blend of pot still, malt and grain whiskey.
16. The espresso martini. Imbibe, Wikipedia: first mixed in the 1980s by the London bartender Dick Bradsell, often dated 1983 at the Soho Brasserie, for a guest who wanted something to wake her up; vodka, fresh espresso, coffee liqueur and sugar; first called the Vodka Espresso.
17. The Champagne Cocktail. Wikipedia, Vintage American Cocktails: Jerry Thomas printed it in 1862, a lump of sugar soaked in Angostura in a wine glass, filled with Champagne, a lemon peel; revised in 1887; no one is certain who invented it.
18. The word cocktail, 1806. Word Histories, Wikipedia: on 13 May 1806 The Balance and Columbian Repository printed the first definition, by the editor Harry Croswell: spirits of any kind, sugar, water and bitters. No result tied the word to Antoine Peychaud; the egg cup tale is not supported.
19. For Sentimental Reasons. Wikipedia: (I Love You) For Sentimental Reasons by the King Cole Trio entered the Billboard Best Seller chart on 22 November 1946 and became Nat King Cole's first number one pop single on 28 December 1946.
20. Prisoner of Love. Wikipedia: a 1931 song by Russ Columbo and Clarence Gaskill with lyrics by Leo Robin; Perry Como's recording reached the Billboard charts on 30 March 1946 and was number one for three weeks from 4 May 1946, the top seller of the year on Billboard's year-end list.
21. Rumors Are Flying. Wikipedia, AllMusic: written by Bennie Benjamin and George David Weiss; Frankie Carle and His Orchestra had a number one with it in October 1946; fifth on Billboard's year-end list for 1946. The menu prints the title as Rumors are Flying.
22. To Each His Own. Wikipedia: music by Jay Livingston, words by Ray Evans, the title song of the 1946 Paramount film; Eddy Howard and His Orchestra took it to number one on 3 August 1946 for eight weeks; the Ink Spots also charted with it; several versions held the Top Ten at once; the film won Olivia de Havilland an Academy Award.
23. Ole Buttermilk Sky. Wikipedia: written by Hoagy Carmichael and Jack Brooks, introduced by Carmichael in the film Canyon Passage (1946); a hit for Kay Kyser, number one on 14 December 1946; nominated for an Academy Award. The song is spelled Ole; the menu prints Old Buttermilk Sky, and the component uses the menu's spelling for the drink.
24. Five Minutes More. Wikipedia: by Sammy Cahn and Jule Styne, 1946; Frank Sinatra's recording reached number one on 14 September 1946 for four weeks.
25. Personality. Wikipedia: by Johnny Burke and Jimmy Van Heusen for the 1946 film Road to Utopia, sung there by Dorothy Lamour; Johnny Mercer and the Pied Pipers took it to number one for the week of 9 March 1946.
26. Oh! What It Seemed to Be. Wikipedia: by Bennie Benjamin, George Weiss and Frankie Carle; number one in 1946 for both Frankie Carle's orchestra and Frank Sinatra.
27. Chicory in New Orleans coffee. Smithsonian, New Orleans Historical, Chowhound: France used roasted chicory root to stretch coffee during Napoleon's Continental Blockade; by 1840 New Orleans was the second largest coffee importer in the United States; during the Civil War the Union blockade cut imports and New Orleanians stretched coffee with chicory, a story some call possibly fabled; the blend stayed for its smoothness and caramel notes.
28. Congregation Coffee. Tripadvisor, Axios, Sprudge: a roaster and café at 240 Pelican Avenue, Algiers Point, founded in 2015; it closed its roastery in 2023 and reopened later that year under Brennan family ownership, as Axios reported; it roasts on Tchoupitoulas Street and has a Magazine Street café.
29. Cold brew. Wikipedia, Breville and roasters: coarse coffee steeped in cold or room temperature water for 12 to 24 hours and strained; smoother and less acidic and less bitter than hot brewed coffee.
30. Revive cold-pressed juice. No result confirms who makes the Revive at Brennan's: one New Orleans juice company sells a juice called Revive with a different recipe, and an unrelated juice bar of that name appears elsewhere. Who presses it stays a question for the bar.
31. Chopin vodka. Wikipedia and retailers: a single-ingredient Polish vodka, the potato bottling made from potatoes of the Podlasie region, distilled four times by Polmos at Siedlce; the brand also makes rye and wheat vodkas.
32. Monkey 47. The brand and retailers: Schwarzwald Dry Gin from Germany's Black Forest, 47 botanicals including lingonberries, spruce shoots and cranberries, bottled at 47 percent after resting in earthenware.
33. Rabbit Hole Dareringer. The distillery: a straight bourbon from Louisville, Kentucky, mash of corn, malted wheat and malted barley, finished in Pedro Ximénez sherry casks, 93 proof.
34. Hendrick's. Wikipedia: launched in 1999 by William Grant and Sons at Girvan, Scotland; two stills of different design; essences of Bulgarian rose and cucumber added after distillation.
35. Cap Corse Mattei. Haus Alpenz, Imbibe: in 1872 Louis-Napoléon Mattei named his aperitif wine after Cap Corse, the northern peninsula of Corsica; a quinquina with cinchona bark, on a base of Vermentino and Muscat, with citron (cédrat); still family owned.
36. Lillet. Wikipedia: founded in 1872 at Podensac, south of Bordeaux, by the brothers Paul and Raymond Lillet; about 85 percent Bordeaux wine, Semillon for the Blanc, and 15 percent citrus liqueurs; Kina Lillet carried quinine; Lillet Blanc replaced it in 1986, lighter and less bitter.
37. Elio Perrone Bigaro. Polaner and retailers: a softly sweet, gently sparkling pink wine from Piedmont, about half Brachetto and half Moscato, about 5 percent alcohol; Bigaro means mottled in Piemontese.
38. Gentilly Gin. Seven Three Distilling and Explore Louisiana: made by Seven Three Distilling Co., opened in 2017 in Tremé, New Orleans, named for the city's 73 neighbourhoods; 17 botanicals inspired by the traiteurs, the old folk healers, elderflower and persimmon among them; 87 proof.
39. Chandon. Wikipedia: Domaine Chandon in Yountville, Napa Valley, founded in 1973 by Moët et Chandon, the first French-owned sparkling wine house in the United States; owned by LVMH; made in the Champagne method.
40. Allspice dram. Wikipedia, Haus Alpenz: pimento dram, a Jamaican liqueur of allspice berries steeped in rum; tasting of clove, cinnamon and nutmeg; in 1930s cocktail books and taken up by the tiki bars.
41. Rittenhouse Rye. Heaven Hill: introduced in 1934 by Continental Distilling of Philadelphia as Rittenhouse Square rye, named for the city's square; Bottled in Bond from 1948; Heaven Hill bought the brand in 1993 and makes it in Bardstown, Kentucky; 100 proof, at least 4 years old.
42. Daron Fine Calvados. Retailers and Distiller: Calvados Pays d'Auge from Normandy, aged at least 2 years in oak, Fine being the youngest grade; the brand belongs to Maison Ferrand.
43. Volcán de mi Tierra Reposado. Retailers: 100 percent Blue Weber agave from Jalisco, rested 135 days in American and French oak; cooked agave, stone fruit, honey and toasted oak.
44. Cathead. Visit Mississippi, the distillery: founded in 2010 in Jackson, the first distillery in Mississippi; its Satsuma Mandarin Vodka is one of its spirits.
45. Green Chartreuse. Wikipedia, Taste France: made by Carthusian monks in the French Alps since 1737 to a secret recipe of 130 herbs and plants; 55 percent; the recipe traced to a manuscript of 1605.
46. Licor 43. Retailers: a golden vanilla liqueur from Cartagena, Spain, made since 1946 from 43 ingredients, citrus, vanilla, herbs and spices.
47. Wölffer Estate zero proof rosé. The Zero Proof and retailers: Wölffer Estate on Long Island makes a non-alcoholic sparkling rosé, pale salmon, with strawberry, peach and citrus; among the first American wineries to make one.
48. Amarula. Wikipedia: a South African cream liqueur of the fruit of the marula tree, fermented, distilled and aged two years in French oak, then blended with cream; 17 percent; elephants love the fruit, so the elephant is its emblem.
49. Abita Root Beer. Abita: brewed in Abita Springs, Louisiana, with artesian spring water and Louisiana cane sugar, by a hot mix process with herbs, vanilla and yucca for foam; Abita Brewing founded in 1986.
50. Black walnut bitters. Retailers: the familiar bottling is Fee Brothers; toasted walnut, warm spice, a dry, earthy finish; suits whiskey and after-dinner drinks with maple, vanilla or caramel. Which bitters the bar pours is a question for the bar.
51. Nocello. Toschi and Wikipedia: an Italian walnut liqueur made by Toschi near Modena in Emilia-Romagna, with walnut and hazelnut; 24 percent; amber.
52. Frangelico. VinePair: an Italian hazelnut liqueur from Piedmont, said to recall a friar named Angelico; its bottle shaped like a friar's habit with a rope belt; Tonda Gentile hazelnuts; launched in 1978.
53. Kahlúa. Wikipedia, the brand: a coffee liqueur from Veracruz, Mexico, introduced in 1936; rum, sugar and arabica coffee.
54. Pedro Ximénez. Sherry.wine, Wine Enthusiast: the sweetest and darkest sherry, from Pedro Ximénez grapes dried in the sun to near raisins; raisin, fig, molasses and dark chocolate; grown mostly in Montilla-Moriles and Jerez.
55. Taittinger La Française. Retailers: the Brut NV of Taittinger, about 40 percent Chardonnay with Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier, a high share of Chardonnay; the house founded in 1734, family owned since 1932, cellars in Reims.
56. Earl Grey. Wikipedia: black tea scented with oil of bergamot orange, grown mostly in Calabria; named for Charles Grey, the second Earl Grey, Prime Minister from 1830 to 1834; the origin stories are uncertain.
57. The dry shake. The Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails and bar guides: shaking without ice first so the egg white whips to a meringue-like foam, then shaking again with ice to chill; the reverse dry shake swaps the order.
58. The spritz. Difford's Guide, Wikipedia: from nineteenth-century Veneto under Austrian rule, when soldiers asked for a splash, spritzen in German, of water in strong local wine; later sparkling wine, soda and a bitter liqueur; Aperol popularised it in the 1950s.
59. Crème de cacao and crème de banane. Wikipedia: crème means a very sweet liqueur, at least 250 grams of sugar a litre under European law; crème de cacao comes white or dark, 20 to 25 percent; crème de banane is bananas macerated in spirit and sweetened.
60. Bodrum and OBX. Travel guides and Wikipedia: Bodrum is a resort and yachting town on Turkey's Aegean coast, once Halicarnassus; OBX is the Outer Banks, barrier islands off North Carolina.

## Repairs after the refuter (4 Oct, late)
