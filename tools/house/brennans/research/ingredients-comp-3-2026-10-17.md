## 1. Hibiscus, the roselle calyx

1. The hibiscus of hibiscus tea and of pickles is Hibiscus sabdariffa, called roselle; the part used is the fleshy red calyx around the seed pod, not the petal. The plant's origin is debated: some ethnobotanists favour Sudan, where it may have been domesticated about 6,000 years ago, others West Africa, others India to Malaysia; the food strain is var. sabdariffa and the fibre strain var. altissima.
   Source: https://njaes.rutgers.edu/fs1298 (Rutgers fact sheet: origin unclear, Sudan favoured by some, India to Malaysia by others; the two varieties); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibiscus_tea (summary: native to and domesticated in West Africa).
2. The major producers are China, Sudan and Thailand; Sudanese roselle is held to be the best, but a United States trade embargo and large scale growing in Mexico, Thailand and China shifted the market. Mexico's own crop grew 56.2 per cent over 2009 to 2019.
   Source: https://njaes.rutgers.edu/fs1298 (China, Sudan and Thailand the major world producers); https://uses.plantnet-project.org/en/Hibiscus_sabdariffa_(PROSEA) (Sudanese roselle viewed as superior, but the United States trade embargo and large scale production in Mexico, Thailand and China shifted the market; checked 17 October 2026); https://www.revista.ccba.uady.mx/ojs/index.php/TSA/article/view/5124 (Roselle production in Mexico: challenges and strategic prospects, Tropical and Subtropical Agroecosystems: national production up 56.2 per cent over 2009 to 2019, by intensification rather than new land).
3. In Mexico it is flor de Jamaica, and agua de Jamaica is the dried calyces boiled 8 to 10 minutes with sugar; in West Africa the drink is bissap, in Egypt karkadé.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibiscus_tea (agua de Jamaica, boiled 8 to 10 minutes then sweetened); https://premierepeau.com/it/pages/glossary-terms/roselle (bissap in West Africa, karkadé in Egypt).
4. (expert) The calyx is tart with malic and citric acids and deep ruby with anthocyanins, which is why a hibiscus pickle turns grapes red and tastes of cranberry; the kitchen dossier reads the hibiscus on the Redfish Véronique the same way (dossier-the-kitchen.md, Redfish Véronique). Whether the kitchen uses dried calyces or a hibiscus tea in the pickle, and where it buys them, is not printed.

## 2. Banana leaf

1. Banana leaves are a standard wrapper in the traditional cooking of Maritime Southeast Asia, carried by the Austronesian peoples across the islands to tropical Polynesia; bananas reached the Americas only when the Spanish brought them in the 1500s, and since then the leaf has been the common wrapper of Central America and southern Mexico. Oaxacan tamales are often steamed in banana leaves, which give a green banana aroma.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana_leaf (Maritime Southeast Asia, Austronesian spread, Oaxacan tamales); https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/16406 (the Spanish brought bananas to the Americas in the 1500s; the leaf as the wrapper of Central America and southern Mexico).
2. The leaf is waterproof, so it traps moisture around what it wraps; it is sold fresh or frozen, and frozen leaves keep for up to six months.
   Source: https://www.saveur.com/how-to-cook-banana-leaves-recipes (folded to enclose food, trapping moisture); https://www.specialtyproduce.com/produce/Banana_Leaves_3403.php (can be frozen for up to six months).
3. Bananas are grown throughout Louisiana as an ornamental; freezes kill the top growth, the rhizome often survives under mulch, and south of Interstate 10 the plants frequently fruit, though the fruit varies widely. Leaves for a kitchen are another matter: the menu names no source.
   Source: https://www.lsuagcenter.com/topics/lawn_garden/ornamentals/landscaping/louisiana-bananas (LSU AgCenter, Louisiana Bananas).
4. (expert) A fresh or thawed leaf is wiped, the central rib cut away, and the leaf passed over a flame or dipped in hot water until it turns glossy and pliable, or it cracks when folded. It gives a faint grassy, tea-like scent (dossier-the-kitchen.md, Gulf Fish en Papillote: softened over a flame, baked at about 200 C for 10 to 15 minutes). The leaf is not eaten.

## 3. Pepitas, the hulled pumpkin seed

1. Pepita is Mexican Spanish, pepita de calabaza, little seed of squash; in Mexico it means the hulled seed. The green hull-less pepita of commerce comes from oilseed varieties of Cucurbita pepo whose seed grows without the thick white hull of a field pumpkin's.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumpkin_seed (pepita de calabaza, little seed of squash; hull-less oil-seed varieties, deep green, high in oil); https://patijinich.com/pumpkin_seeds_or_pepitas/ (in Mexico pepitas usually means hulled seeds).
2. Cucurbita pepo is one of the oldest crops of the Americas: squash remains from Guilá Naquitz cave in Oaxaca, Mexico, are dated to between 10,000 and 8,000 calendar years ago, older than maize and beans, though one early seed in that series is disputed as within the wild size range.
   Source: https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dfb8564c5aef494fe4a475 (Smith, 1997: nine radiocarbon dates, cultivation between 10,000 and 8,000 calendar years ago); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guil%C3%A1_Naquitz_Cave (intentional breeding by no later than 8,000 years BP); https://www.ittc.ku.edu/publications/documents/Rignot1997_Science050997.pdf (the disputed early seed).
3. The hull-less seed is a single recessive gene; the best known hull-less cultivar is the Styrian oil pumpkin of Styria in Austria, where pumpkins grown for both fruit and seed are recorded over about 200 years. The 200 years is the oil-pumpkin tradition, not the naked seed: the recessive mutation that stops the seed coat lignifying appeared in Styria around 1880, and hull-less seeded pumpkins were reported in cultivation only after the First World War (checked 17 October 2026).
   Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.948106/pdf (a single recessive gene on chromosome 12); https://cucurbit.info/authors/joanna-winkler/ (Winkler, 2000: Styrian records going back 200 years; the mutation around 1880; cultivation reported after the First World War); https://cucurbit.info/2000/07/proceedings-of-the-1st-international-oil-pumpkin-conference/ (the preface: a recessive mutation some 130 years before 1999).
4. (expert) Pepitas are toasted dry or in a film of oil until they puff, crackle and turn nutty; raw they are soft and grassy. Where the kitchen's seeds come from is not printed, so neither Mexican nor Austrian seed may be claimed as the house's; the chef has the answer. The kitchen dossier and the chef's review give the respelling peh-PEE-tah (master-review-chef-2026-10-04.md).

## 4. Sweet tea and black tea

1. The oldest printed sweet tea recipe is in Marion Cabell Tyree's Housekeeping in Old Virginia of 1879: green tea steeped, poured over ice with sugar, a squeeze of lemon suggested. Mrs Lincoln's Boston Cook Book of 1884 is cited as the first printed iced tea made with black tea. Earlier cold teas were punches with spirits or wine, such as the tea punch in The Kentucky Housewife of 1839.
   Source: https://whatscookingamerica.net/History/IcedTeaHistory.htm (the 1879 recipe, green tea, two teaspoonfuls to a quart; the 1884 Boston Cook Book; the 1839 punch); https://www.gpb.org/news/2017/05/23/sweet-tea-history-of-the-nectar-of-the-south.
2. Tea is the leaf of Camellia sinensis; black tea is the fully oxidised leaf. The only large scale tea farm in the United States is the Charleston Tea Garden on Wadmalaw Island, South Carolina, 127 acres with about 40 acres under tea, whose bushes descend from plants the French botanist André Michaux brought to the Carolinas in the late 1700s (a state historical marker dates his planting at Middleton Barony on the Ashley River to between 1799 and 1802; the Wadmalaw plants came in 1963 as cuttings from the Pinehurst tea farm at Summerville); smaller farms exist in Alabama, Hawaii, Oregon and Washington. The "only large scale" wording is the marker's and the owner's, so the component says it is called that (checked 17 October 2026).
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_production_in_the_United_States (the only large scale farm, 127 acres; the smaller states); https://charlestonteagarden.com/pages/our-history (the Michaux lineage); https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=251962 (the historical marker: Michaux 1799 to 1802; the only site of large scale commercial tea production); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charleston_Tea_Garden (the 1963 move of cuttings to 127 acres on Wadmalaw Island); https://teatimemagazine.com/charleston-coastal-carolina-tea-destinations/2/ (about 40 acres planted).
3. (expert) Sweet tea is the everyday drink of the American South, the South's house drink: black tea brewed strong and sweetened while hot, so the sugar dissolves, then chilled; a brine of it carries salt as well, and the tea's tannin and the sugar help the skin brown (dossier-the-kitchen.md, Poussin à la Moutarde; the shipped Sweet tea brine and frying card). Which tea the kitchen brews, how much sugar and salt, and how long the hen brines are open questions (open-questions.md).

## 5. Turnips

1. The turnip is Brassica rapa. Genetic work published in Molecular Biology and Evolution points to a first domestication in the mountains near present day Afghanistan between 3,500 and 6,000 years ago; older accounts placed it in northern Europe about 2000 BC, and the archaeological record cannot settle it. It had spread east to China and reached Japan by 700 AD.
   Source: https://www.nsf.gov/news/dont-eating-greens-blame-it-brassica-domestication (the Central Asian domestication); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnip (northern Europe view; Japan by 700 AD); https://origin.ckbk.com/reference/food77337c20s001e196 (Oxford Companion to Food: thought to have originated in northern Europe about 2000 BC).
2. The small white salad turnip called Hakurei, or Tokyo turnip, was bred in Japan in the lean years around the Second World War: one account says the 1950s and the post war shortages, another says plant breeders developed it during the war itself, and neither names a breeder or a release year, so the component says the middle of the last century (checked 17 October 2026). It is sweet enough to eat raw and matures in about a month, and it has spread through American farm to table kitchens.
   Source: https://shepherdexpress.com/food/flash-in-the-pan/hakurei-is-the-caviar-of-turnips/ (1950s Japan, the post war shortages); https://www.epicgardening.com/hakurei-turnips/ (developed by Japanese plant breeders during World War II; Tokyo turnip, about a month to mature).
3. Glazed turnips, navets glacés, are a French classic: blanched, laid in one layer with water, salt, sugar and butter under a paper lid, cooked until the liquid reduces to a glaze that coats them; the water is added back as the butter caramelises, until they are translucent. Vichy carrots, the best known glazed vegetable, are in Escoffier's Guide Culinaire of 1903.
   Source: https://app.ckbk.com/recipe/cuis81374c05s001ss004r004/legumes-glaces (Le Cordon Bleu, Cuisine Foundations, légumes glacés); https://lacuisinedebernard.com/en/turnips-with-sugar-glaze/ (navets glacés); https://www.pardonyourfrench.com/vichy-carrots-french-style-glazed-carrots/ (Vichy carrots in the 1903 Guide Culinaire).
4. (expert) The menu prints "Turnips & Shallots" and nothing about how they are cooked; the kitchen dossier's one breath line says glazed turnips, but that is a reading, not the menu, so the card says the chef confirms it. Which turnip, a purple top or a small Hakurei, is not printed.

## 6. Table grapes

1. California grows about 99 per cent of the table grapes grown commercially in the United States; a 1999 federal crop profile put the share at 97 per cent.
   Source: https://www.thesnack.net/core-article/featured/california-table-grape-commission/california-table-grapes-snapshot/anne-allen/002814 (California Table Grape Commission snapshot: over 99 per cent); https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2015-04/documents/exhibit_i.pdf (1999 crop profile: 97 per cent).
2. (expert) Table grapes are Vitis vinifera bred for the table, thin skinned, crisp and mostly seedless; wine grapes are smaller, thicker skinned and sweeter. The classic Sole Véronique uses peeled muscat grapes (dossier-the-kitchen.md, Redfish Véronique); the menu names no variety for the pickled grapes, so the variety and the grower are questions for the chef.

## 7. Leeks

1. The leek is an ancient crop native to the eastern Mediterranean and the Middle East, domesticated from the wild Allium ampeloprasum, whose Latin name means the allium of the vineyard.
   Source: https://www.britannica.com/plant/leek (native to eastern Mediterranean lands and the Middle East); https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Leek (domesticated from Allium ampeloprasum, the vineyard allium).
2. The leek is a national emblem of Wales. The legend has Saint David telling the Welsh to wear leeks to know friend from foe in a battle against the Saxons; the dates given range from the late fifth century to 640, and Shakespeare's Henry V has the custom. The legend is traditional, not documented history.
   Source: https://www.britannica.com/plant/leek (the emblem); https://plewsgardendesign.co.uk/leeks-for-st-davids-day (Saint David and the battle); https://chelseagreen.com/?p=1652376 (the 640 date; Henry V).
3. (expert) A leek grows by being earthed up, so sand lodges between its layers: it is split lengthwise and washed under running water before it is cooked. The white and pale green are tender; the dark green tops go to stock. Braised in butter and stock it turns silky and sweet, with none of an onion's bite (the shipped Braised leeks and fingerling potatoes card).

## 8. White wine in the kitchen

1. Escoffier's béarnaise is built on a reduction of white wine and vinegar with shallot, chervil, tarragon and crushed peppercorns, strained, then mounted with yolks and butter and finished with fresh tarragon and chervil; Foyot and Choron are its daughters, so the same reduction stands under the sauce Foyot and the Choron on the Eggs Sardou.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A9arnaise_sauce (summary of Escoffier's reduction: wine, vinegar, shallots, chervil, tarragon, crushed peppercorns, strained; tarragon and chervil to finish).
2. The house's own cards already say where white wine goes: beurre blanc is cold butter whisked into a reduction of white wine, vinegar and shallot (the Beurre blanc card, the Redfish Véronique); the mustard cream sauce starts with shallot in butter and a splash of white wine reduced (the Mustard cream sauce card, the Poussin à la Moutarde); the guide prints French béarnaise on the breakfast hanger and sauce Foyot on the Chateaubriand.
3. (expert) A cooking white should be dry, clean and unoaked; its acid and fruit survive the reduction, its alcohol does not, though a trace may remain. Which wine the kitchen reduces is not printed: a question for the chef, and a guest who avoids alcohol altogether is told so and sent to the kitchen.

## 9. Paprika and cayenne

1. Both are Capsicum annuum, the pepper Columbus carried from the Americas to Europe around 1493. The word cayenne most likely comes from a Tupi word for chilli; the link to the town of Cayenne in French Guiana is a folk association, though one account places the pepper's origin there and another near Mexico.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayenne_pepper (Capsicum annuum; the Tupi word; the Cayenne River and town); https://app.ckbk.com/reference/food77337c03s001e138 (Oxford Companion to Food: the place name assumed without firm evidence); https://www.burn-blog.com/9719/pepper-dossier-cayenne/ (Columbus, 1493).
2. Peppers reached Hungary in the 16th century under Ottoman rule; Hungarian farmers bred varieties for drying and grinding in the 19th century, and the two growing districts are around Szeged and Kalocsa in the south. Spain's pimentón de la Vera is dried over oak smoke and comes in dulce, agridulce and picante; the La Vera crop covers about 1,500 hectares and makes about 4.5 million kilograms a year under a protected name.
   Source: https://spice.alibaba.com/spice-basics/history-of-paprika (the Ottoman arrival, the 19th century breeding, Szeged and Kalocsa); https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paprika (pimentón de la Vera, oak smoked, the three grades, 1,500 hectares, 4.5 million kg).
3. The pepper reached Hungary under Ottoman rule in the 16th century (a 1569 Hungarian garden seed list names a Turkish red pepper), was at first ornamental and medicinal, and by the 19th century was the national spice, grown around Szeged and Kalocsa; so the component says it reached Hungary under the Ottomans and became the national spice by the 19th century, not that it was the spice of Hungary from the start (checked 17 October 2026).
   Source: https://www.fieryfoodscentral.com/?p=144 (the 1569 seed list of Margit Széchy, Turkisch rot Pfeffer); https://timelesstravelsteps.com/hungarian-paprika-history/ (Ottoman arrival, ornamental first, the national spice by the 19th century, Szeged and Kalocsa).
4. (expert) Paprika gives colour, sweetness and a mild warmth and burns easily, so it goes into the fat late or into a dry rub; cayenne is the ground dried pod at full heat, used in pinches. Prudhomme's blackening blend was paprika led with cayenne, black and white pepper and herbs (the shipped Blackening card; dossier-the-kitchen.md); the Creole seasoning card says every kitchen keeps its own proportions and the house blend is not printed. The Blackened Tofu's own notes say to name cayenne only if the kitchen confirms the blend.

## 10. The USDA beef grades

1. The USDA grades beef for quality on marbling, the fat within the muscle, and maturity, the animal's age at slaughter: Prime, Choice and Select are the three retail grades, Prime the most marbled from young cattle, Choice the broad middle with small to moderate marbling, Select the leanest with slight marbling. Grading is voluntary and paid for by the packer.
   Source: https://www.masterclass.com/articles/breaking-down-usda-meat-grades-difference-between-prime-choice-select-meat-grades (marbling and maturity; the three grades); https://www.umami.site/food-systems/prime-choice-select-understanding-usda-beef-grades/ (Prime from younger cattle; Choice and Select marbling).
2. How much beef grades Prime depends on the source and the year, and the share has risen sharply. Older trade figures run from under 2 per cent to about 5 per cent (about 2.9 per cent of carcasses as of June 2009 by one count). The USDA's own grading reports put Prime at about 10 per cent of graded beef in fiscal 2024 and 10.8 per cent in fiscal 2025; USDA weekly steer and heifer grading reports in March and September 2024 show Prime at roughly 10 per cent with Choice at 73 to 74 per cent and Select at 12 to 13 per cent; by 2025 and 2026 weekly Prime readings of 13 to 17 per cent are reported, and USDA is reviewing the grade standards. The component therefore gives no fixed small share: it says a few per cent for decades, near 10 per cent in the 2024 and 2025 reports, and higher since (checked 17 October 2026). Choice is the majority. The claim that most Prime goes to restaurants was unsourced and is dropped.
   Source: https://www.chowhound.com/2089883/what-beef-gets-usda-prime-grade (USDA grading reports: 10 per cent in 2024, 10.8 per cent in 2025); https://mccunecoop.com/news/story/29137007/national-str-hfr-estimated-grading-percent and https://heartlandcoop.com/news/story/28562075/national-str-hfr-estimated-grading-percent (USDA national steer and heifer estimated grading per cent, weeks ending 16 March and 7 September 2024); https://www.drovers.com/news/prime-beef-production-surges-select-grade-hits-record-lows (Prime above 17 per cent in recent weeks against 13 per cent a year earlier); https://www.cattlerange.com/articles/2026/04/supply-of-prime-choice-beef-continues-to-increase/ (Prime at 15 per cent); https://www.rfdtv.com/rising-prime-beef-production-drives-usda-grading-review (the USDA review); older figures: https://www.crowdcow.com/cooking/breeds/usda-beef-grades (less than 2 per cent Prime); https://www.smokedbbqsource.com/?p=5371 (about 2.9 per cent as of June 2009; roughly 70 per cent Choice); https://reluctantgourmet.com/?p=34881 (at most 5 per cent).
3. Brennan's prints Prime on the breakfast Creole-Spiced Hanger Steak only; the Creekstone card says Creekstone grades Prime and Choice, and the producer notes say never to call the dinner hanger or the Chateaubriand Prime unless the kitchen confirms it (producers-food-beef-eggs-duck-2026-10-06.md; the shipped Creekstone Farms beef card).

## 11. Steak temperatures, resting and slicing, searing

1. The USDA's guidance for whole cuts of beef, steaks, chops and roasts, is an internal temperature of 145°F measured with a thermometer, followed by a rest of at least three minutes; cooks may choose higher. Kitchens cook to preference below that figure: a chart built on the federal data lists medium-rare at 130 to 135°F, a warm red centre, and notes that where the final reading is below the whole muscle figure that is a stated personal choice.
   Source: https://www.fsis.usda.gov/es/node/4829 (145°F and a three minute rest); https://cheatsheets.davidveksler.com/meat-temperature-chart.html (130°F pulled, 132°F after a five minute rest, as medium-rare; the personal choice note); https://izzycooking.com/?p=58438 (medium-rare 130 to 135°F).
2. A rested steak keeps cooking: the internal temperature rises about 5 to 15°F while it rests, depending on thickness, so it is pulled early. The old story that resting lets juices soak back into the fibres is contested; one food science account reports that at the same internal temperature juice loss was the same rested or not, and that the hotter the meat when cut, the more readily its moisture escapes.
   Source: https://www.bbqguys.com/a/37280/grillabilities/basic/how-to-rest-meat (5 to 15°F carryover); https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250724-meat-resting-science (the Serious Eats experiment and the vapour pressure explanation).
3. Browning is the Maillard reaction, first described by the French chemist Louis Camille Maillard in 1912: amino acids and sugars react on a hot, dry surface, roughly 140 to 165 C, into hundreds of flavour compounds and the brown melanoidins. Surface moisture holds the temperature down, so meat is patted dry before it meets the pan.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maillard_reaction (Maillard, 1912; the mechanism); https://tasteoffrancemag.com/trending/what-is-the-maillard-reaction/ (140 to 165 C); https://theonlinegrill.com/maillard-reaction/ (patting dry).
4. (expert) The hanger is cut across its loose grain so each slice holds short fibres that part easily; the long muscle fibres run the length of the lobe and a slice along them chews like rope (dossier-the-kitchen.md, Creole-Spiced Hanger Steak: hot and fast to medium-rare, rested, sliced across the grain). The Chateaubriand is seared, roasted to about 52 to 54 C at the centre for medium-rare, rested ten minutes and carved thick across the grain (dossier-the-kitchen.md, Roasted Chateaubriand, cook's notes). The guide's temperature advice for the hangers is medium-rare to medium (guide.txt, both hanger cards); the Petite Filet's note asks the temperature with the tasting order. Whether the Chateaubriand is carved at the table or in the kitchen is a lineup question (guide.txt, the Chateaubriand service note).

## 12. Reduction, glazing, braising vegetables and roasting whole (a cook's knowledge)

1. (expert) Reduction is boiling a liquid down to concentrate it: wine and vinegar lose their alcohol and sharpen, stock thickens on its own gelatine into a glaze, cream thickens on its fat. It is the first step of béarnaise, Foyot and Choron, of beurre blanc, of the Madeira glaze and of the marchand de vin the guide calls a savoury red wine reduction (guide.txt, pdf p17); comp-2's Stock and glace card covers the stock side and the Madeira-glazed broccolini card says a Madeira glaze is the wine reduced with butter or stock.
2. (expert) Glazing a vegetable is the reduction in section 5.3: a little liquid, butter and often sugar cooked away until what remains coats the vegetable in a shine; a glaze on a roast or a fruit is the same idea with a syrup, a reduced stock or a reduced wine brushed on and set under heat. The menu prints glazed on the Chateaubriand's summer squash, the Creole Hanger Steak's broccolini, the duck's peaches and the Succotash's summer vegetables; which glaze each carries beyond the named Madeira and cane syrup is the chef's to say.
3. (expert) Braising a vegetable is gentler than braising a cut of meat: leeks, fennel, endive or celery are laid in a shallow pan with butter and a little stock or wine, covered, and cooked slowly until the liquid is nearly gone and the vegetable is soft through and glossy. The shipped Braising in red wine card covers the short rib on the Eggs Owen; the leeks on the Redfish Véronique are the vegetable case.
4. (expert) Roasting a joint whole keeps the centre moist and even while the outside browns; it is seared or started hot, finished at a moderate heat, rested, then carved. A whole roast takes longer than a single steak, which is why the guide tells the server to set the timing before the order (guide.txt, the Chateaubriand service note). Tableside carving is from the guéridon tradition the house already teaches (the lexicon's Guéridon term).
