## 1. The Sazerac Company and Sazerac Rye (Classic Sazerac)

1. A privately held company, headquartered in Metairie near New Orleans with its principal office in Louisville, owned by one family.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sazerac_Company (summary): "a privately held American alcoholic beverage company headquartered in Metairie, Louisiana, with its principal office in Louisville, Kentucky, and owned by William Goldring and his family." Also (summary): "William Goldring began buying shares of the company in 1984, and eventually purchased the entire company."
2. Founding dates differ: 1850 in the infobox (the Sazerac Coffee House), 1869 in the body (Thomas H. Handy's purchase).
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sazerac_Company (summary): "Wikipedia's infobox lists 1850, the year the Sazerac Coffee House was established. Its body text says the company was founded in 1869, when Handy bought the coffee house." And: "The company was founded after the purchase of a bar known as the Sazerac Coffee House in 1869 by Thomas H. Handy."
3. The company traces its roots to the Thomas H. Handy Co., a New Orleans liquor dealer that ran the Sazerac House.
   Source: https://www.spiritsanddistilling.com/dictionary/acref-9780199311132-e-176 (The Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails, summary): "the company traces its roots to the Thomas H. Handy Co., a New Orleans wholesale and retail liquor dealer and operator of the Sazerac House."
4. Handy lost money in railway investments and dissolved his firm, but kept control of Peychaud's bitters; he died in 1893 and the firm carried on under his widow and associates, including his two head bartenders.
   Source: same Oxford Companion entry and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sazerac_Company (summary): "After Handy loses his money in bad railroad investments, he is forced to dissolve Thos. H. Handy Co., though he maintains control of Peychaud's bitters." And: "After Handy died in 1893, the firm continued under the ownership of Handy's widow and several business associates, including his two head bartenders."
5. In 1919, with Prohibition looming, the firm was reorganised as the Sazerac Company and rode out Prohibition as a grocery wholesaler.
   Source: same (summary): "In 1919, with Prohibition looming, the firm was reorganized as the Sazerac Company, and it rode out Prohibition as a grocery wholesaler."
6. In 1948 the company was bought by New Orleans wholesale liquor partners, the family that owns it still.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sazerac_Company (summary): "In 1948, the Sazerac Company was purchased by the Magnolia Liquor Company, a New Orleans wine and spirits distributor." Also https://bizneworleans.com/making-a-house-a-home/ (summary): "the Sazerac Company was acquired in 1948 by Stephen Goldring and Malcolm Woldenberg, partners in a growing wholesale liquor and distribution business." And https://www.louisianatravel.com/distilleries/sazerac-house (summary): "The Goldring family acquired the company in 1948." Stephen Goldring lived from 1908 to 1996 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Goldring, summary).
7. In 1989 it bought brands from Seagram, and in 1992 the distillery now called Buffalo Trace, at Frankfort, Kentucky.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sazerac_Company (summary): "in 1989, the Sazerac Company acquired 7 brands from Seagram and 3 years later purchased the Buffalo Trace Distillery." And (summary, second search): "In 1992, the company purchased what is now Buffalo Trace Distillery."
8. In 2009 its principal office moved to Louisville.
   Source: same (summary): "In 2009, the company's principal office was moved to Louisville, Kentucky, reflecting an increased company focus on bourbon whiskey production."
9. It operates over 500 brands; by one 2026 trade report it is the largest United States spirits supplier by volume.
   Source: https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/look-inside-sazerac-modern-drinks-021946966.html (summary): "Sazerac operates over 500 brands worldwide." And https://www.shankennewsdaily.com/?p=39655 (summary): "it now ranks as the largest U.S. spirits supplier in volume at some 47 million cases, according to Impact Databank, while noting Diageo still leads by value." Not used in the profile: rankings change.
10. Sazerac Rye is a 90 proof straight rye with no age statement, distilled at Buffalo Trace; its mash bill is undisclosed, believed to be the distillery's low rye recipe.
    Source: https://breakingbourbon.com/review/sazerac-rye (summary): "The 90 proof Sazerac Rye is a no-age-statement straight rye from Buffalo Trace, and its mash bill is not officially disclosed. The mashbill is believed to be their low rye mashbill, approximately 51% rye, 39% corn, and 10% malted barley." And: "aged in new charred American oak barrels for an estimated four to six years, and the bottle no longer carries a formal age statement."
11. Reviewers find it light bodied, with apricot, orange peel, plum, caramel and vanilla; a retailer adds rye pepper, dried herbs and citrus zest.
    Source: same (summary): "sweet apricots, orange peel, and plums alongside caramel and vanilla, and notes a light body." And https://theliquorbarn.com/collections/american-whiskey/products/sazerac-rye-750ml (summary): "rye grain, black pepper, and citrus zest with hints of vanilla, mint on the nose, and a palate of rye pepper, dried herbs, caramel, and a touch of oak."
12. It shares its grain recipe with Sazerac 18 Year and Thomas H. Handy; Sazerac Rye Full Proof, at 125 proof, is a separate newer release.
    Source: same (summary): "this expression shares its mashbill with some of the most coveted rye whiskeys in America, including Sazerac 18 Year and Thomas H. Handy." And https://www.fredminnick.com/2025/11/13/buffalo-trace-launches-sazerac-rye-full-proof/ (summary): "the newer Full Proof release, which is bottled at 125 proof."
13. The Sazerac House, at 101 Magazine Street on the corner of Canal, opened on Wednesday 2 October; a museum, event space and production facility with a 500-gallon micro-distillery making Sazerac rye, and a whiskey and bitters production facility.
    Source: https://syncopatedtimes.com/the-sazerac-house-opens-on-magazine-st/ (summary): "the grand opening at 101 Magazine (at Canal) on a Wednesday, October 2nd." And https://www.louisianatravel.com/distilleries/sazerac-house (summary): "It houses a whiskey and bitters production facility ... On the ground floor facing Canal Street, a 500-gallon micro-distillery makes Sazerac rye whiskey." The year is 2019 (2 October 2019 was a Wednesday; research/dossier-the-bar.md: "(high) 2019: the Sazerac House, Canal and Magazine, museum and small distillery"). Tours "are generally free with a reservation".
14. The company story behind the drink: Sewell Taylor sold the Merchants Exchange Coffee House to import Sazerac de Forge et Fils cognac; Aaron Bird renamed it the Sazerac Coffee House; Thomas Handy took it over about 1870 as phylloxera ruined the European vineyards, and rye replaced cognac.
    Source: research/components-drinks-2026-10-05.md search 5 (Wikipedia, VinePair, Country Roads), and https://countryroadsmagazine.com/cuisine/Louisiana-foodways/the-sazerac-endures/ (summary, this pass): "Handy, the bar's bookkeeper, took over the Exchange Coffee House and renamed it the Sazerac Coffee House after its house cocktail."

Brennan's link: the menu prints "Sazerac rye whiskey" in the Classic Sazerac. Rye, bitters and rinse are all Sazerac Company brands (research/dossier-the-bar.md section 5). That is the whole public link.

## 2. Peychaud's Bitters and Peychaud's Barrel-Aged (Classic Sazerac, Origin Story)

1. Created between 1849 and 1857 by Antoine Amédée Peychaud, a Creole apothecary from the French colony of Saint-Domingue; gentian based, comparable to Angostura but with a stronger anise aroma and a background of mint.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peychaud%27s_Bitters (summary): "Peychaud's is a gentian-based bitters, comparable to Angostura but with a stronger anise aroma and a background of mint. It was created between 1849 and 1857 by Antoine Amédée Peychaud, a Creole apothecary from the French colony of Saint-Domingue."
2. Peychaud lived from 1803 to 1883.
   Source: https://www.spiritsanddistilling.com/dictionary/acref-9780199311132-e-824 (The Oxford Companion, summary): "invented by New Orleans pharmacist Antoine Amédée Peychaud (1803 to 1883)".
3. His apothecary stood at what was then 123 Royal Street, 437 Royal Street today, the block of Brennan's at 417.
   Source: research/components-drinks-2026-10-05.md search 2 (the Sazerac Company story page and Wikipedia) and research/bar-master-review-2026-10-04.md ("tell it as the Sazerac Company's account ... check at lineup before telling it").
4. In an 1857 advertisement in the New Orleans Bee he listed the city bars that sold his bitters, the nearby Sazerac House among them.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peychaud%27s_Bitters (summary): "In a 1857 advertisement in the now-defunct New Orleans Bee newspaper, Peychaud noted that his bitters could be found at an array of city bars, which included the nearby Sazerac House."
5. Thomas H. Handy acquired the bitters in the 1870s; one account gives 1873, when Peychaud sold his pharmacy, formula and brand name.
   Source: https://www.diffordsguide.com/cocktails/recipe/3390/sazerac (summary): "Handy bought the coffee house in 1869 and acquired Peychaud's Bitters in 1873, when Antoine Peychaud sold the rights to the brand." And https://www.cooksinfo.com/peychauds-bitters/ (summary): "the rights were bought out by a Thomas H. Handy in 1870 or in the 1870s."
6. Bottled at 35 percent alcohol; produced at the Buffalo Trace Distillery in Frankfort, Kentucky, for the Sazerac Company.
   Source: https://www.diffordsguide.com/beer-wine-spirits/201/peychauds-bitters (summary): "alc./vol: 35%". And https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peychaud%27s_Bitters (summary): "Bottled at 35% ABV (70 proof), Peychaud's is now produced at Buffalo Trace Distillery in Frankfort, Kentucky, under the Sazerac Company."
7. The recipe is a well-kept secret; one source names gentian root, cloves, anise, nutmeg and cinnamon.
   Source: https://cocktail-society.com/barkeeping/peychauds-bitters/ and https://www.saveur.com/article/Wine-and-Drink/Bitter-Sweet (summary): "One source lists gentian root, cloves, anise, nutmeg, and cinnamon. Another notes that the recipe is a well-kept secret."
8. The Sazerac Company sells Peychaud's Barrel-Aged Aromatic Cocktail Bitters: Peychaud's aged 140 days in Sazerac Rye whiskey barrels, 35 percent, adding oak, candied spice and vanilla.
   Source: https://alambika.ca/products/peychauds-barrel-aged-bitters-148ml (summary): "Peychaud's bitters that are aged for 140 days in Sazerac rye whiskey barrels." Retailers (https://67wine.com/products/peychauds-barrel-aged-aromatic-bitters-5-oz, summary): the ageing "adds notes of oak, candied spice, and vanilla to the original recipe." https://www.thespiritsbusiness.com/?p=35446 (summary): "Sazerac has released a rye whiskey barrel-aged version of Peychaud's Bitters ... available now in limited quantities". https://www.diffordsguide.com/beer-wine-spirits/6628/peychauds-barrel-aged-bitters (summary): sampled in February 2020, "spicy oak, cherry, vanilla, aniseed and mint."
9. The Sazerac House on Canal Street houses a whiskey and bitters production facility.
   Source: https://www.louisianatravel.com/distilleries/sazerac-house (summary), as in section 1, item 13.

Brennan's link: the menu prints Peychaud's in the Classic Sazerac and "barrel-aged Peychaud's" in the Origin Story. Whether the Origin Story's bottle is the Sazerac Company's Barrel-Aged release or bitters aged by the bar is not printed and not found. research/dossier-the-bar.md section 7 held that the barrel-aged bitters should not be attributed to a Sazerac Company release; this pass confirms such a release exists, and the attribution to Brennan's still waits for the bar.

Expert knowledge: Peychaud's is lighter and more floral than Angostura and turns a Sazerac rosy red; the Vieux Carré takes both Peychaud's and Angostura.

## 3. Herbsaint (Classic Sazerac)

1. Created in New Orleans in 1934 by J. Marion Legendre and Reginald Parker, who learnt absinthe making in France during the First World War; first sold after the repeal of Prohibition as Legendre Absinthe, though it held no wormwood.
   Source: research/components-drinks-2026-10-05.md search 3 (Wikipedia and retailers), and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbsaint (summary, this pass): "It first went on sale following the repeal of Prohibition, and was unique in its category as an absinthe substitute, as opposed to a pastis."
2. Within months the Federal Alcohol Control Administration objected to the word absinthe, and it became Herbsaint, from herbe sainte, the Creole name for wormwood.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbsaint (summary): "The Federal Alcohol Control Administration soon objected to Legendre's use of the word 'absinthe'." https://bevvy.co/articles/reintroducing-the-original-recipe-for-herbsaint (summary): "just months after launching his product, and in spite of the fact that it did not contain wormwood, the government forced him to remove the word absinthe from his brand name."
3. Absinthe had been banned in the United States since 1912.
   Source: https://www.diffordsguide.com/cocktails/recipe/3390/sazerac-new-orleans-style (summary): "Herbsaint first appeared in 1934, after absinthe was banned in the US in 1912." Also research/components-drinks-2026-10-05.md search 4.
4. The Sazerac Company bought J.M. Legendre and Co. in June 1949.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbsaint (summary): "the Sazerac Company bought J.M. Legendre & Co. in June 1949." And https://liquorandliqueurconnoisseur.com/episode/episode-116-herbsaint (summary): "in 1949 Legendre sold the brand to the Sazerac Company."
5. Its proof changed over the years; a different 90 proof recipe came in the mid-1950s and was the only Herbsaint by the early 1970s.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbsaint (summary): "Herbsaint was originally bottled at 120 proof (60%), but this was later reduced to 100 proof (50%), then changed to a different 90 proof (45%) recipe in the mid-1950s." The podcast places the change in 1950; both agree that by the early 1970s only the 90 proof remained. The 120 proof figure is not corroborated and is not used.
6. In December 2009, for the brand's 75th anniversary, the Sazerac Company reintroduced Legendre's original 100 proof recipe as Herbsaint Original; the 90 proof continues alongside it and remains the predominant bottling.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbsaint (summary): "in December 2009, the Sazerac Company reintroduced J.M. Legendre's original 100 proof recipe as Herbsaint Original." https://archives.beveragebusiness.com/2010/02/herbsaint-original/ (summary): "In honor of the 75th anniversary of Herbsaint, the local Sazerac Company dug into its archives for the original recipe and recreated the absinthe substitute first sold in 1934." And (search 24, summary): "The 90 proof version remains the predominant Herbsaint available today."
7. The company's brand page says its Herbsaint is still made with Legendre's original 1930s recipe, but names no production site.
   Source: https://www.sazerac.com/our-brands/sazerac-brands/herbsaint.html (summary, search 24): "Our Herbsaint is still made with Legendre's original 1930s recipe." This sits awkwardly with Wikipedia's account of a different 90 proof recipe, so neither claim is put to servers.
8. Herbsaint is "a brand name of anise-flavored liquor currently produced by the Sazerac Company and originally made in New Orleans, Louisiana."
   Source: https://www.cooksinfo.com/herbsaint-liqueur (summary, search 24).

Not found: where Herbsaint is distilled or bottled today. No result in search 24 named Buffalo Trace, Frankfort or any other site.

Brennan's link: the menu prints the Herbsaint rinse in the Classic Sazerac; the guide calls it "our local anise liqueur" and spells the sound ERB-saint (dossier-the-bar.md section 5).

## 4. Willett (Thompson's Dream)

1. John David Willett, born in 1841, was a master distiller who died in 1914; his son A. Lambert Willett followed him; Lambert's son Thompson Willett joined as assistant superintendent.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willett_Pot_Still_Reserve (summary): "John David Willett, born in 1841, was a master distiller who died in 1914. His son, A. Lambert Willett, picked up his father's profession. Lambert's son, 'Thompson' Willett, also joined him at the plant as assistant superintendent."
2. Lambert Willett bought a farm and, with his sons and led by Thompson Willett, founded the Willett Distilling Company there in 1936.
   Source: same (summary): "Lambert Willett later purchased a farm and, together with his sons and especially led by Thompson Willett, founded the Willett Distilling Company on the site." https://www.top4.com.au/product/detail/willett-7-year-rye-single-barrel (summary): "ground was broken on the Willett Distillery outside Bardstown in 1936."
3. Thompson Willett was born on 27 January 1909 in Bardstown and was the distillery's founder and president.
   Source: https://www.kentuckybourbonwhiskey.com/willett-story.html (summary): "born January 27, 1909, in Bardstown, and was founder and President of Willett Distillery in Bardstown."
4. The first batch, about 30 barrels, was made on 17 March 1937 by the family's own account; Wikipedia gives 17 March 1936.
   Source: https://www.kentuckybourbonwhiskey.com/willett-story.html (summary): "the first batch of Kentucky Bourbon Whiskey, which amounted to around 30 barrels, was produced on March 17, 1937." And Wikipedia (summary): "the first batch, which was approximately 30 barrels of spirit, was produced on the 17th March 1936." The earlier research (components-drinks search 9) also gave 17 March 1937.
5. Old Bardstown bourbon was introduced in the 1940s.
   Source: https://www.kentuckybourbonwhiskey.com/willett-story.html (summary): "The brand was introduced in the 1940's as Old Bardstown Kentucky Straight Bourbon Whiskey."
6. The distilling facilities shut in the early 1980s, after a brief spell making ethanol during the 1970s energy crisis.
   Source: https://www.connosr.com/willett-family-estate-straight-rye-whiskey-4-year-old-whisky-review-9730 (summary): "the distilling facilities were shut down in the early 1980s after a brief stint producing ethanol during the 1970s energy crisis."
7. On 1 July 1984 Thompson Willett's son-in-law bought the property and formed Kentucky Bourbon Distillers; the family firm long worked as an independent bottler.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willett_Pot_Still_Reserve (summary): "The distillery remained in Willett family ownership until 1st July 1984 when Even G. Kulsveen, son-in-law to Thompson Willett, purchased the property and formed Kentucky Bourbon Distillers Ltd." https://www.kentuckybourbonwhiskey.com/willett-story.html (summary): "His daughter Martha married Even G. Kulsveen in 1972."
8. Distilling restarted on the site on 21 January 2012, after several years of renovation.
   Source: https://www.kentuckybourbonwhiskey.com/willett-story.html and Wikipedia (summary): "After several years of renovations, on January 21st, the family starts up the distillery in 2012."
9. The first Willett-distilled whiskey, a two-year-old rye from the pot still, reached the market around 2015; its own bourbon followed in 2016. Older, longer-aged products had been distilled elsewhere.
   Source: https://vinepair.com/articles/ntk-willett-distillery/ (summary): "the first Willett-distilled whiskey, a two-year-old rye from the pot still, reached the market around 2015, and the first own-distilled bourbon followed in 2016." https://modernthirst.com/?p=1677 (summary): "the distillery had only been operating since 2012, so older longer-aged products were distilled elsewhere."
10. The Family Estate ryes are released at four years old and bottled at 110 proof, by VinePair's account; Top4 describes small batches from four-year-old barrels, hand bottled at barrel strength.
    Source: https://vinepair.com/articles/ntk-willett-distillery/ (summary): "the Family Estate ryes are released at four years old and bottled at 110 proof." https://www.top4.com.au/product/print_detail.php?id=3485 (summary): "an annual release of small batches from four-year-old barrels, hand-bottled at barrel strength."
11. Willett Pot Still Reserve debuted in 2008 in a bottle shaped like a pot still, fashioned from the original still blueprints; its first liquid was distilled elsewhere.
    Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willett_Pot_Still_Reserve (summary): "It debuted in 2008 in the signature pot still shaped bottle fashioned using the original still blueprints. Because the distillery was not producing at that time, the liquid in these bottles was initially produced elsewhere."
12. The distillery's address is 1869 Loretto Road, Bardstown.
    Source: https://www.kentuckybourbonwhiskey.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/WillettDistilleryPressPack.pdf (summary).

Brennan's link: the menu prints "Willett 4-year rye" in Thompson's Dream. The name of the drink is the house's own; no Willett bottling carries it, and the link to Thompson Willett remains an inference (components/drinks.json bar-thompsons-dream-name; dossier-the-bar.md section 6).

Expert knowledge: the Willett ryes are big, hot and spicy beside a 90 proof rye; the Ledger's own library entry calls the Family Estate bottlings sipping whiskey.

## 5. Rittenhouse Rye and Heaven Hill (Rumors are Flying)

1. Rittenhouse was first introduced in Philadelphia in 1934, named for Rittenhouse Square; reintroduced as a four-year-old Bottled-in-Bond expression in 1948.
   Source: https://distiller.com/spirits/rittenhouse-rye-bottled-in-bond (summary): "The brand was first introduced in Philadelphia in 1934 and named for Rittenhouse Square. It was reintroduced as a four-year-old Bottled-in-Bond expression in 1948."
2. Heaven Hill acquired the brand in 1993 and moved production from Pennsylvania to Kentucky.
   Source: same (summary): "Heaven Hill acquired the brand in 1993 and moved production from Pennsylvania to Kentucky." ModernThirst (https://modernthirst.com/2015/01/30/whiskey-review-rittenhouse-rye-bottled-in-bond/, summary): "produced in Louisville by Heaven Hill."
3. Bottled in Bond: four years old, 100 proof (50 percent), from a single distilling season, aged in a federally bonded warehouse.
   Source: same (summary): "It is four years old, bottled at 100 proof (50% ABV), and comes from a single distilling season." And (search 15, summary): "aged at least four years in a federally bonded warehouse."
4. The mash bill is 51 percent rye; Heaven Hill's own spec sheet and Difford's give 35 percent corn and 14 percent malted barley, while two reviewers give 37 and 12.
   Source: https://sites.salsify.com/bdf90657-7017-4b79-9030-f3fd0c5c46cc/a533d9a1-7cc6-4bde-bc5b-e897d8b5da7d/product/410-0160/Rittenhouse-Rye-Whiskey/ (Heaven Hill product sheet) and https://www.diffordsguide.com/beer-wine-spirits/1384/rittenhouse-bottled-in-bond (summary): "35% corn and 14% malted barley". https://breakingbourbon.com/review/rittenhouse-rye-bottled-in-bond (summary): "37% corn and 12% malted barley."
5. Heaven Hill calls it a nod to the classic Pennsylvania or Monongahela ryes, which used far more rye; at 51 percent it is a low rye mash bill typical of modern Kentucky rye.
