# The cellar: Brennan's as a master sommelier reads it

A dossier for a server at Brennan's, 417 Royal Street, New Orleans, built from the 51-page server guide (menus and the live wine list read 26 September 2026) and checked against 86 verified claims from four sommelier lenses. Written to be read on a phone between shifts.

## 0. Read this first

**Three labels, used on every line.**

- (pdf): what the server guide says. Page numbers are the guide's. Prices are copied as printed, with the date 26 September 2026. The guide itself warns on p1 that menus change seasonally and that you should confirm before quoting.
- (search): what a live web snippet says. There are none in this dossier. The session's search allowance was exhausted before any sommelier lens ran, and it was still exhausted when this dossier was written (three fresh queries were refused). WebFetch is blocked for every domain.
- (expert): professional knowledge, graded high, medium or low. Nothing marked (expert) is a fact about Brennan's unless the guide also says it. Treat every (expert) line as something to check with the floor sommelier or the back label before you say it to a guest.

**How the verdicts were applied.** Every claim in the four lenses was checked. Where a claim was refuted, only its correction appears here. Where a claim was disputed or could not be verified, it is marked as such in the text. The corrections that changed the dossier are: the by-the-glass list has six tasting-only wines, not five; the Banyuls 50 per cent floor is for Grenache noir alone; the Turtle Soup "toasty depth" line in the guide is fair; the Eggs Hussarde second choice should stay the still Pinot Noir; the Duck Confit Waffle keeps the Riesling first even if it is dry; the Durand second choice on the crab claws is not a contradiction; the Rare stays on the raw oysters; and the guide's avoid lines are more dish-specific than the lens said.

**House style.** No em dashes anywhere. Where the guide's text is quoted, its dashes have been replaced with commas or colons; the wording is otherwise as printed.

## 1. The programme on one page

**The award.** (pdf, p49) "Wine Spectator Grand Award cellar, strong in Burgundy and Champagne." That is the whole of it. No year, no bottle count, no selection count, and the sources list on p51 cites no Wine Spectator page. (expert, medium) Wine Spectator's Grand Award tier typically means 1,000 or more selections, serious depth in mature vintages, breadth of top producers, harmony with the menu and an in-person inspection; about 100 restaurants worldwide hold it in any year. The year the reopened Brennan's regained it is unverifiable in this run; the lens leaned to 2018 and one examiner recalled 2017. Do not quote a year until it is read from Wine Spectator's own listing, and date it when you do. Grand Awards are re-evaluated annually.

**The cellar's strengths.** (pdf, p49) Burgundy and Champagne. The by-the-glass list bears it out: six Burgundies and three Champagnes among 20 wines. (expert, medium) A Grand Award house in a French-Creole dining room would normally also hold deep Bordeaux and Napa; the guide does not say so. Treat those as unknown until the Binwise bottle list is read.

**The live list.** (pdf, p49 and p51) The by-the-glass list the guide used is the Binwise page (hub.binwise.com), linked from the restaurant's Wine Menu page. The restaurant's own "Cocktails & Wine by the Glass" page "shows an older, different glass list (e.g., Drouhin Chablis, Peter Michael, Rare 2013)". (pdf, p49) "Check the list you're handed on shift." (expert, high) BinWise is a restaurant inventory platform that publishes a hosted list that updates as the cellar changes, so it is the page the app should refresh from. Treat the site's cocktails page as stale for wine.

**Twenty wines, two kinds.** (pdf, p41 to p46) Fourteen carry a printed price, from $14 (C.H. Berres Riesling 2022, Domaine Durand Sauvignon Blanc 2025) to $75 (Rare Brut 2012), plus the $80 half-bottle of Dr. Hermann Erdener Prälat Auslese 2014 (375 ml). Six carry no price and appear only as tasting-menu pours: Charles Lafitte Brut NV, Fichet Château London Mâcon-Igé 2024, Louis Jadot Beaune 1er Cru 2023, Domaine de Châteaumar Cuvée Vincent Côtes du Rhône, Paul Hobbs Coombsville Cabernet Sauvignon 2021, Domaine La Tour Vieille Banyuls Reserva NV. The guide says of the Lafitte "Not on the by-the-glass list, ask whether it can be poured à la carte" (p42); it never says the other five can or cannot be sold by the glass. One internal wrinkle: p1 calls all 20 "wines from the by-the-glass list" while p42 says the Lafitte is not on it.

**Two Coravin pours.** (pdf, p41, p43, p49) Rare Brut 2012 at $75 and Domaine Leflaive Mâcon-Verzé 2022 at $40. The Leflaive is "needle through the cork" (p43). The Rare is "Coravin-preserved: reseal right after each pour" (p41). (expert, high) Those are two different devices. The needle-and-argon system is for still wine only. A preserved Champagne uses Coravin Sparkling, a clamp-on stopper that re-pressurises the opened bottle with CO2. So for the Rare the cork has been pulled; "no cork is pulled" is true only of the Leflaive. The scripts on p41 and p48 say the Rare is "opened with a Coravin"; the accurate phrase is "poured by the glass from a bottle kept fresh under Coravin". Ask the bar which device is in use and what the house limit is on days an opened bottle stays in service.

**The house Champagne.** (pdf, p41) "Brennan's Essential by Piper-Heidsieck Extra Brut NV", $28 a glass on the live list, $30 on the restaurant's own drinks page. See wine 1 below.

**The two tastings.** (pdf, p49) Traditional Breakfast at Brennan's, $80: Brandy Milk Punch, baked apple, turtle soup or gumbo with a Bloody Bull, Eggs Hussarde with Charles Lafitte Brut (4 oz), petite filet with Châteaumar Côtes du Rhône (4 oz), Bananas Foster with Congregation Coffee & Chicory; all listed drinks included. Dinner tasting, $80: Grand Isle Jewel oysters, Louisiana BBQ lobster, Redfish Véronique, Rohan duck, The Snickers; wine pairing supplement $120 for five 4 oz pours (Charles Lafitte Brut, Fichet Mâcon-Igé 2024, Jadot Beaune 1er Cru 2023, Paul Hobbs Coombsville Cabernet 2021, La Tour Vieille Banyuls Reserva). (expert, low) A pairing priced above the food is unusual. The guide is consistent on it across p6, p49 and p51, and both tastings sit at $80 in the restaurant's 80th year (p49 quotes "Celebrating 80 Years in 2026!"), which makes "80 for 80" anniversary pricing plausible rather than a transposition. Confirm on shift before quoting either number.

**Bubbles at Brennan's.** (pdf, p49) Roost Bar & Courtyard, Monday to Friday 2 to 6; Champagne sabering Fridays at 5. Rosé bottles $50 to $125 (Mirabelle to Pommery), bubbly cocktails $12 to $20 (Brennan's Champagne Cocktail $20), bar snacks from Creole spiced nuts $6 to a cheese board $24. (expert, high) Mirabelle is Schramsberg's California sparkling rosé, not Champagne, so say "sparkling rosé" for the bottom of that range and keep the word Champagne for the Pommery. Who sabers, which bottle, and whether guests take part are not stated anywhere.

**The Wine Room.** (pdf, p51) Cited as source 10 and never described: no location, seating, minimum spend or booking route. The guide also contradicts itself on where bottles live: "a red Burgundy from upstairs" (p24, say-it line), "from the cellar" (p24, serve line) and "from our cellar" (p48). (expert) In the French Quarter a "cellar" is often on an upper floor because of the water table, so both may be true. Pick one phrase once a manager confirms where the Wine Room sits.

**The sommelier.** (pdf, p24, p48) "Bring our sommelier over." Nobody is named; the only named staff member is executive chef Kris Padalino (since June 2025, p49). (expert, medium) Braithe Tidwell, an Advanced Sommelier, was wine director in the late 2010s when the award was regained. Who holds the title in 2026 is unknown. Do not name a current wine director; take names, titles, shifts and the signal for summoning one from the manager.

## 2. Service primer

Everything here is (expert, high) unless marked (pdf). It confirms or gently adjusts the guide's service lines.

**Champagne sweetness.** Brut Nature 0 to 3 g/L with no added sugar; Extra Brut 0 to 6; Brut under 12; Extra Dry 12 to 17; Sec 17 to 32; Demi-Sec 32 to 50; Doux over 50. (pdf, p41) calls Extra Brut "bone-dry". Strictly that word belongs to Brut Nature; "very dry" is the safer word at the table.

**Temperatures.** Non-vintage Champagne 8 to 10°C (46 to 50°F); prestige and vintage Champagne 10 to 12°C (50 to 54°F); Riesling, Albariño, Sauvignon 7 to 10°C (45 to 50°F); Chardonnay 10 to 13°C (50 to 55°F); light Pinot 13 to 15°C (55 to 59°F); medium reds 14 to 16°C; Bordeaux and Napa Cabernet 16 to 18°C (60 to 64°F); sweet Riesling 7 to 10°C; Banyuls 12 to 14°C. (pdf) The guide's still-wine ranges all fall inside these bands and can be used as printed. Its Champagne ranges (43 to 46°F for the house Extra Brut, 46 to 50°F for Rare) sit at the cold end. That is a preference, not an error; a low-dosage wine reads austere below 7°C, and a glass warms fast in New Orleans.

**Glass.** Tulip or white-wine glass for Champagne, flute or tulip for the crémant, white-wine glass for the crisp whites, a larger bowl for the Chardonnays, Burgundy bowl for Pinot, Bordeaux glass for the Cabernets, small dessert glass for the sweet wines. (pdf) matches on every wine.

**Coravin.** Two systems, see section 1. Houses that run Coravin by the glass usually log the date of first access, cap the pours per bottle and taste the bottle at lineup. Whether Brennan's does is unknown.

**Decanting.** Young, structured reds: 30 to 60 minutes. Old reds: stand upright a day ahead, decant only to leave the sediment, give no more than about 30 minutes of air. Light reds and all whites: none. (pdf) agrees on every wine; the 30 to 60 minute decant on p46 belongs to the 2021 Paul Hobbs, not the 2010 Rubicon.

**Open-bottle life.** Still table wine, stoppered and refrigerated: two to three days. A sixteen-year-old Cabernet: about a day. Sweet Riesling, recorked and refrigerated: a week or more. Oxidative Banyuls: weeks, as (pdf, p46) says.

### Champagne and sparkling

#### 1. Brennan's Essential by Piper-Heidsieck Extra Brut NV, $28 glass

**One breath.** "Our house Champagne, labelled for us by Piper-Heidsieck. Extra Brut, so very dry and crisp, and it keeps your palate fresh from the oysters to the hollandaise."

**(pdf, p41.)** Pinot Noir, Meunier, Chardonnay; Extra Brut; Champagne. Citrus, orchard fruit, brioche, a firm chalky finish. $28 on the live list, $30 on the restaurant's drinks page, "check the current price". First pick for Turtle Soup, Eggs Hussarde and Grand Isle Jewel Oysters. Serve 43 to 46°F in a tulip or white-wine glass; hold cork and cage together, turn the bottle, ease the cork out with a sigh. The p48 Celebration script calls it "our house Piper-Heidsieck Extra Brut".

**The story (expert).** Piper-Heidsieck, Reims, founded 1785 by Florens-Louis Heidsieck; the Piper name came in the 1830s. Owned since 2011 by the Descours family's EPI group with Charles Heidsieck and Rare. Cellar master Émilien Boutillat since 2018, who followed Régis Camus (high, though re-check the name before printing it). Marilyn Monroe's line, that she went to bed with Chanel No. 5 and woke with a glass of Piper-Heidsieck, is a safe table story.

**What this bottle probably is (unverifiable).** Piper-Heidsieck's trade cuvée is spelt "Essentiel": launched in the mid-2010s, Pinot Noir-led with Meunier and Chardonnay, around four years on lees, a low dosage around 5 to 6 g/L, disgorgement date on the back label. The grapes and category on the Brennan's list match it exactly, and Champagne houses do co-brand standard cuvées for restaurants. But the guide says only that the wine "carries the Brennan's name". Whether it is a relabelled Essentiel or a bespoke blend is unconfirmed. Until the sommelier or Piper-Heidsieck says more, say "labelled for Brennan's", not "made for us".

**Confirmed.** The grape list, the Extra Brut category, both prices and the date (pdf).

**Ask.** Bespoke blend or labelled Essentiel? How is the name spelt on the bottle? What disgorgement date is on current stock? Which price is charged tonight?

#### 2. Pierre Sparr Brut Rosé Crémant d'Alsace NV, $18 glass

**One breath.** "Pink bubbles from Alsace, all Pinot Noir and made exactly like Champagne. Dry, fresh strawberry, lovely with the tartare."

**(pdf, p41.)** Pinot Noir, traditional method. "Crémant d'Alsace rosé must be all Pinot Noir." Strawberry, redcurrant, dry, creamy mousse. First pick for the Steak Tartare Cannoli. Serve 43 to 46°F, flute or tulip, tilt the glass and pour in two stages.

**The story (expert).** The appellation rule is real: Crémant d'Alsace AOC, created 1976, requires second fermentation in bottle and at least nine months on lees, and its rosé may be made only from Pinot Noir (high). Maison Pierre Sparr is at Sigolsheim near Colmar; the family dates its vines to 1680, which is why the label says "since 1680"; in 2009 the business passed to the Cave de Beblenheim cooperative, which kept the brand (medium on the year). Expect about 12 months on lees, brut dosage, about 12 per cent alcohol.

**Confirmed.** Everything the guide says about the grape and the method is correct by rule. No vintage to check.

**Ask.** Nothing pressing. If a guest knows Alsace, the cooperative ownership is the detail to have straight.

#### 3. Rare Brut 2012 (Coravin), $75 glass

**One breath.** "Rare is Piper-Heidsieck's prestige Champagne, now its own house. Mostly Chardonnay, and only about a dozen vintages have ever been released. 2012 is one of the great modern years, and because we keep it under Coravin I can pour you a single glass."

**(pdf, p41, p48, p49.)** Chardonnay-led with Pinot Noir, vintage Champagne. "Rare began as Piper-Heidsieck's prestige cuvée." White peach, candied citrus, toasted almond, long fine mousse. The restaurant's own drinks page lists a 2013 Rare Millésime at the same price: "check which vintage is open." Serve 46 to 50°F, a touch warmer than the house bubbles, tulip or white-wine glass; reseal after each pour; present the bottle. Step up for the Grand Isle oysters, Turtle Soup, Eggs Hussarde and the Crab & Corn Omelette.

**The story (expert).** The label name is Rare Millésime. First vintage 1976, released in 1985 for the house bicentenary. The gold tiara dressing was designed by the Paris goldsmith Arthus-Bertrand in homage to the cuvée Florens-Louis Heidsieck presented to Marie Antoinette in 1785. Declared vintages to professional memory: 1976, 1979, 1985, 1988, 1990, 1998, 1999, 2002, 2006, 2008, 2012, 2013, plus Rare Rosé 2007, 2008 and 2012 (medium on completeness). Since 2018 Rare has been run as its own house inside EPI, with Régis Camus as cellar master from that date; there were reports around 2024 that he was retiring, so say "cellar master since 2018" and do not name a current one. Rare 2012: about 70 per cent Chardonnay, 30 per cent Pinot Noir, roughly eight years in cellar, dosage about 8 g/L, launched 2021; the exact figures are from memory and should be read from the technical sheet. The 2012 season was brutal (frost, hail, rot, tiny crop) and the harvest superb; it ranks with 2002 and 2008 among the century's best. Rare 2013, the next release, is tauter and more citrus-driven.

**Service (expert).** Coravin Sparkling stopper, so a cork has been pulled. 10 to 12°C shows the almond and toast; the guide's 46 to 50°F is at the cool edge. Tulip or Burgundy bowl, never a coupe. Name the vintage you are actually pouring.

**Confirmed.** Price, Coravin status and the 2012 versus 2013 discrepancy (pdf). The guide's "older" label for the restaurant's page is its own inference; either list could be the stale one.

**Ask.** Which vintage is open tonight? Does the cellar hold both? Which Coravin device, and how long does an opened bottle stay in service?

#### 4. Charles Lafitte Brut Champagne NV, tasting pour, no glass price

**One breath.** "A crisp, fruity brut Champagne from a house with roots to 1834, poured to wake the palate right before the Hussarde."

**(pdf, p42, p49.)** Champagne blend, Brut. The pairing Champagne on both tastings, 4 oz: with Eggs Hussarde at breakfast, with the Grand Isle Jewel oysters at dinner. Dry, fresh citrus and apple, light toast. "Not on the by-the-glass list, ask whether it can be poured à la carte." Serve 43 to 46°F in a tulip; keep the open bottle stoppered and iced between courses; "don't double-pour" on the Hussarde (p18).

**The story (expert, medium).** Charles Lafitte trades on a founding date of 1834 and belongs to Vranken-Pommery Monopole, the group of Paul-François Vranken that also owns Pommery, Heidsieck & Co Monopole and Vranken, acquired in the second half of the 1990s. Its entry Bruts are Pinot Noir and Meunier-led three-grape blends at standard brut dosage, sold at supermarket and on-trade prices. Present it as an honest dry Brut; it is softer and rounder than the Extra Brut, which actually suits the sweet-savoury marchand de vin. Which cuvée Brennan's pours (standard Brut, Brut Prestige, or the grand cru Orgueil de France) is unknown.

**Confirmed.** Its role on both tastings and the absence of a glass price (pdf).

**Ask.** Can it be poured à la carte, at what price, and which cuvée is it?

### Riesling

#### 5. C.H. Berres 'Old Vines' Riesling 2022, $14 glass

**One breath.** "A light, electric Riesling from the Mosel, from vines older than most of us. Ripe peach and wet slate, with just enough fruit to cool the gumbo's heat."

**(pdf, p42.)** Riesling, Mosel. Green apple, peach, slate, racy acidity. "Taste the current bottle so you can tell guests honestly how dry or off-dry it is." First pick for Seafood Gumbo and Duck Confit Waffle. Serve 45 to 48°F, white-wine glass, keep well chilled with spicy dishes.

**The sweetness question (disputed).** The guide hedges in four places (p4, p16, p20, p42) and then on p6 calls it "off-dry-leaning Riesling". So it is inconsistent rather than silent. (expert, medium) An export "Old Vines" bottling from the Mosel without a Trocken designation is more often off-dry or feinherb than bone-dry, at about 10.5 to 11.5 per cent alcohol. The alcohol is printed on the label, and in Mosel Riesling a lower figure is a fair sign of more sugar left in the wine. Settle it once at lineup, write the answer on p42, and make the four hedges agree.

**The story (expert, medium).** Weingut C.H. Berres is at Ürzig in the Middle Mosel, between Erden and Zeltingen. The family dates its winemaking to 1510 and presents itself as 21 generations, with Markus Berres in charge since the early 2000s. Its core sites are the Ürziger Würzgarten (red volcanic slate, the "spice garden") and the Erdener Treppchen, a few hundred metres from the Prälat that gives the dessert wine on this list. Attribute the 1510 date and the generation count to the family ("the family dates its vines to 1510"). 2022 on the Mosel was hot and dry: riper fruit, slightly softer acid than 2021.

**Confirmed.** Price, region and the taste-at-lineup instruction (pdf).

**Ask.** Residual sugar and alcohol of the 2022 in stock. Does it read dry, feinherb or off-dry today? Screwcap or cork?

### Dry and crisp whites

#### 6. Pazo das Bruxas Albariño 2025, $16 glass

**One breath.** "Albariño from Spain's Atlantic coast, made by the Torres family. Lemon, white peach and a salty snap: it was born for seafood."

**(pdf, p42.)** Albariño, Rías Baixas. Lemon, white peach, "a saline, almost sea-spray snap; unoaked and fresh". First pick for the Creole Caesar and Gulf Fish en Papillote. Serve 45 to 48°F, white-wine glass, keep it iced between pours because the salinity fades as it warms.

**The story (expert, medium to high).** The guide never says Torres, and that is the most recognisable producer name on the white list. Pazo das Bruxas is the Rías Baixas Albariño of Familia Torres, the Penedès house founded in 1870, now in its fifth generation. First vintage 2014, released 2015. The name means "manor of the witches" and nods to Galician meiga folklore. Fruit from the Val do Salnés, the cool granite coast, and the Ulla river area; stainless steel, a few months on lees, no oak, about 12.5 to 13 per cent. The oaked sibling is Pazo Torre Penelas, a different wine. A 2025 on a September 2026 list is normal: picked September, bottled winter, in the US by late spring (high).

**Confirmed.** Price, region, unoaked style (pdf).

**Ask.** Is the open bottle 2024 or 2025?

#### 7. Domaine Durand Sauvignon Blanc 2025, $14 glass

**One breath.** "A crisp Loire Sauvignon Blanc: grapefruit and cut grass, dry as a bone, and one of the very few wines that is happy next to an artichoke."

**(pdf, p42 to p43.)** Sauvignon Blanc, Loire Valley, no appellation given. Grapefruit, cut grass, flint, bone-dry. First pick for Eggs Sardou. Serve 45 to 48°F, white-wine glass, keep it in the ice bucket.

**The appellation (expert, low).** Nothing in the guide names it. The well-known Domaine Durand of Cornas makes Syrah and no Sauvignon, so that is not this wine. More than one Loire grower trades as Durand. At $14 a glass the by-the-glass economics point to Touraine AOC or IGP Val de Loire rather than Sancerre or Pouilly-Fumé. That is price inference, not a label check. Say "Loire Sauvignon Blanc" and do not say "Sancerre" until the bottle is read.

**Why it loves artichokes (expert, high).** Artichokes contain cynarin, which briefly blocks sweet receptors so that the next sip tastes sweeter or oddly flat; oak and tannin come out metallic. A bone-dry, high-acid, unoaked, herbal white has little fruit sweetness to exaggerate and no oak to turn, and tarragon in the Choron is a direct bridge. In Eggs Sardou the artichoke is cooked and buffered by hollandaise and spinach, which softens the effect further. The guide's p18 explanation is sound.

**Confirmed.** Price, region, the Sardou logic (pdf). 2025 is plausible: Touraine Sauvignon is bottled in spring and sold from then (high).

**Ask.** What appellation is on the label, and who imports it?

### Chardonnay and fuller whites

#### 8. Gainey Estate Vineyards Chardonnay 2023, $17 glass

**One breath.** "A California Chardonnay from Santa Barbara's cool Sta. Rita Hills: creamy lemon curd and baked apple, but the ocean fog keeps it fresh and bright."

**(pdf, p43.)** Chardonnay, Sta. Rita Hills; the say-it line says "from Santa Barbara County". Lemon curd, baked apple, creamy texture, bright acidity. First pick for Louisiana Oysters, Pecan Gulf Fish and Poussin à la Moutarde. Serve 50 to 54°F in a larger white or Chardonnay glass; if it comes out too cold, rest it a few minutes.

**The story (expert, medium to high).** Both place names are right: the AVA sits inside the county. Gainey Vineyard is on Highway 246 in the Santa Ynez Valley. Daniel Gainey bought the ranch in 1962; the winery opened in 1984; the family bought Evan's Ranch in what became the Sta. Rita Hills in 1998, the source of its Pinot and Chardonnay. The AVA was approved in 2001. Its east-west valleys, rare on the Pacific coast, funnel fog and wind inland, so the season is long and cool and acidity holds. The name is written "Sta." after a settlement with Chile's Viña Santa Rita, effective 2006. The wine is barrel-fermented with partial malolactic and lees stirring, about 13.5 to 14.5 per cent, which gives exactly the lemon curd and cream the guide describes. 2023 in California was wet, cool and late, with balanced, fresh wines released 2024 to 2025.

**One caution for the crab dishes.** The guide warns off "heavy, buttery Chardonnay" on the omelette (p23) and brown butter on the claws (p12). Gainey is barrel-fermented. Taste it at lineup and confirm it reads fresh before leading with it on crab (see section 4).

**Confirmed.** Price and appellation as printed (pdf).

**Ask.** Which tier is poured (the Sta. Rita Hills estate bottling or the Limited Selection), what appellation is on the current label, and what alcohol?

#### 9. Domaine Leflaive Mâcon-Verzé 2022 (Coravin), $40 glass

**One breath.** "A Chardonnay from Domaine Leflaive, the legendary biodynamic estate in Puligny-Montrachet, from their vines further south in the Mâconnais: pure, chalky and bright, a taste of the great house by the glass."

**(pdf, p43.)** Chardonnay, Mâconnais. "From one of Burgundy's great white-wine estates, best known for Puligny-Montrachet": citrus, white flowers, chalky texture, gentle richness. First pick for the Crab & Corn Omelette, Louisiana Crab Claws and Redfish Véronique. Serve 50 to 54°F in a white Burgundy bowl. Needle through the cork, pour, return the bottle to the cooler, present the label.

**The story (expert, high on the family and vineyards, medium on dates).** The Leflaive family has been in Puligny since 1717; Joseph Leflaive built the domaine from 1920. It holds Montrachet (a small plot bought in 1991), Chevalier-Montrachet, Bâtard-Montrachet and Bienvenues-Bâtard-Montrachet, plus Puligny premiers crus such as Pucelles, Combettes, Folatières and Clavoillon. Anne-Claude Leflaive began biodynamic trials in 1990; the whole estate was converted by 1997; she ran it until her death in 2015. Her nephew Brice de La Morandière took over that year, with Pierre Vincent as winemaker from 2017; both are present-tense facts that could have changed, so do not state them as current without checking. Do not confuse the domaine with Olivier Leflaive, the négociant house founded in 1984 in the same village.

**The Mâcon wine (expert, medium to high).** Anne-Claude bought about nine hectares at Verzé in 2004; the Mâconnais holding grew to roughly 24 hectares, farmed biodynamically from the start; 2004 was the first vintage. Mâcon-Verzé is the Mâcon appellation with a village name attached, the Mâcon-Villages tier, not Pouilly-Fuissé. Leflaive does also make a Pouilly-Fuissé and a single-plot Mâcon-Verzé "Le Monté", so say "this glass is a Mâcon, not a Pouilly-Fuissé" rather than implying the house makes none. Fermented in barrel with little or no new wood, finished in tank, so the oak is quiet. 2022 was warm, early and generous with better acidity than feared; drinks now and through about 2028.

**Confirmed.** Price, Coravin needle service, Puligny pedigree (pdf).

**Ask.** Is it the straight Mâcon-Verzé or a single-plot cuvée? What closure is on the bottle? What is the house limit on Coravin days?

#### 10. Fichet 'Château London' Mâcon-Igé 2024, tasting pour, no glass price

**One breath.** "An unoaked Mâcon Chardonnay from the Fichet family. Apple and citrus with a touch of roundness, chosen to carry the lobster's butter and stand up to the spice."

**(pdf, p43, p49.)** Chardonnay, Mâconnais. The dinner tasting's white, 4 oz, with the Louisiana BBQ Lobster. Apple, citrus, gentle roundness, fresh finish. Serve 50 to 53°F in a white-wine glass; open at the start of service and keep it in a cooler, not an ice bath.

**The story (expert, medium).** Domaine Fichet is a family estate at the hamlet of La Cra in Igé, northern Mâconnais, run by the brothers Pierre-Yves and Olivier Fichet (whether both still run it could not be checked). "Château London" is the name of a lieu-dit, a named vineyard plot, on the Igé hillside. There is no castle, and "Château" in a Mâconnais plot name does not imply one. The origin of the word "London" could not be confirmed, so the safe table line is "a named vineyard plot in the village of Igé" with no story about England. Mâcon-Igé is the Mâcon appellation with a village name, the same tier as Mâcon-Verzé; never say "premier cru", since the Mâconnais has none outside Pouilly-Fuissé. Trade notes describe the cuvée as stainless-steel, lees-aged, no oak. Not to be confused with Jean-Philippe Fichet of Meursault. 2024 in Burgundy was one of the wettest seasons on record, with frost, mildew and a small late harvest; the Mâconnais lost less than the Côte d'Or, and the whites are fresh and moderate in alcohol. A 2024 Mâcon on a September 2026 list is normal.

**One breath.** "Coppola's Rutherford flagship at sixteen years: cassis, tobacco and that Rutherford dust, powerful but silky."

## 4. The pairings, read hard

The guide holds 35 pairing blocks: 4 tasting courses, 8 starters, 2 soups, 1 salad, 14 entrées, 6 desserts (pdf). Sides and signature drinks carry no wine block. The pairings lens tallied 22 agree, 11 agree with a caveat, 2 disagree. That tally is the lens's own judgement and could not be checked independently; another examiner might count the Pineapple Tarte Tatin's condensed-milk anglaise as a third disagreement. The dish-level logic is mostly sound. The faults are structural.

### 4.1 Structural faults the house should settle once

1. **Availability.** The guide offers two tasting-only wines à la carte with a hedge: Banyuls (first pick for three desserts, second for two) and the Jadot Beaune 1er Cru (second choice on the Chateaubriand and the Redfish). A training guide must not teach a server to offer a wine the house may not sell by the glass. Settle both.
2. **The sweet-wine gap.** One $80 half-bottle for seven dishes. See wine 19.
3. **The Berres sweetness.** Inconsistent, not silent (disputed): four hedges and one "off-dry-leaning". Six pairings hinge on it. Taste, decide, print.
4. **Price-to-plate.** Leflaive at $40 is first pick for $22 crab claws and a $28 omelette. The guide already uses "Gainey first, Leflaive step up" on the oysters, the Pecan Gulf Fish and the Poussin; use the same order on both crab dishes, after confirming the Gainey reads fresh rather than oaky. A $70 Rubicon as a step up on the breakfast filet is a line not to open unprompted.
5. **Two second choices that break the guide's own sweetness rule.** The Pierre Sparr Brut Rosé on the Blackberry Trifle (p8) and the Framboise French Toast (p9). On both pages the guide predicts the wine will taste "drier and tarter" and its own Avoid line forbids the same style. Remove it as a recommendation. No dry wine on the list beats the Auslese with berry curds; say so, and offer the Catalina Island or coffee. The Revive cold-pressed juice ($10, p49) is never used and could serve here.
6. **Two uncertainties the guide already flags.** Essential $28 or $30; Rare 2012 or 2013. Both need a decision.

### 4.2 Where the hostile read was itself corrected

These are the lens claims that did not survive verification. The guide stands on each.

- **Turtle Soup (p14 to p15).** The lens called "its toasty depth echoes the aged Sherry" overstated and gave the Sherry echo to the Rare as an "oxidative" wine. Correction: the Essentiel's extended lees ageing gives genuine brioche and toast, so the line is a fair secondary point behind dryness and bubbles, which the guide already leads with; p15 already says the Rare meets the Sherry "even more closely". Neither Piper wine is oxidative; Rare is a lees-aged, candied, toasted Chardonnay-led cuvée. If anything is tightened, say "brioche and toast from long lees ageing". The Leflaive remains the right still-wine alternative on weight, and a low-tannin Pinot is a legitimate red call.
- **Eggs Hussarde (p17 to p18).** The lens wanted the Pierre Sparr as second choice. Correction: the guide's formula (p3) defines a second choice as a different style, and the first pick is sparkling, so the still Damien Martin Pinot is the correct second; a light, earthy Pinot is the classic partner for a mushroom and red-wine brown sauce. Offer the Sparr as a third, same-style option (pink, $18, bubbles) exactly as the guide does on Sardou and Owen. Confirm the kitchen's marchand de vin build before describing it.
- **Louisiana Crab Claws (p12).** The lens said the Durand second choice contradicts p10. Correction: p10 warns off lean whites only for the acid-free BBQ butter on the oysters; the claws carry a brown butter vinaigrette with fines herbes, so the acid rule applies and a Loire Sauvignon is a legitimate different-style second (crab and Sancerre is a classic). Gainey belongs as the lower-priced same-style lead, not the second. If a bubbles option is wanted, the house Extra Brut or the Rare, whose own page lists crab claws.
- **Rare on raw oysters (p13).** The lens wanted the $75 step up moved to crab and omelette. Correction: p41 lists raw oysters first among Rare's matches and p13 names chalk as the reason; a chalky, Chardonnay-led vintage Champagne is a standard oyster pairing. The view that it carries too much toast is one taste, labelled (expert, low). Leave it.
- **Duck Confit Waffle (p20).** The lens said that if the Berres is dry, lead with the Pinot. Correction: keep the Riesling first even if dry, and tell the guest the maple may make it read a touch tart, as p20 instructs. A dry white with no tannin fails more gently than a dry red with some: maple firms tannin and egg yolk turns it metallic, which the guide's own lines say. Offer the Pinot only to a guest who wants red, served cool. The closest match on the list is a 2 to 3 oz pour of the Auslese, which depends on the house pouring the half-bottle by the glass.
- **Seafood Gumbo (p15 to p16).** The lens called the Pinot a "near-equal". Disputed: it is a confident second for a guest who wants red, not an equal. It runs about 13 per cent against roughly 10 to 11, has no sugar to cushion heat, and carries a little tannin against shrimp, crab and oysters, which the guide's Avoid line flags. If the kitchen's spice runs hotter than "mild to moderate", the gap widens.
- **The Avoid lines.** The lens said most read "oaky Chardonnay and tannic reds". Correction: of 35 lines, 12 name both, 11 name one of them (often with another style), and 12 name neither; ten sweet blocks warn off dry wine or Banyuls, the Rohan duck warns off lean whites and tart light reds, the breakfast hanger warns off jammy reds and crisp whites. Every line gives a reason tied to a named component of the plate. The Caesar line is one of the formulaic 12, not an exception. The fair criticism is narrower: the 12 seafood, egg and tomato lines share one template, so the reason clause does all the teaching.

### 4.3 Where the hostile read stands

- **Bananas Foster (p33) and Bread Pudding (p35): lead with Banyuls.** (expert, medium) Rum, butter, brown sugar and flambéed caramel, or whiskey caramel and pecan praline, sit in the caramel-and-nut group, whose textbook partners are oxidative fortifieds: tawny port, Bual Madeira, Banyuls Reserva. The flip rests on weight and bridge, not sweetness; a Prälat Auslese usually carries more residual sugar than a Banyuls Reserva, so the sweetness rule does not favour either. The Auslese stays as the bright second for contrast, and it is the only one of the two that can be sold today, until Banyuls is confirmed à la carte. Nothing changes until that answer exists.
- **Rohan Duck (p6 to p7).** A young 2021 Napa Cabernet against cane-syrup peaches is a real tension and the guide admits it ("The glaze's sweetness can firm up tannin, so let the fruit lead"). From the list, the Durban is the better technical red: ripe fruit, round tannin, body for the fat. The inconsistency stands: the Avoid line excludes "tart light reds" while the second choice is a light Bourgogne the guide itself calls bright in acidity. Either state that a ripe 2023 Bourgogne is not tart, or change the second choice.
- **BBQ Lobster (p5 to p6).** By the heat rule the Riesling is the sharper tool for the 'nduja; the Mâcon is chosen for the butter and is fixed on the tasting. The server-facing line admits it; the guest-facing line does not. One honest sentence: "the Mâcon is poured for the lobster and the butter; if the 'nduja runs hot, the Berres is lighter and a touch sweeter, and we can swap it."
- **Chateaubriand (p24 to p25).** Aged Cabernet with lean tenderloin and sauce Foyot is textbook. Caveats stand: $70 a glass is $140 for two, close to a bottle; the open-bottle life is unstated; the second choice has no glass price. Moulin d'Issan is the classic at $16; name the Beaune only if it is poured à la carte; the p48 script's sommelier handoff is right.
- **Breakfast intake.** The Châteaumar is the right red. The caveat is arithmetic: a brandy milk punch, a vodka Bloody Bull, 4 oz Champagne and 4 oz red before a rum flambé is roughly four standard drinks in a morning. The guide offers the spirit-free swaps (p47, p50) but never tells servers to watch intake or offer them early. Add that line.
- **Confirmed as correct, with the reason in one sentence:** Grand Isle oysters and Extra Brut (salt makes wine taste fuller, bubbles scrub brine); Creole Tomato Tostada and Minuty (acid matches acid, burrata softens the edge); Creole Caesar and Albariño (unoaked and saline, so smoke does not turn bitter and umami has no tannin to harden; the Extra Brut second is an equal); Eggs Sardou and Durand (the artichoke exception); Eggs Owen and Durban (both sauces are red wine); Blackened Tofu and Pinot (umami hates tannin, heat hates alcohol, mushroom is the bridge; say "cayenne" only if the kitchen confirms the blend); dinner Hanger and Durban (chili crisp wants soft tannin served cool); The Snickers and Banyuls (chocolate makes dry wine bitter; salt makes a sweet wine sweeter; 4 oz is generous, 2 to 3 oz is the norm); Lemon Tart and Auslese (only sweet plus piercing acid survives; the Banyuls avoid line is right); Baked Apple and Auslese (the best block in the book); Steak Tartare Cannoli and Sparr (bubbles and fat; the rosé rule is real); Papillote and Albariño; Poussin and Gainey (the Sparr is a co-equal for fried chicken).

### 4.4 Zero-proof

(pdf, p50) Catalina Island (Seedlip Grove 42, local watermelon, tonic, $15) and Black Hills (Lyre's non-alcoholic agave, basil purée, lime, $14); coffee $11, New Orleans-style with chicory from Congregation, and the Café Glacé de la Maison (chicory cold brew, cardamom-clove syrup, orange flower foam). (expert) The guide's two-bucket rule is sound and learnable in a minute: Catalina stands in for Champagne and rosé, Black Hills for a crisp white or a soft red. Coffee with chicory for the desserts is simply correct: roast and bitterness balance sugar, and the guide rightly keeps it away from the lemon and pineapple desserts. The Café Glacé's cardamom-clove syrup is a real bridge to maple on the duck waffle and to a spiced baked apple; one caution, the menu line for the baked apple names no spice, so confirm the crumble is spiced before selling that bridge. The lens's proposal to swap Black Hills onto the raw oysters is a taste judgement that cannot be settled: lime and basil echo the mignonette, but the mignonette already supplies acid and the tonic supplies the one thing the plate lacks, bubbles. Test both at lineup.

### 4.5 Five pairings to teach from

1. Grand Isle Jewel Oysters and the house Extra Brut: salt and acid. "The salt makes the wine taste fuller and the bubbles keep the oyster fresh."
2. Creole Tomato Tostada and Minuty: acid matches acid. "Fresh tomatoes are tart, so the wine has to be bright too."
3. Eggs Sardou and the Loire Sauvignon: the difficult ingredient and the bridge. "Artichokes are tricky, but Loire Sauvignon loves them."
4. Baked Apple and the Auslese, with the Lemon Tart as companion: sweetness, and why acid keeps a sweet wine light.
5. Dinner Creole Hanger and Durban: heat. "The chili crisp has real heat, so I want a red that's ripe and soft, served cool."

### 4.6 The five hardest plates

Duck Confit Waffle (acid and sweetness at once, and the only wine with both is an $80 half-bottle); Eggs Hussarde (two sauces pulling opposite ways, plus coffee-cured bacon, at breakfast); Bananas Foster (sweeter and heavier than almost any wine, and the "we don't drink" conversation on p47); Blackened Tofu (umami and heat on a neutral protein; only Pinot and Riesling survive, served cool); Roasted Rohan Duck (fat says Cabernet, sweet glaze says no tannin, and the tasting pours the Cabernet).

Fields: id; listName as printed on the list; labelName as printed on the bottle (e.g. 'Rare Millésime 2012' for the list's 'Rare Brut 2012'); producer; category (sparkling, aromatic white, crisp white, Chardonnay, rosé, light red, medium red, full red, sweet, fortified); appellation; tier (regional, village, premier cru, cru classé, AVA, none); country; grapes[] each with grape, share, basis, confidence; vintage; vintageStatus (printed, NV, not printed, confirm on bottle); vintageNote; price{amount, unit (glass, half-bottle, tasting pour), currency, source, readOn}; altPrices[] (e.g. Essential $30 on the site page); role[] (by the glass, tasting pour, step up, second choice, celebration pour); soldALaCarte (yes, no, confirm; six wines are 'confirm' today); preservation (none, Coravin needle, Coravin Sparkling, stopper and fridge, same-night sell-through); openBottleLife; serve{tempF, tempC, expertTempF, glass, decant, sediment}; sweetness (dry, off-dry, sweet, unresolved; Berres is 'unresolved'); abv from the label; oneBreath; story20s; story45s; firstPickFor[]; secondChoiceFor[]; stepUpFor[]; avoidWith[]; facts[] each with text, basis, confidence, verified; askTheSommelier[]; verification{status, lastChecked, searchAvailable}. 4. ServiceRule. Fields: scope (category or wine id); temperatureF; temperatureC; glass; decant; preservation; openBottleLife; pourSize; basis; houseConfirmed boolean. Examples: sparkling NV {43 to 46°F pdf, 45 to 50°F expert preference, tulip or white-wine glass, no decant}; Rubicon {60 to 64°F, large Bordeaux, upright a day ahead, decant only off sediment, max 30 minutes air, preservation 'unknown, ask', houseConfirmed false}.

5. Correction (the consolidated table in section 5). Fields: page; quote; problem; correction; confidence; status (proposed, accepted, rejected, refuted); owner (sommelier, chef, manager, app). Every entry must be stored without em or en dashes.

6. OpenQuestion (section 6). Fields: text; audience (sommelier, chef, manager); blocksRecords[] (the wine or pairing ids that cannot be finalised until answered); status (open, answered); answer; answeredOn; answeredBy. Example: 'Is the La Tour Vieille Banyuls Reserva poured à la carte, at breakfast and dinner, and at what price per 2 to 3 oz?' blocks pairing-bananas-foster, pairing-bread-pudding, pairing-cafe-brulot-coffee-cake, pairing-cherries-jubilee, pairing-the-snickers.
