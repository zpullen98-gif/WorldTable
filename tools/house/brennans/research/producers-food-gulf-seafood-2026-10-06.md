## 1. Grand Isle Jewels off-bottom oysters (Grand Isle Jewel Oysters, dinner tasting course one)

1. Grand Isle Jewels is a brand launched by the Jefferson Parish Economic Development Commission (JEDCO) for oysters grown off-bottom at Grand Isle, the first regional umbrella brand for these growers.
   Source: https://jedco.org/jedco-reveals-grand-isle-jewels-off-bottom-oyster-brand/ (snippet, summary): "The Jefferson Parish Economic Development Commission (JEDCO) launched 'Grand Isle Jewel Off-Bottom Oysters,' an original brand campaign marketing regionally-sourced boutique oysters as a unique seafood product. This is the first-ever regional umbrella brand uniting Grand Isle products of innovative off-bottom oyster cultivation."
2. The brand was announced in April 2025.
   Source: https://www.axios.com/local/new-orleans/2025/04/25/grand-isle-jewels-seafood-brand (title and date in URL): "Grand Isle oyster farmers unite under single brand", 25 April 2025. Also gap-sourcing-live-2026-10-03.md section 2.
3. Eight oyster farmers off Grand Isle grow under the brand, in floating cages instead of harvesting from the bottom.
   Source: https://www.axios.com/local/new-orleans/2025/04/25/grand-isle-jewels-seafood-brand (snippet, summary): "Eight oyster farmers off Grand Isle have banded together under the new brand and are growing oysters in floating cages instead of harvesting from the bottom."
4. The name honours both the island's legacy of piracy and Jules Melancon, a multigenerational oysterman who brought off-bottom farming to Grand Isle in 2012 and died in 2023.
   Source: https://jedco.org/jedco-reveals-grand-isle-jewels-off-bottom-oyster-brand/ (snippet, summary): "Grand Isle Jewels is named both in reference to the legacy of piracy in Grand Isle and Jules Melancon, an oyster farmer who brought off-bottom farming to the area in 2012, starting the revitalization of a 150 year-old industry that failed in the 1980s. Jules Melancon was a multigenerational oysterman from the region who was the first to employ the off-bottom method in 2012 and who passed away in 2023."
5. Grand Isle's oyster industry was about 150 years old and had failed in the 1980s; off-bottom farming began its revival.
   Source: as fact 4.
6. Method: hardy caged oyster seed grown just below the surface in aquaparks chosen for temperature, water flow and salinity; the Grand Isle Aquapark mixes ocean and estuary water.
   Source: https://jedco.org/jedco-reveals-grand-isle-jewels-off-bottom-oyster-brand/ (snippet, summary): "growing hardy, caged oyster 'seeds' just below the water's surface in aquaparks selected for optimal temperature, water flow and salinity." And https://jedco.org/grand-isle-jewels-oyster-brand-lands-large-distribution-deal-as-jefferson-parish-and-port-expand-jewels-infrastructure/ (snippet, summary): "the cages filled with hundreds of thousands of hardy oyster larvae that float in Grand Isle Aquapark's unique mix of ocean and estuary water sources."
7. The producers describe the flavour as briny and bright.
   Source: as fact 6, second URL (snippet, summary): "This one-of-a-kind environment and optimal salinity give Grand Isle Jewels a uniquely briny and bright flavor."
8. Distribution: the brand sells directly to restaurants in the Southeast and Texas and has a distribution partnership with Inland Foods, which market-tested about 10,000 Grand Isle Jewels products before expanding.
   Source: https://www.seafoodsource.com/news/aquaculture/new-louisiana-regional-oyster-brand-wins-large-us-distribution-deal and the JEDCO distribution release (snippet, summary): "Grand Isle Jewels directly serves restaurants in the Southeastern U.S. and Texas and is expanding distribution through a partnership with Inland Foods, one of the largest specialty seafood suppliers in America. Inland Foods conducted market testing in the area, distributing about 10,000 Grand Isle Jewels products."
9. The off-bottom method was born in Grand Isle (in the brand's own framing) and is now used across the Gulf, the Eastern seaboard, Canada, Australia and parts of Europe.
   Source: fact 1 URL (snippet, summary): "off-bottom oyster cultivation, which was born in Grand Isle and has been adopted as a sustainable aquaculture practice across the U.S. Gulf Coast and Eastern seaboard, as well as in Canada, Australia and parts of Europe." Note: this is JEDCO's claim; off-bottom culture is old elsewhere in the world, so a server should say "the island's own revival of the method", not "invented here".

## 2. Louisiana oysters from the reefs and leases (Louisiana Oysters, Seafood Gumbo, Creole Caesar dressing)

1. Louisiana leads the nation in oyster production: from 2003 through 2022 it accounted for 32.3% of US oyster landings, and it produced 7,220,000 pounds of oyster meat in 2022.
   Source: https://www.wlf.louisiana.gov/page/commercial-oyster (snippet, summary): "Louisiana leads the nation in oyster production ... from 2003 through 2022, Louisiana accounted for 32.3% of the nation's oyster landings and produced 7,220,000 pounds of oyster meat in 2022 alone."
2. Oystermen have traditionally dredged from about 1.7 million acres of public reefs and about 400,403 acres of private leases from the state.
   Source: https://was.org/Meeting/Program/PaperDetail/165996 (Aquaculture 2025 abstract, snippet, summary): "oystermen who harvest by traditional dredge methods from the 1.7 million acres of public reefs or from their managed 400,403 acres of private leases from the state."
3. Private leases now dominate: in 2023 public reefs gave 7.3% of commercial landings and private leases 92.7%. Since about 2007, freshwater influx diminished the main public grounds east of the Mississippi, moving the split from about 50:50 to over 95% private.
   Source: as fact 2 and https://www.wlf.louisiana.gov/page/commercial-oyster (snippet, summary): "Since about 2007, the influx of freshwater ... has diminished the principal public oyster grounds on the east side of the Mississippi River resulting in a precipitous transition from an approximate 50:50 public-private production split to the private leases now accounting for over 95%"; "in 2023, public reefs accounted for 7.3% of commercial oyster landings while private leases accounted for 92.7%."
4. The Louisiana Wildlife and Fisheries Commission sets the public oyster season each year from LDWF biologists' stock assessment and public comment; public seed grounds typically open in September (for example 23 September 2024, and 8 September 2025 for seed oysters in the Vermilion and Cote Blanche bays), with market oysters for sale opening later (14 October 2024 there).
   Source: https://www.wlf.louisiana.gov/news/the-louisiana-wildlife-and-fisheries-commission-sets-the-20242025-public-oyster-season (snippet, summary): "opened at one-half hour before sunrise on Monday, September 23, 2024 ... although the harvest of market oysters for direct sale did not commence before Monday, October 14, 2024"; and https://www.doa.la.gov/media/qqqlyohg/2509emr013.docx (snippet): "shall open to the harvest of seed oysters for bedding purposes at one-half hour before sunrise on Monday, September 8, 2025." On the stock assessment: search summary of the WLF season pages.
5. Why Gulf oysters are milder: the Gulf is less salty than the Atlantic, partly from Mississippi River freshwater, so Gulf oysters are plump, meaty, mild and slightly sweet, with saltiness more subdued than East Coast oysters.
   Source: https://www.fultonfishmarket.com/blog/french-oysters-guide and related results (snippet, summary): "Gulf oysters are known for their plump and meaty texture, complemented by a mild, slightly sweet flavor, with the saltiness of Gulf oysters being more subdued than their East Coast counterparts ... The Gulf of Mexico has lower salinity levels compared to the Atlantic Ocean ... influenced by freshwater input from major rivers like the Mississippi River." Note: a trade blog, not a scientific body; the farmed Grand Isle Jewels (section 1) are deliberately grown in saltier water and are described as briny.

Correction to the existing component louisiana-oysters: it says "Most are wild from public and leased reefs". Leased bottoms are private, and private leases now give over 90% of landings (92.7% in 2023). Corrected sentence: "Most come from private leases, beds a family works on state water bottoms, with a small share from the state's public reefs; a growing number are farmed in cages off the bottom."

Expert knowledge: the species is the Eastern oyster, Crassostrea virginica (also in the existing component).

## 3. Louisiana and Gulf shrimp (New Orleans Shrimp & Grits, Popcorn Shrimp, Seafood Gumbo, children's shrimp)

1. Louisiana's shrimp fishery is mainly two species, brown shrimp (Farfantepenaeus aztecus) and white shrimp (Litopenaeus setiferus); it is the state's most valuable commercial fishery, with about 5,500 licensed commercial shrimpers.
   Source: https://www.wlf.louisiana.gov/assets/Resources/Publications/Shrimp/shrimp_brochure.pdf (snippet, summary): "Louisiana's shrimp fishery consists primarily of two species: the brown shrimp (Farfantepenaeus aztecus) and the white shrimp (Litopenaeus setiferus). The shrimp fishery is Louisiana's most valuable commercial fishery with approximately 5,500 shrimpers licensed."
2. Louisiana is first in US commercial shrimp landings; in 2012 nearly 102 million pounds were landed, worth just over $146 million at the dock.
   Source: as fact 1 (snippet, summary): "Louisiana is number one in commercial shrimp landings in the USA ... in 2012 ... nearly 102 million pounds of shrimp were landed in Louisiana with a dockside value of just over $146 million."
3. Seasons: the spring, brown shrimp season generally runs May to July; the fall, white shrimp season generally mid-August to mid-December, some waters into January. The Louisiana Wildlife and Fisheries Commission sets them by area on LDWF advice.
   Source: https://wlf.louisiana.gov/page/shrimp-season (snippet, summary): "The spring/brown shrimp season generally runs May to July, while the fall/white shrimp season is generally open mid-August to mid-December ... The Louisiana Wildlife and Fisheries Commission sets shrimp seasons for Louisiana's state waters by area according to LDWF's recommendations."
4. Almost 90 percent of the shrimp eaten in the US is imported, half of it farm-raised, which is why "Louisiana shrimp" on a menu matters.
   Source: https://www.theadvocate.com/baton_rouge/sports/outdoors/12-fascinating-shrimp-facts/article_141db1e9-1fcf-51b1-85e3-7dc0e699c9b6.html (snippet, summary): "Almost 90 percent of all the shrimp consumed in the United States is imported, and half of that is farm-raised."
5. History: by the 1880s the southeastern marshes held communities on raised shrimp-drying platforms; at the peak, 1900 to 1915, at least 95 such communities, drying shrimp for Asia, the Philippines and Hawaii. Most merchants who owned the processing companies were Chinese.
   Source: https://64parishes.org/shrimp-drying-in-louisiana (snippet, summary): "By the 1880s, the coastal marshes of Louisiana's southeastern parishes were dotted with thriving communities centered around elevated shrimp drying platforms. At its peak (1900-1915), the industry boasted at least 95 drying platform communities." and "Most of the Louisiana merchants who owned shrimp distribution and processing companies were Chinese."
6. Manila Village, at the mouth of Barataria Bay, was built on stilts over oyster reefs by Filipino fishermen led by Quintin de la Cruz in the late 1800s; it had its own post office.
   Source: https://filipinola.com/manila-village/ (snippet, summary): "a group of Filipino fishermen led by Quintin de la Cruz built a few shelters on stilts on top of some oyster reefs at the mouth of Barataria Bay ... They called it Manila Village." And 64parishes.org (snippet, summary): "one, at Manila Village, even had its own post office."
7. The fishermen were of many origins: Chinese, Filipino, Japanese, Italian, Croatian, Isleño, Creole, Cajun, African American and Native American.
   Source: https://64parishes.org/shrimp-drying-in-louisiana (snippet, summary), as quoted in the search result.
8. Louisiana law (Act 372 of 2019) requires a restaurant that serves imported crawfish or shrimp to print on the menu: "Some items served at this establishment may contain imported crawfish or shrimp. Ask for more information." Compliance has been uneven.
   Source: https://legis.la.gov/legis/Law.aspx?d=1147535 (RS 40:5.5.4, snippet, summary) and https://lailluminator.com/2024/05/16/lawmakers-pass-sweeping-changes-for-foreign-seafood-sold-at-louisiana-restaurants/ (snippet, summary): "although a state law adopted in 2019 requires restaurants to indicate on their menus or on a sign if they serve imported shrimp or crawfish, many restaurants have not complied."

Expert knowledge: brown shrimp taste more of iodine and the sea, white shrimp are sweeter and milder (in the existing component; not contradicted).

   Source: https://www.wlf.louisiana.gov/page/commercial-fishing (snippet, summary): "Louisiana's commercial blue crab fishery is the largest in both the Gulf and the United States and supplies about a quarter of the blue crab harvested in the United States."

   Source: https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/BuYFu/8/ ("Louisiana fishermen catch the most blue crabs in America", snippet, summary): "Louisiana accounted for 30.6% of all blue crabs landed in 2018, and in 2024, Louisiana's harvest was 40 million pounds."

   Source: https://www.myneworleans.com/crab-cakes-and-stuffed-crabs/ (snippet, summary): "Seafood stores around town offer three types of crab meat by the pound: jumbo lump, backfin lump, and claw. Many cooks prefer claw meat for stuffing crabs because claw meat is sweeter."

## 6. Redfish (Redfish Véronique)

1. Paul Prudhomme served blackened redfish at K-Paul's one March evening in 1980; from 1983 he took pop-ups to cities such as New York and San Francisco, with hours-long queues.
   Source: https://www.foodrepublic.com/1631780/origin-why-redfish-banned/ (snippet, summary): "One March evening in 1980, Paul Prudhomme served his pan-seared blackened redfish to a small group at K-Paul's ... In 1983, Prudhomme began hosting pop-ups in major cities like New York and San Francisco."
2. Imitation nationwide drove heavy overfishing of red drum (Sciaenops ocellatus): 12.7 million pounds were taken in one year in the Gulf.
   Source: as fact 1 and https://www.thedailymeal.com/eat/10-things-you-didnt-know-about-paul-prudhomme-0/ (snippet, summary): "the fish itself, Sciaenops ocellatus, also known as red drum, became endangered through aggressive overfishing. 12.7 million pounds were harvested in just one year in the Gulf alone."
3. Louisiana's Wildlife and Fisheries Commission banned commercial redfish fishing in 1988; one source dates a full commercial ban to 1987. Sources agree on the late 1980s.
   Source: https://www.foodrepublic.com/1631780/origin-why-redfish-banned/ (snippet, summary): "In 1988, Louisiana's Wildlife and Fisheries Commission enacted a temporary ban, forbidding commercial fishermen from catching redfish ... Another source indicates that a full-on ban on commercial fishing of redfish was fully in effect by 1987."
4. Redfish were an important commercial and restaurant fish until 1988; that year the recreational limit became 5 fish, only one over 27 inches, minimum 16 inches; there is no longer commercial fishing for redfish in Louisiana.
   Source: https://www.seagrantfish.lsu.edu/resources/factsheets/redfish.htm (LSU AgCenter and Louisiana Sea Grant fact sheet, snippet, summary): "Redfish (red drum) were an important commercial and popular restaurant fish until 1988 ... there is no longer commercial fishing for redfish in Louisiana." And https://www.theadvocate.com/baton_rouge/news/environment/louisiana-redfish-limits-changing-for-first-time-in-decades/article_f4164977-a0bb-50d7-bf2c-0fde4f403185.html (snippet, summary) on the 1988 limits and a recent change "for first time in decades".
5. Louisiana law exempts redfish raised in aquaculture from the wild-fish rules (Act 157), which is how farmed redfish can be sold.
   Source: https://gsmfc.org/publications/Miscellaneous/State%20Legislation%20Affecting%20Marine%20Fisheries%201990.PDF (snippet, summary): "Act 157 exempts fish raised in aquaculture operations."
6. Red drum aquaculture grew from the collapse of the 1980s, partly to restock the Gulf for Texas Parks and Wildlife; Texas leads the nation in red drum farms.
   Source: https://agrilifetoday.tamu.edu/?p=340555 and https://www.agmrc.org/commodities-products/aquaculture/aquaculture-fin-fish-species/red-drum-or-redfish (snippets, summary): "The origins of red drum aquaculture can be traced back to the 1980s when consumer demand and overfishing led to a population collapse ... to assist the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department in stock restoration programs ... Texas led the nation with 8 red drum farms."
7. Brennan's chef has published a blackened redfish dish of his own at Brennan's, "a reinvention of a New Orleans classic" (StarChefs).
   Source: https://www.starchefs.com/features/tag/ryan+hacker (snippet, summary): "Chef Ryan Hacker shares insight behind his blackened redfish dish at Brennan's, a reinvention of a New Orleans classic." Note: not used in the app; the current chef and the current redfish source are for lineup.

So restaurant redfish in Louisiana is farm-raised, or wild fish from another state where commercial harvest is allowed. Which, for ours, is a chef question. The existing redfish component is right in substance; its line "banned commercial redfish fishing later that decade" stands.

## 7. Louisiana crawfish (Crawfish Meat Pies)

1. More than 50 percent of Louisiana's crawfish aquaculture is done with rice: rice farmers flood their fields as crawfish ponds after the rice harvest in July and August, a second crop on the same clay soil.
   Source: https://blog.deanies.com/press-room/louisiana-crawfish-aquaculture-is-largest-by-far-in-us/ and https://communityimpact.com/the-woodlands/news/crawfish-season-3/ (snippets, summary): "More than 50 percent of Louisiana's crawfish aquaculture is practiced in conjunction with rice production ... fields flooded as crawfish ponds after rice is harvested in July and August."
2. Louisiana land in crawfish aquaculture grew from 120,654 acres in 1990 to 225,789 acres, against about 7,500 acres in the rest of the United States.
   Source: https://www.aquaculturenorthamerica.com/crawfish-popularity-hits-new-high/ (snippet, summary): "grown from 120,654 acres in 1990 to 225,789 acres. That's compared to just 7,500 total acres in production in the rest of the United States."
3. Wild crawfish are trapped in the Atchafalaya Basin, the largest river swamp in the US; wild catch is about 20% of Louisiana's crawfish industry, farmed about 80%.
   Source: https://ambrook.com/offrange/sustainability/when-the-crawfish-run-dry (snippet, summary): "Wild-caught crawfish are harvested in the Atchafalaya Basin ... the largest area of river swamp in the US ... Wild-caught crawfish constitute just 20% of Louisiana's crawfish industry."
4. The 2019 imported crawfish and shrimp menu law applies (section 3, fact 8). The Roost menu prints "Crawfish Meat Pies" with no origin.
   Source: section 3, fact 8; pages/brennansneworleans-menus-pasted-2026-10-03.txt line 337.

The existing crawfish component is right ("Louisiana produces most of the country's supply"). Whether ours is Louisiana tail meat is a chef question.

## 8. Turtle meat (Turtle Soup)

1. Louisiana banned commercial trapping of the alligator snapping turtle (the loggerhead) by a regulation that took effect on 20 November 2004, limiting personal take to one per day per boat or vehicle; licensed turtle farmers may still sell alligator snappers hatched from their breeding stock.
   Source: https://www.lsu.edu/departments/seagrantfish/news/2004/turtleharvest.htm (Louisiana Sea Grant, snippet, summary): "The alligator snapping turtle, or loggerhead, once a common site in Louisiana markets, can no longer legally be trapped for commercial sale ... That moratorium took effect Nov. 20 2004 ... licensed turtle farmers will still be allowed to sell alligator snappers that hatch from their breeding stock."
2. The common snapping turtle is a different matter: Louisiana law allows holders of a reptile-and-amphibian collection licence to take and sell unlimited common snapping turtles with no closed season (as reported in 2016 to 2017).
   Source: https://www.biologicaldiversity.org/news/press_releases/2017/freshwater-turtles-01-05-2017.php (snippet, summary): "Louisiana law currently allows holders of a reptile-and-amphibian-collection license to take and sell unlimited numbers of common snapping turtles with no closed season." Note: an advocacy group's statement of the law at that date; current rules not re-checked.
3. Most of Louisiana's turtle trade involves farm-hatched animals.
   Source: https://bizneworleans.com/la-environmentalists-push-for-ban-on-commercial-turtle-harvests/ (snippet, summary): "most of the Louisiana turtle trade involves animals hatched on farms."
4. Commander's Palace uses farm-raised alligator snapping turtle because farms give a steady supply (The Advocate, Curious Louisiana).
   Source: https://www.theadvocate.com/curious_louisiana/turtle-meat-turtle-soup-commanders-curious-louisiana/article_8a34b104-ae37-5aa7-8c31-0edcba160a01.html (snippet, summary): "Commander's Palace uses alligator snapping turtles that are farm-raised because farms provide a steady supply." The same summary says "Brennan's chefs use snappers for their turtle soup", and mentions a recipe of "40 percent turtle meat and 60 percent veal"; the summary does not make clear whose recipe that is, and Brennan's menu today prints 100% turtle meat, so neither line is used for Brennan's.
5. Fresh or frozen turtle meat can be bought from specialty markets or by mail order from seafood dealers in and around New Orleans.
   Source: https://thelocalpalate.com/recipes/brennans-turtle-soup (Brennan's Turtle Soup recipe, snippet, summary): "Fresh or frozen turtle meat can be bought from specialty markets or by mail order from seafood dealers in and around New Orleans."
6. Turtle soup has been eaten in Louisiana since the 1700s and is served at Arnaud's, Brennan's, Mandina's and Felix's among others.
   Source: The Advocate Curious Louisiana article as fact 4 (snippet, summary): "Turtle soup is available at other New Orleans restaurants, including Arnaud's, Brennan's, Mandina's and Felix's. It's a dish that has been popular in Louisiana ever since the 1700s."

What "100% turtle meat" means: the menu promises the meat in the soup is all turtle, with no veal or beef stretching it (see turtle-soup-story). It does not say which turtle or whether farmed.

Correction to the existing component turtle-meat: it says restaurant turtle in Louisiana "is farm-raised freshwater turtle, usually snapping turtle, bought from licensed suppliers; it is not a wild or endangered species."
