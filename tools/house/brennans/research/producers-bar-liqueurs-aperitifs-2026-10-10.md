## 1. Green Chartreuse: the Carthusian monks (Five Minutes More)

1. The 1605 manuscript: a marshal of Henry IV's artillery, the Maréchal d'Estrées, gave the Carthusians a recipe manuscript; it listed 130 botanicals and was sent to La Grande Chartreuse; it was hard to decipher.
   Source: https://www.diffordsguide.com/producers/14/distillerie-chartreuse-aiguenoire/history and https://cocktailsandbars.com/everything-you-need-to-know-about-chartreuse/ (summary): "Several sources say a marshal of Henry IV's artillery, Maréchal d'Estrées, gave the Carthusians a recipe-manuscript that year." "In 1605, Francois Hannibal d'Estreés, Marshal of King Henry IV's Artillery came across a secret recipe with a list of 130 botanicals. The manuscript was then sent to La Grande Chartreuse."
2. 1737 or 1764: "no one could even decode the recipe until 1737, when the monastery's apothecary finally produced a liqueur following the ancient text"; others date the formalisation to 1764 and credit Father Jérôme Maubec.
   Source: same (summary). And search 21, https://distilled.substack.com/p/tarragona-a-tale-of-two-distilleries (summary): "In 1764, this formula using 130 plants was perfected and formalized by Father Jérôme Maubec."
3. Green Chartreuse was introduced in 1764; France Today marked Chartreuse Verte's 250 years.
   Source: search 1 (summary): "Sources agree that Green Chartreuse was introduced in 1764." https://francetoday.com/food-drink/wine_and_spirits/chartreuse_verte_celebrates_250_years/ (title: "Chartreuse Verte Celebrates 250 Years"). Yellow Chartreuse is dated to 1838 by one source.
4. The 1903 expulsion and Tarragona: a small group went to Tarragona to resume production under a new brand name; the French state sold the brand, used until 1929 by the Compagnie Fermière de la Grande Chartreuse; in 1929 the monks were again permitted to use the name; the Tarragona distillery ran until 1989.
   Source: https://distilled.substack.com/p/tarragona-a-tale-of-two-distilleries (summary): "A small contingent made their way to Tarragona to immediately resume production of Chartreuse under a new brand name." "Sold by the State, their brand was used until 1929 by the Compagnie Fermière de la Grande Chartreuse." "It wasn't until 1929 that the monks were once again permitted to use the name 'Chartreuse'." "The Carthusians returned from exile in 1932 to resuscitate their distillery in Fourvoirie." The distillery in Tarragona "continued operating until 1989". One source dates the nationalisation to 1904.
5. Aiguenoire: the historic Voiron distillery, created in 1936, had to leave under regulations adopted in 2014; the new Aiguenoire facility opened on 30 August 2018; the 2020 edition was the first bottling produced there. The Caves de la Chartreuse in Voiron opened as a visitors centre.
   Source: https://www.diffordsguide.com/producers/14/distillerie-chartreuse-aiguenoire/history (summary): "The new facility was opened on 30 August 2018, and the 2020 edition was the first bottling to be produced in Aiguenoire." And https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chartreuse_(liqueur) (summary): "production continued here until 2018 when a new distillery was opened in nearby Aiguenoire, leaving the Caves de la Chartreuse in Voiron to open as a visitors centre."
6. The recipe and the plants: only the Carthusians know the recipe, by one source only two monks; the dried plants are sorted, crushed, weighed and mixed according to the recipe of 1605 at the monastery, then sent to the distillery. Chartreuse Diffusion is the company that sells it. The Voiron cellar is called the longest liqueur cellar in the world, 164 metres.
   Source: https://chartreuse-tourisme.com/en/preparing-your-stay/taste/local-products/the-chartreuse-a-liqueur and https://www.whisky.fr/en/menu/blog/articles-on-chartreuse/chartreuse-origin (summary): "The Carthusians of the great Carthusian monastery are the only ones who know the famous recipe." "Only the Carthusian Fathers know the names of the 130 plants used to make the Chartreuse liqueur." "Dried plants are sorted, crushed, weighed and mixed according to the recipe of 1605 at the monastery, then sent to the distillery." "the longest liqueur cellar in the world is located (164 metres long!)."
7. The 2023 cap: the monks decided in 2021 not to raise output; their letter of 16 January 2023 said they limit production to protect their monastic life and devote their time to solitude and prayer, citing environmental concerns too: "we look to do less but better and for longer." Production was set at 1.6 million bottles a year, the highest since the late 1800s; the United States was "limited to 90 percent of its 2021 volume"; all markets, France included, were put under allocation. Frederick Wildman is the only US importer.
   Source: https://vinepair.com/articles/chartreuse-shortage-explained/ and https://drinksint.com/news/fullstory.php/aid/11046/.html and https://www.guildsomm.com/public_content/features/articles/b/virginie-boone/posts/what-you-should-know-about-chartreuse (summary, quoting the letter): "They are limiting production to focus on their primary goal: protect their monastic life and devote their time to solitude and prayer." "Production was set at 1.6 million bottles annually." "limited to 90 percent of its 2021 volume."
8. From the earlier pass (components-drinks-2026-10-05.md, search 45; bar-master-review-2026-10-04.md): made by Carthusian monks in the French Alps to a secret recipe of 130 herbs and plants; Green Chartreuse is bottled at 55 percent.

Brennan's link: printed in Five Minutes More, with Cathead Mandarin Vodka, Licor 43 and lemon, under Billboard Songs from 1946.

Expert knowledge: the green colour comes from the plants, not a dye (the existing component says "the colour is natural"); Yellow Chartreuse is the gentler, sweeter sister.

## 2. Licor 43: Zamora Company, Cartagena (Five Minutes More)

1. Founding: the recipe is credited to three siblings of the Zamora family and a brother-in-law; they created it in a small distillery in Cartagena; commercial production began there in 1946, bottled by hand.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Licor_43 and https://www.diffordsguide.com/en-au/producer/1093/licor-43/story (summary): "The recipe is credited to Diego Zamora with his siblings Ángel and Josefina and Josefina's husband, Emilio Restoy. They created it in a small distillery in Cartagena. Commercial production began there in 1946, when the liqueur was bottled by hand."
2. The legend: the Romans are said to have found a golden liqueur called liqvor mirabilis in Carthago Nova in 209 BC; the brand says the recipe is based on it.
   Source: same (summary): "According to the story, the Romans found a golden liqueur called liqvor mirabilis in Carthago Nova in 209 BC." "the Licor 43 website says the recipe is based on Liqvor Mirabilis, which it describes as an elixir from Carthago Nova in the 3rd century."
3. The 43 ingredients and secrecy: the name is the count of ingredients; citrus and fruit juices, vanilla, aromatic herbs and spices; only five are public: vanilla, orange peel, Eureka lemon, coriander and tea. Only the family and the factory's director know the recipe (another source: only three people in the founding family).
   Source: https://www.milenio.com/estilo/gastronomia/perfecto-con-cafe and https://www.alcoholprofessor.com/blog-posts/licor-43 (summary): "only five are publicly known: vanilla, orange peel, Eureka lemon, coriander and tea." "the brand has revealed 5 components: orange, lemon, coriander, green tea, and vanilla." "Only the Zamora family and the factory's director know the secret recipe."
4. Standing: the most popular liqueur in Spain, in more than 60 countries.
   Source: search 3 (summary): "Licor 43 is the most popular liqueur in Spain. It is present in more than 60 countries."
5. Ownership and the factory: listed with Zamora Company as its parent; the factory houses a distillery, a visitors centre and an interactive museum.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Licor_43 (summary): "Licor 43 is listed with Zamora Company as its parent." "The factory now houses a distillery, a visitors centre and an interactive museum."
6. Strength: 31 percent.
   Source: https://farehamwinecellar.co.uk/Products/spirit/liqueur/french-liqueur/fruit-liqueur/licor-43-orange-vanilla-liqueur/ (title): "Licor 43, Cuarenta Y Tres (Orange / Vanilla Liqueur) 31%".
7. Conflict: one account says a "Cuarenta y Tres" was made from about 1895 in a small Cartagena distillery bought in 1924; Wikipedia's infobox gives 1924 without a citation. Not used.
8. From the earlier pass (components-drinks-2026-10-05.md, search 46; bar-master-review-2026-10-04.md): a golden vanilla liqueur from Cartagena, Spain, made since 1946 from 43 ingredients from a secret family recipe; citrus and vanilla lead.

Brennan's link: printed in Five Minutes More. Brennan's opened in 1946, the year of the songs the list is named for; the date matches Licor 43's by coincidence only.

Expert knowledge: in Spain it is often taken with coffee, and with espresso over ice it makes the Carajillo the Ledger already lists.

## 3. Lillet Blanc: Lillet, Podensac (Riviera, To Each His Own)

1. Founding: the brothers Paul and Raymond Lillet, wine and spirits merchants, set up La Maison Lillet in 1872 in Podensac, south of Bordeaux; early output was mainly fruit liqueurs.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillet and https://vinepair.com/articles/ntk-lillet-liqueur-guide/ (summary): "The brothers Paul and Raymond Lillet, already wine and spirits merchants, set up La Maison Lillet in 1872 in Podensac, south of Bordeaux." "Early output was mainly fruit liquors."
2. Kina Lillet, the first wine-based product, launched in 1887; the name joins the family name with quinquina, the bark that gives quinine.
   Source: same (summary): "Kina Lillet launched in 1887. The name combines the family name with quinquina, the bark that quinine comes from."
3. 1986: Lillet Blanc (1986 to the present) is a sweeter variant with reduced quinine, and it replaced Kina Lillet; one source says the cinchona was dropped in 1985.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillet (summary): "Lillet Blanc (1986 to the present) is a sweeter variant of the white-wine-based version with reduced quinine content, and it replaced Kina Lillet." (The source's range mark is written out here.)
4. The blend: wines 85 percent, fruit liqueurs and cinchona 15 percent; the liqueurs macerate the peels of sweet orange (from Morocco or Spain) and bitter orange (from Haiti or Peru) for several weeks in alcohol; matured in oak casks in the Podensac cellars. Grapes for the Blanc: Sémillon and Sauvignon Blanc (Sémillon for Blanc and Rosé, Merlot for Rouge by another source); 17 percent.
   Source: https://bottleofitaly.com/en/pages/produttore-lillet and https://www.ocado.com/products/lillet-blanc-white-wine-based-aperitif/236158011 and https://closdesspiritueux.com/28109-pineau-and-co-lillet-blanc---17.html (summary): "LILLET is matured according to tradition in oak casks in our Podensac wine cellars, south of Bordeaux." "Lillet is made from wines (85%) and fruit liqueurs and cinchona (15%)." "Fruit liqueurs are obtained by macerating fruit peels such as sweet orange (from Morocco or Spain) and bitter orange (from Haiti or Peru) for several weeks in alcohol." "Grape varieties: Sémillon, Sauvignon Blanc Alcohol content: 17 % vol."
5. Ownership: in 1985 the Lillet family sold the business to a Bordeaux owner; in 2008 it was sold to a subsidiary of the Pernod Ricard group.
   Source: https://bottleofitaly.com/en/pages/produttore-lillet (summary): "In 1985, the Lillet family sold the business to Bruno Borie. Then, in 2008, it was sold to a subsidiary of the Pernod Ricard group."
6. The Vesper: Lillet is the aperitif of James Bond's Vesper Martini.
   Source: https://gentologie.com/en/fine-dining/wines-and-spirits/spirits/lillet/ (title): "Lillet: the essential ingredient in James Bond's Vesper Martini".
7. From the earlier pass (components-drinks-2026-10-05.md, search 36; bar-master-review-2026-10-04.md): founded in 1872 at Podensac by Paul and Raymond Lillet; about 85 percent Bordeaux wine and 15 percent citrus liqueurs; Lillet Blanc replaced Kina Lillet in 1986, lighter and less bitter.

Brennan's link: printed in the Riviera (with sparkling wine and lemon; the menu spells it Lilet) and in To Each His Own (with Daron Fine Calvados and quince liqueur).

## 4. Cap Corse Mattei Blanc Quinquina: L.N. Mattei, Bastia (Spoonbill)

1. Founding: Louis-Napoléon Mattei opened his first shop in Bastia in 1872, selling cigars, coffee, wine and liqueurs; in 1874 he experimented with distilling cédrat, a fruit grown in Cap Corse, which gave the Cédratine liqueur; in 1875 came a medicinal wine made with quinquina.
   Source: https://www.visit-corsica.com/Mon-sejour/Patrimoine-culturel/Tout-le-patrimoine-culturel/MOULIN-MATTEI (summary): "Mattei opened his first shop in Bastia in 1872, initially selling cigars, coffee, wine and liqueurs. In 1874 he experimented with distilling cédrat, a fruit grown in Cap Corse, which gave rise to the brand's emblematic Cédratine liqueur. In 1875 came the Amaro, a medicinal wine made with quinquina."
2. The bark: the importer says Mattei discovered cinchona bark on a voyage to the Caribbean and brought it to Cap Corse to blend with local Muscat and Vermentinu wine; TasteAtlas says he meant quinquina, known against malaria, to combat malaria.
   Source: https://alpenz.com/producer-mattei.html and https://tasteatlas.com/cap-corse (summary): "Mattei discovered the beneficial properties of cinchona tree bark during a voyage to the Caribbean, and he brought it to Cap Corse to blend with local wine made from Muscat and Vermentinu grapes." "mixing the local muscatel wine with quinquina or cinchona, a medicinal plant known for its anti-malarial properties, to combat malaria."
3. The Blanc: a mistelle, grape must stopped from fermenting with neutral alcohol; Muscat and Vermentinu musts with neutral alcohol, no added sugar (Difford's); nonfermented Vermentino and Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains (Imbibe); flavoured with plants, fruits and spices; cinchona adds spice and depth in the mid and back palate; cédrat, the Corsican citron, adds citrus aroma and a silky texture; 17 percent.
   Source: https://www.diffordsguide.com/beer-wine-spirits/wine-fortified/vermouth-and-aromatized-wines/BWS006053/ln-mattei-cap-corse-blanc and https://imbibemagazine.com/?p=63312 and https://alpenz.com/product-cap_corse_blanc.html (summary): "It's a mistelle, meaning grape must stopped from fermenting with neutral alcohol." "Muscat and Vermentinu grape musts blended with neutral alcohol, with no added sugar." "nonfermented Vermentino and Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains." "adds spice and depth in the mid and back palate." "adds unique citrus aroma and a silky texture." "It is 17% ABV."
4. Ownership and making: the company is still family owned, and all macerations, ageing and bottling are done in house (the importer's materials); made today in the workshops of the Borgo industrial zone.
   Source: https://alpenz.com/producer-mattei.html and https://www.gillespudlowski.com/?p=61852 (summary): "the company is still today family owned, and all macerations, aging and bottling are done in house." "the aperitif is now made in the workshops of the Borgo industrial zone."
5. The Moulin Mattei: a century later a descendant or successor of Louis-Napoléon Mattei restored the windmill on Cap Corse and turned it into an advertisement for the quinquina; the Conservatoire du Littoral bought it in 2004 and restored it to its 1930s advertising appearance.
   Source: https://www.bouger-voyager.com/moulin-mattei/ and https://www.visit-corsica.com/Mon-sejour/Patrimoine-culturel/Tout-le-patrimoine-culturel/MOULIN-MATTEI (summary): "a century later, Louis-Napoléon Mattei's descendant (or successor) restored the windmill and turned it into an advertising object for his quinquina aperitif. The mill was later bought by the Conservatoire du Littoral in 2004 and restored to its 1930s advertising appearance."
6. The range: a Rouge and a Blanc are sold, and Haus Alpenz is the US importer.
   Source: https://dandm.com/products/cap-corse-mattei-rouge-vin-aperitif-quinquina and https://dandm.com/products/cap-corse-mattei-blanc-vin-aperitif-quinquina (titles); search 7 (summary): "Haus Alpenz is the US importer."
7. From the earlier pass (components-drinks-2026-10-05.md, search 35): Cap Corse is the northern peninsula of Corsica; a quinquina with cinchona bark on a base of Vermentino and Muscat with citron (cédrat); still family owned.

Brennan's link: printed in the Spoonbill, with Hendrick's Gin and chive oil; the Roost page calls it "Cap Corse Mattei Blanc Quinquina vermouth".

Expert knowledge: a vermouth is an aromatised wine defined by wormwood, a quinquina one defined by cinchona bark; they are cousins, and a quinquina can stand in for dry vermouth in a martini-style drink.

## 5. Amarula Cream Liqueur: Amarula, Phalaborwa and Stellenbosch (Floating in South Africa)

1. The spirit came first: a clear marula spirit was first bottled in 1983; cream was introduced and the cream liqueur reached shelves in 1989.
   Source: https://amarula.com/?p=538 ("Our History", summary): "The Spirit of Africa was born when we first bottled a clear spirit distilled from the exotic marula fruit." "We introduced cream to our recipe, and the Amarula cream liqueur hits shelves domestically." And search 8 (summary): "The clear marula spirit dates to 1983, and the cream liqueur reached shelves in 1989."
2. Distell and Heineken Beverages: first marketed in September 1989 by Southern Liqueur Company, a wholly owned subsidiary of Distell Group; in 2023 Distell was acquired by Heineken Beverages, and with it the Amarula brand.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarula (summary): "the liqueur was first marketed in September 1989 by Southern Liqueur Company, a wholly owned subsidiary of Distell Group, and that in 2023, Distell was acquired by Heineken Beverages, and with that the Amarula brand."
3. The harvest: from roughly mid-January to March near Phalaborwa; fallen fruit is collected by the women of the local rural communities and delivered to the Amarula production plant in Phalaborwa, where rotating blades separate the flesh from the hard seeds.

## 9. Pedro Ximénez sherry: the Jerez zone and Montilla-Moriles (Banane au Chocolat)

1. Asoleo: the grapes are left on the vine until very ripe, harvested by hand and laid on straw mats to dry in the sun; this shrinks the berries, raises the sugar and reduces the water; the must is so dense that fermentation often stalls. The drying time varies by source (about two days, or at least 21 days at one producer).
   Source: https://www.winewithseth.com/winewiki/pedro-ximenez-sherry/ and https://www.alliancewine.com/bodegas-ximenez-spinola/pedro-ximenez-solera-1918-0-2754 (summary): "Grapes are left on the vine until very ripe, then harvested by hand and laid on straw mats to dry in the sun. This technique, called asoleo, shrinks the berries, increases sugar content, and reduces water."
2. Where it grows: most PX is grown in Montilla-Moriles; Jerez producers typically source PX must or wine from there and age and blend it in their own bodegas, where it must rest a minimum of two years before being labelled sherry; only wines from the Jerez DO may be called sherry.
   Source: https://puninwine.com/pages/glossary/pedro-ximenez-sherry and https://www.sherrynotes.com/?p=5570 (summary): "Producers in Jerez typically source PX must or wine from Montilla-Moriles, then age and blend it in their own bodegas, where it must rest for a minimum of two years before being labeled as sherry." "Only versions made within the Jerez DO are legally entitled to the Sherry name."
3. The Jerez rules: the Consejo Regulador allows PX grapes only for sweet wines; PX grown in Jerez is used to sweeten oloroso or amontillado into cream sherries.
   Source: https://www.winewithseth.com/winewiki/pedro-ximenez/ (summary): "The rules of the Consejo Regulador in Jerez only allow PX grapes to be used for sweet wines." "Pedro Ximénez grown in Jerez is typically used to sweeten oxidized wines like oloroso or amontillado, creating cream sherries."
4. Sugar: residual sugar figures from 212 to 500 grams a litre and from 300 to 450 grams a litre both appear. Not used as a number in the app.
5. The grape's parent: DNA analysis in 2007 identified Pedro Ximénez as an offspring of the Arabic table grape Gibi. The story that the grape was brought from Germany by a soldier found no support.
   Source: https://mitchellrabinowitz.substack.com/p/the-history-of-the-pedro-ximenez (summary): "DNA analysis in 2007 identified Pedro Ximénez as an offspring of the Arabic table grape Gibi." Search 15 (summary): "I found nothing on the Peter Siemens name legend."
6. Sherry and its D.O.: Sherry is the fortified wine of the Jerez zone; D.O. Jerez-Xeres-Sherry was recognised in 1933 (producers-food-european-protected-2026-10-06.md, section 9).
7. From the earlier pass (components-drinks-2026-10-05.md, search 54): the sweetest and darkest sherry, from Pedro Ximénez grapes dried in the sun to near raisins; raisin, fig, molasses and dark chocolate; grown mostly in Montilla-Moriles and Jerez.
