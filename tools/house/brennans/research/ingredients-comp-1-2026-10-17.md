## 1. Shallots

1. The shallot is a small, mild member of the onion family. Until 2010 it was classed as its own species, Allium ascalonicum; in 2010 it was folded into Allium cepa, the common onion, and is now usually written Allium cepa var. aggregatum, the aggregatum group, because the bulbs grow in clusters.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shallot (summary): the taxon was synonymised with Allium cepa in 2010; the older name Allium ascalonicum still appears.
2. The English word comes, through French échalote and the older eschalot, from a Latin phrase meaning the onion of Ascalon, the ancient port now Ashkelon on the coast of Israel; whether the plant began there or was only traded through it is debated.
   Source: https://www.dictionary.com/browse/shallot (etymology chain, eschalot from French échalote); https://www.balashon.com/2006/07/scallion_05.html (whether the onions began in Ashkelon or passed through it is debated).
3. (expert) Shallots are sweeter and finer than onion, with less sulphur bite, so they go raw into mignonette and vinaigrette where an onion would be harsh, and they melt into reductions for béarnaise, beurre blanc and marchand de vin. The grey shallot of France and the long banana shallot are the two most seen in restaurant kitchens; the menu names no variety.

## 2. Parsley

1. Parsley, Petroselinum crispum, is native to the Mediterranean. The Greeks used it for funeral wreaths and the crowns of winning athletes and did not eat it; the Romans made it a kitchen herb and a garnish.
   Source: https://blog.herbsociety.org/herb-of-the-month/parsley-herb-of-the-month-and-herb-of-the-year-2/ (native to southern Europe and the Mediterranean; the Greek wreaths and tombs; the Romans cultivated it as a garnish).
2. Two forms: flat-leaf, called Italian, with the sharper flavour cooks prefer, and curly, milder and mostly a garnish. Theophrastus described both before 300 BC.
   Source: https://uaex.uada.edu/yard-garden/resource-library/plant-week/Petroselinum-crispum-Parsley-12-16-2016-Ark.aspx (curly milder and ornamental, flat-leaf sharper and used in cooking); https://www.selfsufficientish.com/parsley.html (Theophrastus on the two types).
3. (expert) Parsley is in three French finishes on the current menus: fines herbes (with chives, tarragon and chervil), persillade (with garlic) and gremolata (with garlic and lemon zest), and in the classic Brabant potato finish of butter, garlic and parsley, which the kitchen dossier asks the chef to confirm.

## 3. Black pepper

1. Black pepper is the dried fruit of the vine Piper nigrum, native to the Malabar Coast of Kerala in south-western India; green, black and white peppercorns all come from the same vine at different stages of ripeness and preparation.
   Source: https://greenhouse.biology.indiana.edu/features/crops/piper-nigrum.html (native to the Malabar Coast; the forms of the berry); https://uwsp.edu/?p=59719 (origin and history).
2. Tellicherry is a grade of large, late-picked Malabar peppercorn named for the port the British shipped pepper from in the 1600s, now called Thalassery.
   Source: https://www.peppermate.com/blogs/recipes/where-do-peppercorns-come-from-from-vine-to-grinder (Tellicherry named for the port now Thalassery).
3. In 2020 Vietnam was the largest producer and exporter of pepper. The International Pepper Community, the trade body, put the 2020 world crop at 561,500 tonnes and Vietnam's share at 43 per cent; its yearbook tables list Vietnam at 220,000 tonnes of black pepper (240,000 of all pepper), Brazil at 91,200 (95,000), India at 59,800 (61,000), Indonesia at 38,737 (86,083) and Sri Lanka at 23,730 (23,970), so Brazil, Indonesia, India and Sri Lanka stand behind Vietnam, and their order among themselves depends on the table. Vietnam shipped 285,292 tonnes in 2020, 59 per cent of world exports.
   Source: https://www.ipcnet.org/other-news/?id=973 (IPC: 561,500 tonnes in 2020, Vietnam 43 per cent of production and 59 per cent of exports at 285,292 tonnes); https://www.ipcnet.org/n/psy2022/html/pdf/1.02.pdf and https://www.ipcnet.org/n/psy2022/html/pdf/1.04.pdf (Pepper Statistical Yearbook 2022, the country tables). An earlier figure of 270,192 tonnes and 36 per cent, taken from a Wikipedia production table said to follow FAOSTAT, could not be confirmed on 17 October 2026 (the page could not be reached through the proxy) and disagrees with the IPC count, so the components carry the IPC figure only.
4. (expert) Pepper is cracked rather than ground for mignonette; the French word mignonnette means that coarse crack. Its heat, piperine, is slow and warming and sits in the back of the mouth, unlike the sharp front heat of chilli. In the New Orleans barbecue sauce it is the main seasoning after butter.

## 4. Vinegar

1. The word is Old French vinaigre, vin aigre, sour wine, in English since the early 1300s; the Romans called it vinum acetum, wine turned sour.
   Source: https://www.etymonline.com/word/vinegar.
2. The United States has no standard of identity for vinegar, but the FDA's labelling policy holds table vinegar to at least 4 grams of acetic acid per 100 mL, that is 4 per cent acidity, and most commercial vinegars run 5 per cent.
   Source: https://www.fda.gov/media/71937/download (CPG Sec. 525.825, vinegar definitions; not less than 4 per cent; the common varieties contain about 4 grams per 100 mL at 20 C).
3. (expert) The kitchen's vinegars are chosen by their base: white wine and Champagne vinegars for mignonette and beurre blanc, red wine for marchand de vin, cider and rice vinegars for pickles, sherry vinegar for warm dressings. Which vinegar goes into a given Brennan's sauce or pickle is not printed; it is a question for the chef.

## 5. Butter

1. Under United States law butter is made only from milk or cream, with or without salt and colouring, and holds not less than 80 per cent milk fat by weight; the definition is the Act of 4 March 1923, 21 U.S.C. 321a.
   Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2018-title21/html/USCODE-2018-title21-chap9-subchapII-sec321a.htm.
2. (expert) European-style butters are churned to 82 per cent or more and are preferred for brown butter and pastry because they carry less water. Brown butter, beurre noisette, is butter cooked until its milk solids toast; clarified butter has the solids and water removed and is the base of hollandaise. The menu names no butter maker; Vital Farms, whose egg the breakfast hanger prints, sells butter as well as eggs (producers-food-beef-eggs-duck-2026-10-06.md), but nothing says the house uses it.

## 6. Cream for whipping

1. Heavy cream in the United States is cream with not less than 36 per cent milkfat (21 CFR 131.150); light whipping cream is 30 to under 36 per cent (21 CFR 131.157). Cream below about 30 per cent fat will not hold air.
   Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-131 (sections 131.150 and 131.157).
2. (expert) Whipping works by beating air into fat: the fat globules partly join and trap the bubbles, so the cream must be cold, the bowl cold, and the beating stopped at soft or firm peaks; a minute more and it turns grainy and then to butter. Mascarpone and crème fraîche are whipped the same way, as the menu prints for the mascarpone mousse and the whipped crème fraîche.

## 7. Chocolate

1. The cacao tree, Theobroma cacao, is native to the upper Amazon of north-western South America, where its genetic diversity is greatest; the oldest evidence of its use, about 5,300 years ago, comes from a site in south-eastern Ecuador reported in 2018.
   Source: https://digitalcommons.kennesaw.edu/facpubs/4424 (Zarrillo and colleagues, Nature Ecology and Evolution, 2018: starch grains, theobromine residues and ancient DNA from about 5,300 years ago in south-east Ecuador; the upper Amazon as the centre of origin).
2. In the United States, sweet chocolate labelled semisweet or bittersweet must contain not less than 35 per cent chocolate liquor by weight (21 CFR 163.123); milk chocolate not less than 10 per cent chocolate liquor and 12 per cent total milk solids (21 CFR 163.130).
   Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2020-title21-vol2/pdf/CFR-2020-title21-vol2-part163.pdf (sections 163.123 and 163.130).
3. (expert) Bittersweet in a pastry kitchen usually means 60 to 72 per cent cacao: dark, with enough sugar to bake with. Coffee and chicory deepen chocolate because they share its roasted, bitter notes. No maker is named for either chocolate on the menus (producers-food-european-protected-2026-10-06.md, section 11, on the Snickers); the maker is a question for the chef.

## 8. The spiced citrus glaze

1. (expert) A glaze on a cake is sugar dissolved in a little liquid and set thin over the top: icing sugar beaten with citrus juice, or a syrup of sugar, juice and zest boiled briefly and brushed on while the cake is warm so it soaks in and dries to a sheen. The menu prints "Spiced Citrus Glaze" and no more.
2. The spices the glaze borrows are those of café brûlot as the guide's own line describes the drink: orange and lemon peel, cinnamon and clove, flamed with brandy (guide.txt, the Café Brûlot Coffee Cake card; dossier-the-kitchen.md on Antoine's Café Brûlot Diabolique). Which citrus and which spices go into the glaze, and whether it carries any brandy, are questions for the chef.

## 9. Local raspberries

1. Raspberries are difficult in Louisiana's heat; the LSU AgCenter says the only variety that does reasonably well in the state is Dorman Red, grown like a blackberry and planted between November and February.
   Source: https://www.lsuagcenter.com/portals/blogs/southerngarden/doraspberriesgrowwellinsoutheastlouisiana.
2. Dormanred (also written Dorman Red) was developed by Mississippi State University as an ever-bearing, trailing raspberry adapted to the southern states; it was announced in a 1972 bulletin of the Mississippi Agricultural and Forestry Experiment Station, "Dormanred raspberry: a new variety for Mississippi", by J. P. Overcash. The LSU AgCenter advice in item 1 is a 2015 post by its consumer horticulture specialist.
   Source: https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C766/home-garden-raspberries-and-blackberries/ (University of Georgia extension: "Developed by Mississippi State, Dormanred is an ever-bearing trail-type raspberry well adapted for growing in southern states"); https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/who/Overcash%2c%20J%2e%20P%2e%20%28Jean%20Parks%29%2c%201917%2d (the 1972 MAFES bulletin listed); the LSU post's date from its own page, confirmed 17 October 2026.
3. (expert) A raspberry is a cluster of tiny drupelets around a hollow core, which is why it is the most fragile of the soft fruits: picked ripe, it keeps a day or two and bruises at a touch. It is sharper than a strawberry and more perfumed than a blackberry. The menu prints "Local Raspberries"; which farm, and whether local means Louisiana or the wider Gulf South, is a question for the chef.

## 10. Heirloom tomatoes

1. An heirloom is an open-pollinated variety whose saved seed grows true to type, unlike a hybrid, whose seed is made by controlled crossing each year and does not come true. How old a variety must be is not agreed: some say 50 years, some 100, some before the Second World War.
   Source: https://ucanr.edu/site/mg-sonoma/heirloom-tomatoes (University of California Master Gardener programme: the scientific definition is an open-pollinated variety; hybrids are not open-pollinated); https://gardenerspath.com/plants/vegetables/what-is-heirloom-tomato/ (no agreed age; 50 or 100 years; pre-war).
2. Cherokee Purple, a dusky, dark-shouldered tomato with a rich, smoky flavour, and Brandywine, the big pink beefsteak, are the heirlooms most often named.
   Source: https://ucanr.edu/site/mg-sonoma/heirloom-tomatoes (Cherokee Purple described); https://www.tastingtable.com/695506/heirloom-tomato/ (Brandywine seed grows true).
3. (expert) Heirlooms are grown for flavour and colour, not for shipping: thin skins, odd shapes, red, pink, yellow, green and near-black. They bruise and crack, so they are sold close to home, which is why a Louisiana menu can print them only in season. The Creole tomato, the Louisiana summer tomato, is a separate story (producers-food-louisiana-produce-pantry-2026-10-06.md; the creole-tomato producer profile). Which farm grows the heirlooms is a question for the chef.

## 11. Worcestershire sauce

1. Worcestershire sauce was first sold by Lea & Perrins, the chemists John Wheeley Lea and William Henry Perrins of Broad Street, Worcester, England; the partnership dates from 1823 and the sauce from 1837 (some accounts say the first bottles reached the public in 1838, after the first batch had matured in casks).
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcestershire_sauce; https://www.soyinfocenter.com/books/152 (History of Worcestershire Sauce 1837 to 2012; the 1823 partnership; the casks).
2. The company's own story, that a governor of Bengal, Lord Sandys, brought a recipe home from India and had the two chemists make it up, is tradition, not record; the recipe's origin is unclear.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcestershire_sauce (the origin of the recipe is unclear; the Sandys story is company tradition).
3. It has been made in a factory on Midland Road in Worcester since 1897. Lea & Perrins passed to HP Foods in 1930 and, with HP, to Heinz in 2005.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcestershire_sauce (Midland Road, 16 October 1897; HP Foods 1930; Heinz 2005).
4. The British bottle lists malt vinegar, spirit vinegar, molasses, sugar, salt, anchovies, tamarind extract, onions, garlic, spices and flavourings; the American bottle is built on distilled white vinegar and molasses and lists anchovies too, so the sauce is a fish product for a guest who asks.
   Source: https://www.tesco.com/shop/en-GB/products/250179532 (the British ingredient list); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcestershire_sauce (the United States label).
5. New Orleans barbecue shrimp, the style the Louisiana Oysters and the BBQ Lobster follow, was born at Pascal's Manale in 1954 as a pan sauce of butter, black pepper, Worcestershire, garlic and lemon (dossier-the-kitchen.md; the New Orleans barbecue sauce component). A classic Caesar dressing also carries Worcestershire (the smoked oyster dressing component). Whether the house sauce and the Caesar dressing use it, and which brand, are questions for the chef.
6. Checked 17 October 2026: the corporate dates (the Midland Road factory from 16 October 1897; HP Foods in 1930; HP Foods sold by Danone to Heinz in June 2005) are confirmed by Grace's Guide and by the HP Foods and Lea & Perrins entries on Wikipedia; the 1823 partnership and the 1837 first sale by the Soy Info Center chronology.
   Source: https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Lea_and_Perrins; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_Foods; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lea_%26_Perrins; https://www.soyinfocenter.com/books/152.
7. Turtle Soup is left out of the Worcestershire component's items: Creole turtle soup is classically seasoned with Worcestershire (the Creole turtle soup story) and the kitchen dossier's summary of Brennan's published recipe lists it, but the menu does not print it and the house recipe page's excerpt reachable on 17 October 2026 showed only the opening steps, so it stays a question for the chef.

## 12. Potatoes

1. The potato was domesticated about 8,000 years ago in the Andes near Lake Titicaca, on the border of Peru and Bolivia; the Spanish met it in the Inca lands in the 1530s and carried it to Europe in the 1570s.
   Source: https://www.fao.org/4/i0500e/i0500e02a.pdf (FAO, International Year of the Potato, 2008: the story begins about 8,000 years ago near Lake Titicaca); https://spice.alibaba.com/spice-basics/potato-origins-history (the Spanish and the 1570s; a commercial page, used only for the date the FAO page omits).
2. (expert) Cooks sort potatoes by starch: waxy ones (fingerlings, red and new potatoes) hold their shape when boiled and roasted, floury ones (russets) fry crisp and mash light, and all-purpose ones (Yukon Gold) sit between. A rösti, a pavé and a fried potato shell want starch for crispness; a fingerling beside fish wants to stay whole. Brabant potatoes are the New Orleans fried cube, named by tradition for the Brabant of Belgium and the Netherlands (the Brabant potatoes component). The menu names no potato variety or grower.

## 13. Vinaigrette

1. (expert) A vinaigrette is an acid and a fat shaken or whisked together, with a seasoning: the French kitchen's rule of thumb is about three parts oil to one part vinegar, with salt, pepper and often mustard or shallot to help the two hold together for a few minutes. It is a temporary emulsion, so it is whisked again before each use. A warm vinaigrette replaces some or all of the oil with warm fat, brown butter or bacon fat, and is spooned over at once. The word is the diminutive of vinaigre.
   Source: https://www.etymonline.com/word/vinaigrette (the word).
2. On the Louisiana Crab Claws the guide's line is butter cooked until nutty and toasty, balanced with acidity so it is rich but bright (guide.txt, the crab claws card); the kitchen dossier reads it as beurre noisette stopped with acid and shallot (dossier-the-kitchen.md, expert). Which acid, lemon or vinegar, and whether shallot is in it, are questions for the chef.

## 14. Pickling

1. There are two kinds of pickle. A quick pickle, the restaurant's usual, steeps vegetables or fruit in a brine of vinegar, water, salt, sugar and spice, often poured hot, and is ready in hours; no fermentation takes place. A fermented pickle sits in a salt brine only, and bacteria turn its sugars to lactic acid over three or four weeks.
   Source: https://madeincookware.com/blogs/quick-pickle-vs-lacto-fermentation; https://thecookscook.com/guides/fermented-versus-quick-pickles/.
2. (expert) A hot brine softens and seasons quickly; a cold brine keeps crunch. Fruit pickles, like the hibiscus-pickled grapes on the Redfish Véronique, lean sweet. The Louisiana Oysters print pickled collard greens, the Fried Pickles a medley of housemade pickles, the Roost Burger pickles; the Grand Isle oysters print preserved Fresno chilli, and the mignonette component notes that preserved may mean salted or pickled, so the Grand Isle Jewel Oysters are not listed among the pickling component's items: the menu prints preserved, not pickled, and the question goes to the chef. Which brine, and how long, are questions for the chef.

## 15. Curing

1. (expert) To cure is to draw water out of food with salt, often with sugar, so that it keeps and its flavour concentrates; the salt pulls moisture by osmosis and makes the food inhospitable to spoilage. Bacon and Canadian bacon are cured pork; tasso is cured and smoked pork shoulder; a confit leg is salted before it is cooked in fat; preserved lemons are cured in salt; and the egg yolk on the Steak Tartare Cannoli is cured in salt and sugar until it can be grated (the egg yolk bottarga, coffee-cured Canadian bacon, tasso, confit and preserved lemon components). Commercial bacon cures usually include nitrite, which gives the pink colour; whether the house bacon is cured in house or bought is a question for the chef.

## 16. Spinach

1. Spinach, Spinacia oleracea, began in Persia, and its name comes through Spanish espinaca and Arabic from the Persian aspanakh; the Moors brought it to Spain, by one account in the 11th century, and it reached the rest of Europe from there.
   Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/spinach-a-vegetable-in-need-of-some-love; https://gfft.georgiaorganics.org/s/Spinach-History.pdf.
2. The story that Catherine de' Medici brought spinach to France when she married the future Henry II in 1533, and that dishes à la Florentine are named for her Florence, is a legend: spinach was in French cooking by the 14th century. The word Florentine for a spinach dish is the legend's lasting trace.
   Source: https://gfft.georgiaorganics.org/s/Spinach-History.pdf (the legend and why it is exaggerated).
3. (expert) Spinach is nine parts water: a full pan wilts to a handful in a minute, so a kitchen buys it by the case. It is blanched, squeezed and chopped for creamed spinach, wilted in fat for brown butter or garlic spinach. The current menus print it four ways: brown butter spinach in the Turtle Soup, Parmesan creamed spinach on the Eggs Sardou, garlic spinach with the Petite Filet Mignon and the Creamed Spinach side.
