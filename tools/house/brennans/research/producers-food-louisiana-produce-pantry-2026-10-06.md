## 1. Creole tomatoes (Creole Tomato Tostada)

1. Growers in St. Bernard and Plaquemines parishes traditionally sold their tomatoes as Creole, crediting vine ripening and rich alluvial soil.
   Source: https://www.lsuagcenter.com/profiles/lbenedict/articles/page1469026887857 ("Will the Real Creole Tomato Please Stand Up?", LSU AgCenter) (snippet): "Traditionally, farmers in St. Bernard and Plaquemines parishes marketed their tomatoes as Creole. According to these producers, allowing the tomatoes to vine-ripen and grow in a rich alluvial soil created a unique flavor distinguishing their tomatoes from other Louisiana- and U.S.-produced tomatoes."
2. In the early 1900s, before variety names, tomatoes from St. Charles, St. John and St. James parishes were also simply called Creole.
   Source: same LSU AgCenter article (snippet): "In the early 1900s, there were no known variety names for tomatoes and tomatoes from St. Charles, St. John and St. James parishes were simply referred to as 'Creole.'"
3. LSU AgCenter researcher Teme Hernandez released a variety named Creole in 1969; it was not commonly grown in St. Bernard and Plaquemines, and its low yield meant the seed stock was not kept.
   Source: same LSU AgCenter article and https://www.lsuagcenter.com/profiles/lblack/articles/page1734968050087 ("'Creole' (L134)") (snippet): "Former LSU AgCenter researcher Teme Hernandez released a tomato variety named Creole in 1969. However, the variety Creole was not commonly grown in St. Bernard and Plaquemines parishes. Likely because of its poor production, less than six pounds per plant, the AgCenter did not maintain a seed stock of Hernandez's Creole."
4. The AgCenter grew a demonstration plot of Creole tomatoes at the Botanic Gardens at Burden in spring 2015.
   Source: same LSU AgCenter article (snippet): "On March 24, 2015, the Creole tomato seedlings were planted at the AgCenter Botanic Gardens at Burden."
5. The French Market's Creole Tomato Festival began in 1985 and is held every June to mark the season.
   Source: https://64parishes.org/?p=43813 and https://thebayouinsider.substack.com/p/creole-tomato-festival-returns-to (summary): "The Creole Tomato Festival in New Orleans began in 1985 to signal the start of a new season and is held every summer in June." The festival "functions as a means to honor and highlight local tomatoes and the farmers who grow them".
6. The French Market has stood since 1791.
   Source: https://thebayouinsider.substack.com/p/creole-tomato-festival-returns-to (snippet): "Since 1791, the historic French Market has remained an enduring symbol of pride and progress for the people of New Orleans."
7. Season: one search summary gives "Traditional Creole tomato season usually runs from May to January" (source among the results above, not pinned to one page). Not used for servers.

Brennan's link: none public beyond the printed dish name. Expert knowledge: Creole tomatoes are picked ripe and bruise easily, so they are a local, short-travel tomato; the dish follows the season.

## 2. Louisiana cane syrup and Steen's (Roasted Rohan Duck Breast)

The menu prints "Cane Syrup-glazed Peaches" and names no maker. Steen's is described here as the best-known maker of the style, not as Brennan's supplier.

1. Steen's syrup has been made since 1910 in Abbeville, Louisiana, by C. S. Steen's Syrup Mill, Inc.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steen%27s_cane_syrup (snippet): "Steen's syrup has been made since 1910 in Abbeville, Louisiana, by C. S. Steen's Syrup Mill, Inc."
2. In 1910 an early freeze hit Charley Steen's cane crop; he built a small mill and turned the frozen cane into a few barrels of syrup.
   Source: https://www.steenssyrup.com/about (snippet): "The Steen's story began in 1910 when Charley Steen's cane crop was hit with an early freeze. Facing the possibility of a huge loss, he fashioned a small mill and salvaged his frozen crop into a few barrels of syrup."
3. By 1930 Charley and Elizabeth Steen and their children had built a larger mill in Abbeville on the banks of Bayou Vermilion.
   Source: https://www.steenssyrup.com/about (snippet): "By 1930, Charley and Elizabeth Steen and their children built a larger mill in the heart of Abbeville, Louisiana on the banks of Bayou Vermilion."
4. Cane syrup is made by simply concentrating cane juice through long cooking in open kettles; Steen's adds nothing.
   Source: https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/en/ark-of-taste-slow-food/traditional-cane-syrup/ (snippet): "a traditional American sweetener made by the simple concentration of cane juice through long cooking in open kettles." And https://gardenandgun.com/end-of-the-line-raising-cane (summary): the company "still sells the famous cane syrup, cooking it up in open kettles and adding nothing to the mix, which is why it's labeled Steen's 100% Pure Cane Syrup."
5. Traditional cane syrup is on Slow Food's Ark of Taste.
   Source: https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/en/ark-of-taste-slow-food/traditional-cane-syrup/ (page title: "Traditional Cane Syrup", Ark of Taste, Slow Food Foundation).
6. The mill is family run: Charley Steen III, the founder's grandson, ran it (Garden & Gun); more recently the fourth generation, brother and sister Jimmie and Carole Steen, lead it.
   Source: https://gardenandgun.com/end-of-the-line-raising-cane (snippet): "Charley Steen III, who runs the mill, is the founder's grandson." And search summary of the Steen's results: "the C. S. Steen Syrup Mill is now led by Jimmie and Carole Steen, a brother and sister team and the fourth generation of the Steen family." (Generational detail is kept out of server prose beyond "family run".)
7. The yellow can became a symbol in Cajun and Creole kitchens from the 1950s.
   Source: search summary (Steen's results, chefshop.com / myneworleans.com "Made in Acadiana"): "In the 1950s, Steen's Pure Cane Syrup gained widespread popularity in South Louisiana. Its distinctive yellow can became a symbol of local sweetness".

## 3. Chicory (Café Brûlot Coffee Cake, New Orleans Style Coffee, the breakfast tasting coffee)

1. The French used roasted chicory when Napoleon's Continental Blockade of 1808 cut France off from coffee; the British blockade from 1806 also curtailed imports.
   Source: https://themanual.com/culture/chicory-coffee-history (summary): "one of the earliest records of chicory being used as a coffee substitute comes from the French Napoleonic Wars, when Napoleon Bonaparte executed the 'Continental Blockade' in 1808, which effectively severed France from its coffee imports." Also https://eatshistory.com/what-is-chicory-coffee-the-wartime-substitute-that-quietly-outlived-every-war-it-was-created-for/ (summary): "the British naval blockade of continental Europe from 1806 onward drastically curtailed coffee imports to France".
2. Roasted chicory had been used as a coffee substitute in Europe for about two hundred years before it reached Louisiana.
   Source: https://eatshistory.com/what-is-chicory-coffee-the-wartime-substitute-that-quietly-outlived-every-war-it-was-created-for/ (summary): "Roasted chicory had already been used as a coffee substitute in Europe for 200 years before it ever reached Louisiana."
3. The city of Lille in France developed chicory cultivation for coffee in the eighteenth century; the root is dug, cut, dried, roasted and ground.
   Source: https://www.chowhound.com/1708994/chicory-new-orleans-coffee-alternative and https://www.tastingtable.com/1136655/what-makes-chicory-coffee-unique/ (summary): "In France, the city of Lille developed chicory cultivation for this purpose in the 18th century. The root is dug up, cut, dried, roasted, and ground."
4. By 1840 New Orleans was the second largest coffee importer in the United States.
   Source: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/chicory-coffee-mix-new-orleans-made-own-comes-180949950/ (summary): "By 1840, the port of New Orleans was the second largest importer of coffee in the United States".
5. The Civil War blockade: during the Civil War, beginning in 1861, Union naval blockades cut off the port of New Orleans and Louisianans stretched coffee with chicory.
   Source: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/chicory-coffee-mix-new-orleans-made-own-comes-180949950/ (summary): "During the American Civil War, Louisianans added chicory root to their coffee when Union naval blockades cut off the port of New Orleans." And https://aol.com/chicory-coffee-does-orleans-130000272.html (summary): "During the Civil War beginning in 1861, Union forces enacted a naval blockade on the port of New Orleans, which halted all shipments of foreign goods".
6. Smithsonian's framing: "a cost-saving workaround but stuck around for tradition's sake."
   Source: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/chicory-coffee-mix-new-orleans-made-own-comes-180949950/ (subtitle snippet).
7. Chicory is the root of a blue-flowered perennial; Café du Monde makes its coffee with it.
   Source: https://royalcoffee.com/chicory/ / dirtycoast.com (summary): "Café du Monde makes their coffee with chicory, the root of a blue-flowered perennial plant."
8. Most commercial chicory is grown in France and Belgium; New Orleans' PJ's Coffee uses Leroux French chicory. Chicory is also grown in South Africa and, increasingly, in Nebraska.
   Source: https://shop.pjscoffee.com/blogs/news/the-history-of-using-chicory (summary): "most chicory is grown commercially in France and Belgium. PJ's Coffee of New Orleans offers their customers an authentic taste with Leroux French Chicory." And https://themanual.com/culture/chicory-coffee-history (summary): "grown in France, South Africa, and increasingly stateside in places like Nebraska."

Brennan's link: the menu prints chicory in the New Orleans Style Coffee ("Blend Of Chicory & Dark Roast"), the breakfast tasting ("Brennan's Private Blend Congregation Coffee & Chicory") and the Café Brûlot Coffee Cake. Where Brennan's chicory is grown is not public. Expert knowledge: chicory adds a roasty, slightly bitter depth and body to coffee.

## 4. Louisiana pecans and the pralinières (Pecan Gulf Fish, Baked Apple, Café Brûlot Coffee Cake, New Orleans Bread Pudding)

Pecans
1. Louisiana is one of few states where pecans are native; native nuts tend to be small, flavourful and high in oil, unlike improved varieties bred for size.
   Source: https://lsuagcenter.com/articles/page1738860540620 ("From tree to table: A guide to growing pecans in Louisiana", LSU AgCenter) and https://www.lsuagcenter.com/articles/page1748959422645 ("Uncovering Louisiana's Hidden Pecan Gems") (summary): "Louisiana is one of few states where pecans are native. Native pecans are distinct from improved pecans that have been bred to produce larger nuts, with nuts from native pecan trees tending to be small, flavorful and have high oil content."
2. Louisiana has between 19,000 and 21,000 acres of native pecans and usually ranks fifth to seventh nationally.
   Source: https://www.lsuagcenter.com/profiles/rbogren/articles/page1478784973366 (snippet): "Louisiana has between 19,000 and 21,000 acres of native pecans, and Louisiana's national ranking for pecan production varies from year to year but generally falls between fifth and seventh place."
3. The LSU AgCenter runs the only university-operated research station in the United States devoted solely to pecans.
   Source: LSU AgCenter results above (summary): "The LSU AgCenter has a research station, the only university-operated pecan research facility in the United States, devoted solely to pecans."
4. Antoine, an enslaved gardener at Oak Alley Plantation in St. James Parish, grafted pecan trees in the winter of 1846 (sources give 1846 or 1847) and eventually grafted 126 trees; his nut was later named Centennial and won a prize at the 1876 Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Alley_Plantation (snippet): "Antoine, a slave and gardener of Oak Alley Plantation, was a master of grafting techniques and succeeded in the winter of 1846 in producing a variety of pecan that could be cracked with bare hands". https://live-zocalopublicsquare.ws.asu.edu/?p=89973 ("The Slave Gardener Who Turned the Pecan Into a Cash Crop") (summary): "Eventually, Antoine grafted 126 trees at Oak Alley." Also "The pecan was later named the Centennial Variety when entered in competition at the 1876 Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia, where it won a prize." And https://www.pecansouthmagazine.com/magazine/article/the-beginning-of-modern-pecan-culture-centennial/ ("The beginning of modern pecan culture").

Pralines
5. The praline is named for César, duc de Choiseul, comte du Plessis-Praslin, whose chef made sugared almonds; the Ursuline nuns are credited with bringing the candy to New Orleans early in the eighteenth century.
   Source: https://vianolavie.wp.tulane.edu/?p=112531 ("Pralines: more than just a candy", Tulane) (summary): "Early in the eighteenth century, French colonizers (namely the Ursuline nuns) first introduced the praline candy to the Crescent City. The delicacy's namesake was a French duke called César, duc de Choiseul comte du Plessis-Praslin ... his chef created the sugared almond praline".
6. The pecan praline came from the city's Black women, who used the local nut and added milk or cream.
   Source: same Tulane page (summary): "The version of New Orleans pralines we enjoy today, of the pecan variety, came from the city's Black women, who took Praslin's chef's medicinal sweet and adapted it to Louisiana, making use of the abundant, regional nut, and adding milk or cream to thicken the sugary mixture."
7. In the mid to late nineteenth century, Black women called pralinières sold pralines in the French Quarter; free and enslaved women both made them, and some bought their freedom with the proceeds.
   Source: same Tulane page and https://countryroadsmagazine.com/cuisine/Louisiana-foodways/the-praline-ladies/ (summary): "In the mid-to-late 19th century, it was common for Black women, known as 'pralinieres,' to sell pralines in the French Quarter." "Both free and enslaved women made their signature pralines ... and sold them on the streets of the French Quarter. In this way, many were able to buy their own freedom and that of their families."

Expert knowledge: pecan is said pih-KAHN in Louisiana, as components/dishes.json (key pecans) records it; praline is said PRAH-leen locally, as the pralines component records it (PRAH-leens).

## 5. Louisiana rice and popcorn rice (Seafood Gumbo)

1. Della is an aromatic long-grain rice with a popcorn aroma, released by the LSU AgCenter for consumers who want a fluffy long-grain texture and a strong popcorn-like aroma; it is tall, lodges easily and yields poorly.
   Source: https://www.lsuagcenter.com/portals/our_offices/research_stations/rice/features/publications/lsu-agcenter-releases-two-new-rice-varieties-for-2012 (snippet): "The aromatic parent Della was released by the LSU AgCenter 38 years ago for certain domestic consumers who prefer a fluffy long-grain cooking texture yet a strong popcorn-like aroma." Also: "a tall, lodging-susceptible variety with a very low yield potential".
2. The LSU Rice Research Station was set up in 1909 on 60 acres on the west side of Crowley.
   Source: https://www.lsuagcenter.com/portals/communications/news/news_archive/2009/june/headline_news/rice-research-station-starts-second--century and https://www.farmprogress.com/management/rice-station-celebrates-100-years-of-research (summary): "The Louisiana State University Agriculture Center set up a Rice Research Station in 1909 ... established on a 60-acre plot of land on the west side of Crowley."
3. Konriko's Wild Pecan Rice is an aromatic long-grain brown rice of the Della variety, also known as popcorn rice, with a roasted-nut aroma. (Named as an example of the style, not as Brennan's rice.)
   Source: https://countryroadsmagazine.com/cuisine/Louisiana-foodways/konriko-rice/ (summary): "Konriko's Wild Pecan Rice is a highly specialized, aromatic, long grain, brown rice of the Della variety (also known as popcorn rice), that gives off an aroma like roasted nuts."
4. The Conrad Rice Mill in New Iberia, founded in 1912 by Philip Amalus Conrad, is the oldest independently owned rice mill in the United States still operating; the Konriko name (Con-Ri-Co) dates from the 1950s; Mike Davis bought the mill in February 1975.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conrad_Rice_Mill (snippet): "Established in 1912, it is the oldest independently owned rice mill in the United States still in operation." https://www.conradrice.com/about/history (summary): "Philip Amalus Conrad ... founded the Conrad Rice Mill and Planting Company in 1912." Search summary: "In the 1950s, he launched the brand name Konriko Rice, an acronym for Con(rad) Ri(ce) Co(mpany)." https://countryroadsmagazine.com/cuisine/Louisiana-foodways/konriko-rice/ (summary): "Mike Davis ... became rice miller in February 1975 when he bought Conrad Rice Mill in New Iberia."
5. Aroma chemistry (from components-dishes search 24): the popcorn aroma is 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline, shared with basmati and jasmine.

Brennan's link: none public. Which popcorn rice the kitchen uses is a lineup question.

## 6. South Louisiana rice (South Louisiana Rice Dressing)

1. Rice growing in southwest Louisiana began in earnest in the early 1880s; by 1890 Louisiana passed South Carolina as the leading rice state.
   Source: https://www.lsuagcenter.com/portals/our_offices/research_stations/rice/features/publications/historical-perspective-of-southwest-louisiana-rice-production (summary): "Rice production in southwest Louisiana began in earnest during the early 1880s." "The 1890 crop was a record breaker at 80 million pounds, making Louisiana the No. 1 rice producing state, surpassing the former leader, South Carolina."
2. Crowley, the seat of Acadia Parish, became the centre of the rice industry of southwestern Louisiana.
   Source: https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/geology/publications/bul/845/sec3.htm (USGS Bulletin 845) (summary): "Crowley, the parish seat of Acadia Parish, became the center of the great rice industry of southwestern Louisiana".
3. Between 1800 and 1920 southwestern Louisiana became the first mechanised commercial rice region in the world.
   Source: https://trace.tennessee.edu/entities/publication/88e159b2-1518-409a-9b32-83e2ef53d854 (dissertation abstract) (summary): "Between 1800 and 1920, southwestern Louisiana was transformed from an economically poor region of subsistence agriculture and open range cattle ranching into the first mechanized commercial rice region in the world."
4. Southwest Louisiana has nearly 500,000 acres of rice land.
   Source: LSU AgCenter historical perspective (summary): "Southwest Louisiana boasts nearly 500,000 acres of prime rice producing land."

## 7. Andouille and LaPlace (Seafood Gumbo)

1. LaPlace, in St. John the Baptist Parish, is the Andouille Capital of the World, declared by Governor Edwin Edwards.
   Source: https://lobservateur.com/?p=86191 ("Our Stories, Our History: Andouille", L'Observateur) and WWNO results (summary): "a distinction that was formalized when then-Governor Edwin Edwards officially declared LaPlace as 'The Andouille Capital of the World.'"
2. Jacob's World Famous Andouille opened in 1928, when Nelson and Camille Jacob began selling smoked andouille from their Milesville home.
   Source: https://lobservateur.com/?p=101648 ("Jacob's World Famous Andouille sets sights on next 90 years") (summary): "The history of Jacob's World Famous Andouille dates back to 1928, when Nelson and Camille Jacob, known locally as Bragger and Mam-Bragger, began selling homemade hats, clothing and smoked Andouille in the front yard of their Milesville home."
3. Three LaPlace shops are famous for andouille: Jacob's, Bailey's and Wayne Jacob's Smokehouse (unaffiliated with Jacob's); Wayne Jacob's opened in 1950.
   Source: https://www.wwno.org/food/2012-10-11/where-yeat-where-an-andouille-tradition-endures and https://bizneworleans.com/spice-of-life/ (summary): "Jacob's is one of three butcher shops famous for Andouille in LaPlace. Bailey's sits in the busiest strip mall in the town next door to Jacob's, and Wayne Jacob's, unaffiliated to Lion's store, rests two miles away near the river." "Jacob's is known as the smokiest, Wayne Jacob's claims the spice and girth, and Bailey's finds a happy medium." https://bizneworleans.com/two-family-legacies-combine-at-wayne-jacobs-smokehouse/ (summary): "Wayne Jacob's Smokehouse, opened in 1950, has been passed down through the generations."
4. LaPlace andouille is "thick as a wrist, brown as tobacco, and steeped in pecan wood smoke."
   Source: https://bizneworleans.com/spice-of-life/ (snippet).
5. The Germans of the German Coast brought a strong sausage-making tradition, alongside French, Acadian, Native American, Spanish and African influences; Aaron Lion is the fourth-generation owner of Jacob's.
   Source: https://bizneworleans.com/spice-of-life/ and L'Observateur (summary): "The Germans of the German Coast brought the strong tradition of making sausage, along with influences from French, Acadians, Native Americans, Spanish and Africans". "According to Aaron Lion, fourth generation owner of Jacob's ..."
6. The first Andouille Festival was held in LaPlace on 28 and 29 October 1972.
   Source: https://lobservateur.com/?p=62513 ("Andouille a flavorful staple") (summary): "In 1972, the citizens of LaPlace turned their appreciation for andouille into a celebration with the first Andouille Festival held Oct. 28-29 of that year."
7. After Hurricane Ida (2021) WWNO reported the LaPlace tradition "smoking again".
   Source: https://www.wwno.org/podcast/where-yeat/2021-09-28/where-yeat-after-hurricane-ida-a-louisiana-food-tradition-is-smoking-again (title).

Expert knowledge: LaPlace lies in the River Parishes, the parishes along the Mississippi upriver from New Orleans that include the old German Coast.

## 8. Tasso and the boucherie (New Orleans Shrimp & Grits)

1. Tasso is a smoked, spiced, cured meat of south Louisiana; "ham" is a misnomer because it is cut from the shoulder, not the hind leg.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasso_ham (snippet): "Tasso ham is a smoked, spiced, and cured meat, a specialty of south Louisiana cuisine. Despite its name, 'ham' is a misnomer since tasso is not made from the hind leg of a hog, but rather the hog's shoulder."
2. The shoulder is sliced across the grain into pieces about 3 inches thick, cured briefly in salt and sugar (three or four hours), rinsed, rubbed with cayenne and garlic, and hot-smoked until cooked through.
   Source: same Wikipedia page (snippet): "sliced across the grain into pieces about 3 in (7.5 cm) thick. These are dredged in a salt cure, which usually includes sodium nitrite and sugar. The meat is left to cure briefly, only three or four hours, then rinsed, rubbed with a spice mixture containing cayenne pepper and garlic, and hot-smoked until cooked through."
3. It is more often a flavour base for stews and braised vegetables than eaten alone; jambalaya is the classic.
   Source: same Wikipedia page (snippet): "Tasso is most often found in recipes of Creole or Cajun origin, such as jambalaya. Though tasso may be eaten on its own, it is more often used as part of a flavor base for stews or braised vegetables."
4. At a boucherie, tasso is made from the last pieces of lean meat; it is a seasoning meat.
   Source: https://www.lafayettetravel.com/blog/stories/post/boucherie/ (summary): "Tasso is the last pieces of lean meat at a Boucherie. Tasso can be any piece of lean meat ... Tasso is a seasoning meat."
5. The boucherie, the communal butchering of a hog in Acadiana, gives the Cajun larder boudin, cracklin', andouille and tasso.
   Source: https://www.lafayettetravel.com/blog/stories/post/boucherie/ (summary): "The boucherie is the communal butchering of a hog that fed families, sustained communities, and built a cuisine still central to Cajun and Creole identity." "The boucherie gives us boudin, cracklin', andouille, tasso and other staples of the Cajun larder."
6. Boucanière is French Acadian for smokehouse.
   Source: https://www.foxnews.com/food-drink/boudin-balls-and-boucaniere.amp (summary): "Boucanière is French Acadian for 'smokehouse'."

Brennan's link: none public; the menu prints tasso and no maker. Lineup question: who makes the house's tasso, or is it smoked in-house.
