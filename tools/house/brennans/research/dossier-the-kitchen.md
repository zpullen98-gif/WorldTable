# The kitchen: Brennan's as a master chef reads it

A dossier for a server at 417 Royal Street, built to be read on a phone between shifts. Every dish has a one-breath line, a longer story, what is confirmed, and what to ask the chef or the sommelier.

**How to read the labels.** (pdf) is what the server guide says, with the page. (search) is what a search snippet said, with the site named; these were gathered earlier in this workflow, since live search had run out by the time this dossier was written. (expert) is professional knowledge and is never a statement about Brennan's specifically. Where the guide and a snippet disagree, both are given and neither is chosen silently. Claims the workflow refuted appear here only in corrected form. Claims it could not verify are marked [unconfirmed]; contested ones are marked [disputed].

**Prices.** Every price is copied from the guide, whose menus were read on 26 September 2026. No price here comes from anywhere else, except one magnum noted as a search price. Menus change with the season; the guide itself warns that the dinner card is a late-summer menu and a fall change is due (pdf p49).

**Allergens.** Only the guide's own service notes are reported, labelled (pdf). Nothing is added.

## 0. What a cook carries in one minute

- (expert) The house style is French technique on Creole and Gulf ingredients. The guide says it in its own words: the sauce usually matters more than the protein, for the guest and for the wine (pdf p2). Learn the sauces first.
- (pdf p49) Two desserts are flamed at the table, Bananas Foster and Cherries Jubilee. Chef Kris Padalino, executive chef since June 2025, has said she wants more tableside cooking back. Ask at training who performs it, server or captain, and learn the house procedure.
- (search, Biz New Orleans; CooksInfo; FSR; Axios New Orleans) The kitchen promotes from within: Roussel from dishwasher to chef, Randolph from dishwasher to chef, Hacker from the 2014 line to chef, Padalino from pastry to chef. Say that to a new cook on day one.
- (pdf p49, p50) It is the 80th year. Brennan's opened in 1946 and has been at 417 Royal Street since 1956. The 80 dollar tasting menus run through 2026. Bananas Foster, invented here in 1951, turns 75.
- (pdf p49) Hours: breakfast and lunch Monday to Friday 9 to 2, Saturday and Sunday 8 to 2; dinner nightly 6 to 10. Breakfast and lunch carries 7 appetisers (three of them sweet), 8 entrées and 7 sides. Dinner carries 7 appetisers, 6 entrées and 5 sides at $12. One dessert menu serves both. Turtle soup, gumbo and the Creole Caesar appear at both meals.

## 1. The house, briefed for the kitchen

### The first life, 1946 to 2013

**One breath.** "Eighty years this year. Owen Brennan opened on Bourbon Street in 1946 and the house moved here to Royal Street in 1956."

- (search, Wikipedia "Brennan's" and "Owen Brennan (restaurateur)") Owen Edward Brennan took the Old Absinthe House on Bourbon Street in 1943 and in July 1946 opened Owen Brennan's French & Creole Restaurant across the street, known to everyone as the Vieux Carré. Sources split on whether he opened a new restaurant or bought and renamed one; the year and the street agree everywhere.
- (search, brennansneworleans.com/traditions) The house legend: Count Arnaud teased Owen that an Irishman's cooking ended at boiled potatoes, and Owen set out to prove him wrong. Tell it as the house legend, never as a dated event.
- (search, UPI archive 20 May 1982; brennansneworleans.com/traditions) Owen saw that most New Orleans restaurants served only lunch and dinner, took the then-popular novel Dinner at Antoine's as his cue, and asked, why not Breakfast at Brennan's? (expert) The author is Frances Parkinson Keyes; the 1948 novel was a bestseller, though the snippets say only "popular".
- (search, UPI 1982; nomenu.com) The rooster is Chanticleer, left by Owen Sr to depict early-morning vitality. The main dining room is the Chanteclair Room, the French spelling; no snippet says in words that the room is named for the bird, so say the room shares the rooster's name.
- (search, CooksInfo; Southern Foodways Alliance) Paul Blangé, born 1900 in Holland, came to New Orleans in 1922 as a ship's cook for United Fruit, worked at Arnaud's, Kolb's, Galatoire's and the Jung Hotel, and was kept on as the first chef when the Brennans took the Vieux Carré in 1946. He stayed until his death in July 1977 and was buried with a knife, a fork and a Brennan's menu on his chest. His portrait still hangs in the restaurant. He is credited with Bananas Foster, Eggs Hussarde and Chicken Pontalba.
- (search, Find a Grave; Wikipedia "Ella Brennan") Owen died of a coronary in his sleep on Friday 4 November 1955, aged 45, with the move to Royal Street under way. The banks pulled out; Ella Brennan found her own backing and the Royal Street house opened in 1956. No snippet fixes the month. (expert) Owen never served a meal at 417 Royal.
- (search, Southern Foodways Alliance; Gambit; Wikipedia "Ella Brennan") The family split in November 1973, settled in 1974: Owen's widow Maude and sons Pip, Jimmy and Ted kept Royal Street; Ella, Dick, John, Adelaide and Dottie took Commander's Palace, bought in 1969. The day-precise 5 November is from one source and is best said as "November 1973". Brennan's of Houston (1967) stayed on Ella's side and has been run by Alex Brennan-Martin since 1982; a guest who ate at Brennan's in Houston ate at a cousin.
- (expert, correcting the workflow's draft) Michael Roussel started at Brennan's in 1956, trained under Blangé, rose from dishwasher to executive chef after 1977 and died in 2005, still in post, after 49 years. He did not retire. Lazone Randolph, a dishwasher from 1965, succeeded him and led the kitchen until the 2013 closure; Chicken Lazone is named for him. (search, Biz New Orleans) The kitchen in those decades was Black-led.
- (search, NPR; Wine Spectator; oeufetboeuf.com) Katrina never flooded Royal Street; the power was out for about a month and the cellar cooked. More than 35,000 bottles, valued at about one million dollars, were lost. The restaurant reopened in June 2006 (the day-precise 8 June is unconfirmed). The Wine Spectator Grand Award, held since 1983, lapsed and came back in 2021.
- (search, Aiken Standard obituary; seafoodsource.com; Fox News; WWL-TV) Jimmy Brennan died in 2010. The building went at a foreclosure sale in spring 2013; the restaurant closed on 28 June 2013; Ralph Brennan and Terry White bought the building and the business at auction; Pip Brennan's court bid to undo the sale failed. (expert) Keep the guest answer short and kind: the family business failed financially, a cousin bought it at auction and brought it back.

### The second life, 2014 onward

**One breath.** "It closed in 2013 after a family dispute. Ralph Brennan, Ella's nephew, and his partner Terry White restored it top to bottom and reopened in November 2014."

- (search, seafoodsource.com; restaurantbusinessonline.com; house press release "Restored, Reborn, and Reopening") More than six million dollars for the 16,000 square foot building, 17 months closed, every mechanical system replaced, two new kitchens added, an estimated 20 million dollars in all, 350 seats, interiors by Richard Keith Langham. Reopened 25 November 2014 per the house press release; one secondary source (Clio) says 27 November. Use the house date.
- (search, restaurant-hospitality.com; brennansneworleans.com) The Chanteclair Room, about 100 seats, replaced four smaller rooms and reads as a French orangerie opening onto the courtyard. The Morphy Room is an upstairs parlour. The Roost Bar carries a verre églomisé birdcage mural (ostrich, rooster, flamingo, hen), Julie Neill birdcages over the bar, and Robert Ortiz tables of crushed eggshell in resin. Lu Brow was the opening bar chef; no snippet names the bar lead today.
- (search, brennansneworleans.com/wine-room; Wine Spectator; explorelouisiana.com) The Wine Room and cellar occupy the old carriage house and stables behind the courtyard: a 16 foot table milled from a single sinker cypress pulled from a Mississippi bayou, sixteen leather chairs, a cellar of around 15,000 bottles (one year quoted 17,000) and about 1,400 labels, strong in Burgundy and Champagne. (pdf p49) Wine Spectator Grand Award cellar.
- (search, house press release "The Muthas & The Othas"; Axios New Orleans 1 May 2026) Ten turtles were found living in the courtyard during the 2014 renovation and adopted. Slade Rushing named them: five French mother sauces, Béchamel, Hollandaise, Espagnole, Tomate, Velouté; five New Orleans sauces, Rémoulade, Ravigote, Bordelaise, Mignonette, Cocktail. Cocktail is the only male. Their return to the fountain in 2015 began the annual Slowest Second Line on Earth; the 11th parade rolled Saturday 2 May 2026 from Bienville and Chartres with the Tidal Wave Brass Band. (expert) Say it before the guest asks: the turtle soup is not the turtles.
- (search, Wine Spectator; FSR; Wikipedia) Slade Rushing, of MiLa with his wife Allison Vines-Rushing (James Beard Rising Star Chef 2004), ran the kitchen from the 2014 reopening to 2019; the house counts five James Beard finalist nominations in those years. (search, fb101.com; StarChefs; brennansneworleans.com/awards) Ryan Hacker, with Texas roots, rose from the reopening line to executive sous chef, was named executive chef in November 2019 (announced February 2020), saw the house become a 2022 James Beard Outstanding Restaurant finalist, was a 2024 Louisiana Chefs to Watch pick, and left in spring 2025.
- (search, Axios New Orleans 4 June 2025; Country Roads; Poppy Tooker 30 May 2026; WhereYat) Kris Padalino became executive chef in June 2025 after eight years in the house as pastry chef and executive sous chef: Le Cordon Bleu Pasadena, kitchens in Los Angeles and Denver, a maths and chemistry background, a Sicilian family, an interest in Vietnamese flavours, and the house's own phrase, the first female executive chef in eight decades. She wants tableside cooking back ("we got away from that during covid") and bird-themed cocktails. (expert, inference only) The Italian grammar on the current menus, 'nduja, cured yolk, a potato cannoli, financiers, Grana Padano, fits a pastry-trained chef with Sicilian roots; nothing published says she wrote those dishes.
- (pdf p49, p50; search, brennansneworleans.com events pages; Axios 4 March 2026) The 80th year: a Friday 2 January 2026 kick-off in the courtyard with WDSU reviving the 1950s live noonday broadcasts; 80 dollar breakfast and dinner tastings through the year; a Celebration Tour (Ketchum 27 to 30 January, Washington DC 17 to 19 April, Reims 30 July to 2 August, New York 22 to 24 October, Charleston 13 to 15 November, Palm Beach in December), single-sourced to the house page; a Billecart-Salmon "Brennan's 80th Anniversary" Brut Sous Bois magnum at 350 dollars, a search price not in the PDF. (search, Axios New Orleans 6 April 2026; WTOP April 2026) Bananas Foster Day, 6 April 2026: a courtyard "banana bacchanal" from 4 pm, free tastings for the first 75 guests, a flame competition, a Banana King crowned, Champagne sabred, Richard Foster's grandsons present; then a Breakfast at Brennan's pop-up at The Occidental in Washington with Ralph Brennan and Chef Padalino. One verifier in this workflow could not re-check the party details, so treat them as reported.

### The building

**One breath.** "1795. It was Louisiana's first bank, then the home of Paul Morphy, the chess champion. Look for the bank's initials in the balcony ironwork."

- (expert; the day-precise dates could not be live-checked) Vincent Rillieux, great-grandfather of the painter Edgar Degas, bought the burnt lot in January 1795, a month after the great fire of December 1794, and built the house. José Faurie owned it from 1801 and sold it in 1805 to the Banque de la Louisiane, the first bank in Louisiana; the L B monogram survives in the balcony railing. Martin Gordon bought it in 1820; the Morphy family held it from 1841 to 1891. (search, Wikipedia mirrors) Paul Morphy, unofficial world chess champion 1858 to 1862, died there on 10 July 1884, aged 47, after a cold bath on a hot afternoon; the family sold in 1891. William Ratcliffe Irby gave the house to Tulane around 1920; Owen leased it from Tulane in 1954; the restaurant bought it in 1984 (expert, re-check the year).
- (expert, from memory of the 2014 reporting) The 2014 repaint used Benjamin Moore "Tomato Cream Sauce" for the stucco and "Mayonnaise" for the trim. (search, GitHub mirror of the Benjamin Moore database) Both are real colours, a salmon pink and an off-white. No source dates the first pink coat; say "as long as anyone remembers".

## 3. Tasting courses

### Baked Apple (breakfast tasting, course one; pdf p4)

**One breath.** "A whole baked apple with an oat, pecan and raisin crumble, a brown sugar glaze and a spoon of sweetened crème fraîche, that slightly tangy cultured cream."

**The story.** (pdf p4) Menu: oatmeal-pecan-raisin crumble, brown sugar glaze, sweetened crème fraîche; comes after the milk punch and before the soup. (expert) The French pomme bonne femme meets the British crumble. What the house makes is really a streusel: flour, butter and sugar rubbed together with oats and nuts, so it bakes crisp on top while the apple collapses beneath, and the brown sugar caramelises against the apple's juice; that is where the "glaze" comes from. (search, brennansneworleans.com/recipes/baked-apple) A house recipe page describes apples peeled, trimmed flat so they stand, cored with a melon baller, and topped with a pecan oat mix of flour, soft butter, light brown sugar, cinnamon, rolled oats, raisins and pecans. [unconfirmed: the page could not be re-read in this run; do not quote its quantities]

**Cook's notes.** (expert) Stand the apple on a trimmed base or it rolls. Bake until a skewer slides through with no resistance; hold warm and uncovered so the topping stays crisp. Quenelle the crème fraîche at the pass, off the heat side.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p4) Service note: tree nuts (pecans), dairy; oats may carry gluten, confirm. The $80 includes tasting portions and every drink.

**Ask the chef.** Which apple, and whether the tasting portion is a whole or half apple. If the recipe page's flour topping is what the kitchen uses, the gluten hedge becomes a plain yes.

### Petite Filet Mignon (breakfast tasting, course four; pdf p5)

**One breath.** "A small cut from the tenderest part of the beef, cooked to your temperature, on a crisp Swiss-style potato rösti with garlicky wilted spinach, and a pour of Côtes du Rhône."

**The story.** (pdf p5) Potato rösti, garlic spinach, 4 oz Domaine de Châteaumar "Cuvée Vincent" Côtes du Rhône. (expert) Filet mignon is the tapered end of the tenderloin, the least worked muscle, so the most tender and the least flavoured; the plate leans on the garlic spinach and the wine for savour. Rösti is the Bernese potato cake: raw grated for crispness, or par-boiled, chilled and grated the next day, which holds together and is the restaurant way. (search, Wikipedia) The Swiss German is [ˈrøːʃti], Röschti in Bernese, so the guide's RUHSH-tee is right; standard German is nearer RERS-tee. Do not "correct" the sh.

**Cook's notes.** (expert) Temperatures come from the ticket taken at the start of the tasting, so the fire is called by course. A 3 to 4 oz cut overcooks fast: sear hard, finish gently, rest two to three minutes. Rösti can be cooked in a sheet ahead, portioned and re-crisped to order. Spinach wilted à la minute with sliced garlic, squeezed, seasoned last so it does not go grey.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p5) Ask the temperature when taking the tasting order; likely dairy. The pairing matches the live breakfast page (search).

**Ask the chef.** Raw-grated or par-cooked rösti; the size of the tasting cut.

### Louisiana BBQ Lobster (dinner tasting, course two; pdf p5 to p6)

**One breath.** "Lobster in the New Orleans barbecue style, which is not a smoker or a tomato sauce but the buttery, peppery style barbecue shrimp made famous, over a white bean stew with 'nduja, en-DOO-yah, a spreadable spicy pork sausage from Calabria that melts into the beans."

**The story.** (pdf p5 to p6) Menu: 'nduja, white bean stew; Fichet "Château London" Mâcon-Igé 2024 on the pairing. (search, Rouses; WhereYat; Pascal's Manale history) Barbecue shrimp was born at Pascal's Manale, Uptown, in the mid-1950s, usually dated 1954: a regular, Vincent Sutro, described a butter and pepper shrimp dish from Chicago and Pascal Radosta recreated it; the name is usually explained by the brick colour of the sauce. (search, Wikipedia; nduja.info) 'Nduja comes from Spilinga in Calabria: pork trimmings and fatback with Calabrian chilli, often a quarter to a third of the mix by weight, which is why it spreads and why it is hot. (expert) There is no Escoffier sauce here. The New Orleans style is a pan emulsion, a beurre monté built on Worcestershire, black pepper, garlic and lemon. The white bean stew is a cassoulet idea; 'nduja rendered into warm beans gives fat, salt and chilli at once, the Calabrian cousin of andouille in red beans.

**The house sauce is its own thing.** (search, diningwithfrankie.com Dec 2016; johnsloanreviews.com March 2017) In the Rushing years the à la carte New Orleans BBQ Lobster was lobster on a crouton in a lobster-stock butter with smoked chilli butter, lemon confit and thyme, not the Manale shrimp sauce. Whether that is still the sauce under Chef Padalino is unknown. The guide's own instruction stands: confirm the kitchen's BBQ sauce at lineup (pdf p6).

**Cook's notes.** (expert) Blanch, shell and portion the lobster ahead, then finish it in the sauce so it never boils. The sauce is an emulsion; it breaks if it boils or sits. Render 'nduja gently, it scorches bitter. Beans loose and spoonable so the lobster sits on them.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p6) Shellfish, pork, dairy, moderate heat. Pairing disagreement: see section 2 (Matrot Meursault on the indexed page) [unconfirmed].

**Ask the chef.** Blanched and finished or cooked from raw in the sauce; whether the 'nduja is rendered into the beans or a finishing dollop; what is in tonight's BBQ sauce.

### Roasted Rohan Duck Breast (dinner tasting, course four; pdf p6 to p7)

**One breath.** "Rohan duck, a special cross raised in New York for its tender, rosy meat, roasted so the skin renders and the meat stays rosy, with peaches glazed in Louisiana cane syrup and candied hazelnuts."

**The story.** (pdf p6 to p7) Menu: cane syrup-glazed peaches, candied hazelnuts; Paul Hobbs Coombsville Cabernet 2021. (search, D'Artagnan; Tasting Table) Rohan is a proprietary hybrid created for D'Artagnan, Pekin and heritage Mallard with some Muscovy character, raised on a farm in New York State without antibiotics, named for the Rohan family of Brittany; the company's own words are tender, rosy and mild. So "breed" (pdf p7) is loose; say "a special cross". The menu does not name D'Artagnan, so a server does not volunteer it unless asked. (search, Wikipedia; Saveur) Cane syrup, Steen's of Abbeville being the classic since 1910, is cane juice reduced in open kettles with no sugar removed; it is sweeter, thinner and milder than molasses, so "molasses-like" (pdf p7) overstates it. Say "Louisiana cane syrup, a lighter, more buttery cousin of molasses". The guide never names Steen's; do not. (expert) The French family is canard aux pêches and canard à l'orange, with the cane glaze standing in for the gastrique; candied hazelnuts are a praline.

**Cook's notes.** (expert) Score the fat in a tight crosshatch without cutting the flesh, start skin down in a cold dry pan, pour off the fat as it renders, flip for a minute, finish in the oven to 52 to 54 C for medium-rare, rest five minutes, slice across the grain. If the dry-aged version is live it has lost water and cooks faster, so pull earlier. Peaches halved and glazed under the salamander so they colour without collapsing. Keep praline dry; humidity makes it sticky in an hour.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p7) Usually medium-rare to medium, confirm; tree nuts. The autumn version on the indexed page (hazelnut crust, sweet potato, winter squash, sage, duck jus) is [unconfirmed]; the guide itself expects a fall change.

**Ask the chef.** Which duck plate is live tonight; the temperature the kitchen serves; whether the breast is dry-aged.

## 4. Starters

### Blackberry Trifle, breakfast and lunch $14 (pdf p7 to p8)

**One breath.** "A layered trifle of blackberry curd, lemon curd, coconut chia pudding and little financiers, small buttery almond cakes; tart rather than sugary, lovely in the middle of the table while you wait for your eggs."

**The story.** (expert) The English trifle rebuilt in French pastry terms: curds for the custard, financiers for the sponge, chia pudding as the modern insert. Curd is yolk, sugar, fruit juice and butter cooked to a nappe. (expert) A financier is ground almond, egg white, sugar, a little flour and beurre noisette, baked in small moulds; the standard account credits the Paris pâtissier Lasne near the Bourse in the 1890s, who shaped them like gold bars for the financiers of the exchange. "Biscuits" on the menu is the house word for small bakes, not an American scone.

**Cook's notes.** (expert) Curds made the day before and set cold; chia needs at least four hours in coconut milk; financiers baked in the morning, the beurre noisette is what makes them taste buttery rather than cakey. Build to order in a glass, spoon not pour, so the curds do not streak. Cold starter, goes out fast.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p8) Coconut, eggs, dairy, gluten and likely almonds ("financiers are traditionally made with almond flour, confirm"); mention the seedy chia texture. (search, brennansneworleans.com) Listed at $14 as the guide prints.

**Ask the chef.** Are the financiers almond-based; built to order or pre-layered.

### Framboise French Toast, breakfast and lunch $16 (pdf p8 to p9)

**One breath.** "Framboise, frahm-BWAHZ, is French for raspberry: thick house citrus brioche griddled in butter, mascarpone mousse, a smooth raspberry coulis and local raspberries. Sold as a starter, made to share."

**The story.** (expert) Pain perdu, the French and Creole "lost bread": day-old bread soaked in egg and milk and fried in butter, with brioche the richest base. Mascarpone is a Lombard fresh cheese made from cream, so "whipped Italian cream cheese" (pdf p8) is a fair guest word; say mahs-kar-POH-neh, four syllables, never mars-ca-PONE. Coulis is a sieved fruit purée.

**Cook's notes.** (expert) Slice the brioche thick, about 2.5 cm, and let it dry a little or it soaks to mush. Soak, drain, griddle in clarified butter at medium so the centre sets before the outside browns. Pipe the mousse at the pass, not on the flat-top side; pass the coulis through a chinois, seeds read as careless.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p9) Gluten, eggs, dairy. (search, brennansneworleans.com) $16 with the same four components. No published review describes it.

**Ask the chef.** Nothing pressing; the pronunciation and the components are settled.

### Café Brûlot Coffee Cake, breakfast and lunch $14 (pdf p9 to p10)

**One breath.** "Café brûlot, ka-FAY broo-LOH, is New Orleans' after-dinner coffee flamed with brandy, orange and lemon peel, cinnamon and clove. The cake borrows those flavours: chicory coffee and bittersweet chocolate, pecans, a spiced citrus glaze and whipped crème fraîche."

**The story.** (expert) Antoine's credits Jules Alciatore, the founder's son and proprietor from 1887, with Café Brûlot Diabolique around 1890: a clove-studded spiral of orange peel, brandy and liqueur flamed and ladled down the peel into spiced coffee. The story that it hid brandy during Prohibition is tradition, not record. A burnt-brandy brûlot existed in Creole homes before Antoine's; the restaurant's contribution was the coffee, the bowl and the showmanship. Brûlot comes from brûler, to burn. (expert) The cake is a devil's-food idea where the liquid is chicory coffee, so coffee and chicory deepen the chocolate, with the brûlot spice carried in the glaze instead of the flame.

**Cook's notes.** (expert) Baked ahead; the only à la minute work is warming a slice if the house serves it warm, the glaze, pecans and a quenelle of crème fraîche. Keep the glaze thin enough to run.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p10) Pecans, gluten, eggs, dairy, caffeine. (search, brennansneworleans.com; Coolinary menu page) $14.

**Ask the chef.** Warm or room temperature; nothing else.

### Louisiana Oysters, breakfast and lunch $19 (pdf p10 to p11)

**One breath.** "Louisiana oysters in our New Orleans BBQ sauce, the buttery, peppery style, not a smoky one, with pickled collard greens for acid, shallots, and a fried saltine crumble for crunch."

**The story, with one correction.** (pdf p10) Menu: New Orleans BBQ sauce, pickled collard greens, shallots, fried saltine crumble. The 45-second line describes the style "made famous by barbecue shrimp: a rich, buttery sauce with lots of black pepper and savory Worcestershire". (expert) That is an accurate description of the Manale genre, and the guide's voice rules allow general truths about a technique; it is not a statement of the house recipe, and the workflow's draft was wrong to call it an invented house detail. What remains true is that the contents of Brennan's 2026 oyster sauce are unconfirmed, and the house's one documented BBQ sauce, on the 2016 to 2017 lobster, was a smoked chilli and lemon confit butter (search). Safe table line: "a buttery, peppery New Orleans BBQ sauce". Any Worcestershire or anchovy question goes to the kitchen, as the guide already directs (pdf p11). (expert) Pickled collards do the job a mignonette does; the saltine crumble is the raw bar's oyster cracker turned into a garnish.

**Cook's notes.** (expert) If poached in the sauce, the oysters go in last and come off when the edges curl, thirty to sixty seconds; a moment longer and they shrink and leak liquor into the emulsion, which thins it. If fried, dredge in seasoned flour or cornmeal, 180 C, ninety seconds. Collards cut fine at the pass; crumble added at the window or it goes soft.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p11) Shellfish, dairy, gluten (saltines); Worcestershire traditionally contains anchovy. No snippet says how the oysters are cooked or how many come per order.

**Ask the chef.** Poached, fried or broiled; count per order; what is in the house BBQ sauce.

### Creole Tomato Tostada, dinner $18 (pdf p11)

**One breath.** "Louisiana heirloom and Creole tomatoes, our summer tomato, with creamy burrata, a crisp tostada we make in-house with okra, and a clear, intensely tomatoey smoked tomato water poured over."

**The story.** (expert) A Caprese taken apart. Burrata is a Puglian mozzarella pouch filled with stracciatella and cream, so "mozzarella with a creamy centre" (pdf p11) is exactly right; say boo-RAH-tah. Tomato water is the clear juice that drips from salted, blended tomatoes hung in muslin overnight; smoking it is the modern turn. An okra tostada implies okra worked into a masa or batter and fried flat; no source explains the house method. (expert; search, LSU AgCenter) A Creole tomato is a vine-ripened tomato from the river parishes below New Orleans, Plaquemines and St Bernard above all; LSU released a cultivar named 'Creole' in 1969; the season runs late May to early July, and the first Creoles mark the start of summer on local tables.

**The story.** (expert) Sole Véronique is traditionally credited to Escoffier at the Carlton Hotel in London, named for André Messager's operetta Véronique, a Paris hit of December 1898. Say "traditionally credited to Escoffier" and frame it as a story. The classic is poached sole, the liquor reduced into a buttered or creamed sauce, glazed, with peeled muscat grapes. **Confirmed.** (pdf p25) Fish, dairy; paired on the tasting with Louis Jadot Beaune 1er Cru. (search, brennansneworleans.com dinner tasting page) Confirmed as the third course of the 80th anniversary dinner tasting; a Restaurant Week appearance is also reported [unconfirmed].

### Gulf Fish en Papillote, dinner $38 (pdf p26)

**One breath.** "En papillote, ahn pah-pee-YOHT, means in paper; here the wrapper is a banana leaf, so the fish steams in its own juices with Louisiana crab, tomato, Castelvetrano olives, the bright green, mild, buttery Sicilian ones, Marcona almonds and sorghum, a chewy ancient grain. If it is opened at the table, mind the steam."

**The story.** (expert) Antoine's credits Jules Alciatore with Pompano en Papillote in honour of the Brazilian aviation pioneer Alberto Santos-Dumont, the puffed parchment standing for his balloon; whether he was physically in New Orleans is sometimes questioned, so say "in honour of". Cooking in paper is older French practice; Antoine's gave the city a famous dish, not the method. The guide's gloss "sealed in a wrapper" is fair but not literal: a papillote is a twist of paper, the frill on a cutlet bone, from papillon, butterfly. (expert) The banana leaf is softened over a flame, holds moisture like paper and adds a faint grassy, tea-like aroma; the parcel bakes at about 200 C for 10 to 15 minutes. Sorghum is an African grain cooked like farro, naturally gluten-free. Castelvetrano olives are the Nocellara del Belice cultivar from the Valle del Belice in Trapani province, western Sicily, lye-cured briefly and kept in light brine, which keeps them green and mild.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p26) Know today's fish; fish, shellfish, tree nuts; sorghum is gluten-free but confirm the whole dish; ask whether it is opened at the table and warn guests about the steam.

**Ask the chef.** Who opens it, and where.

### Poussin à la Moutarde, dinner $29 (pdf p26 to p27)

**One breath.** "Poussin, poo-SAN, is a young, small chicken, and we use a Cornish hen, as the menu says: brined in sweet tea, fried crisp, with glazed turnips and shallots and a mustard cream sauce. Southern fried chicken meets a French bistro. One of the best values on the dinner menu."

**The story.** (expert) The EU standard calls a poussin a bird under 750 g; the American Rock Cornish game hen is an immature chicken of five to six weeks at up to 2 lb, a little larger than a strict poussin but the nearest US equivalent, and the printed menu names the Cornish hen, so there is no sleight of hand. Poulet à la moutarde is the Burgundian bistro classic. A sweet tea brine is a Southern fried chicken brine: strong sweet tea with salt; the sugar and tannin help browning. Mustard goes into the cream sauce off the heat, because it loses its bite if it boils.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p27) Poultry, dairy, mustard, likely gluten in the coating; a whole small bird, may have bones.

### Blackened Tofu, dinner $25 (pdf p27 to p28)

**One breath.** "Tofu made here in New Orleans, blackened, the Louisiana technique of coating in spice and searing in a very hot pan that Paul Prudhomme made famous in 1980, with rice grits from a family farm in the Mississippi Delta, broken rice cooked creamy like grits, and preserved shiitakes, shee-TAH-keh, for deep savour."

**The story.** (expert; search, Magic Seasoning Blends) Blackening is Paul Prudhomme's invention at K-Paul's Louisiana Kitchen, opened July 1979; blackened redfish is dated to 1980: fillets dipped in melted butter, coated in a paprika-led blend with cayenne, peppers, thyme, oregano, garlic and onion, seared in a dry cast-iron skillet heated past smoking. The craze strained Gulf redfish stocks. So "the Louisiana technique" is accurate but young, and the butter in the method is exactly why the guide warns about vegans (pdf p28). (pdf p27; search, brennansneworleans.com) Both print "New Orleans Tien Dat tofu"; no search found the maker. (expert) New Orleans East and the Westbank have Vietnamese tofu makers with no web presence, so a real local maker is plausible. Say only "made here in New Orleans" until the kitchen supplies one sentence. (search, Farm Progress; MSU Extension; sunbeltexpo.com) Two Brooks Farm, Sumner, Mississippi Delta: Mike Wagner started it in 1992, his children Abbey and Lawrence now hold key roles, the farm floods its fields for wintering ducks and geese and farms with that rhythm, sells 14 products including rice, flours and grits, and Wagner was Mississippi's 2022 Sunbelt Ag Expo Farmer of the Year. Shiitake: shii (the shii tree, on whose logs it was grown) plus take, mushroom; the common error is shih-TAH-kee.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p28) Soy; ask whether it is fully vegan and whether the rice grits use stock; moderate spice. (pdf p47) The vegetarian-at-dinner script: tostada, then the tofu, with smoked cauliflower on the side, confirming butter and stocks.

### Creole Hanger Steak, dinner $42 (pdf p28 to p29)

**One breath.** "The dinner hanger: a Creekstone Farms hanger in our house Creole spice, with Madeira-glazed broccolini, Madeira being the fortified wine, a little sweet and nutty, a persillade, pair-see-YAHD, of chopped parsley and garlic with pepitas, pumpkin seeds, for crunch, and a chilli crisp, the crunchy Chinese chilli oil, which brings real heat."

**The story.** (expert) Persillade is the French last-second pan finish of parsley and garlic; pepita is Mexican Spanish for the hulled pumpkin seed; chilli crisp is the Guizhou condiment of chilli flakes fried in oil with garlic, often soybeans and sesame, made famous by Lao Gan Ma. Madeira is the Portuguese fortified wine deliberately heated as it ages, so it tastes of caramel and walnut; a glaze is Madeira reduced with butter or stock. A French garnish, a Mexican seed and a Chinese condiment on a Creole-spiced steak is the modern Brennan's idiom and worth saying at the table.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p29) Medium-rare to medium; warn spice-shy guests and offer the chilli crisp on the side if the kitchen agrees; pumpkin seeds; the chilli crisp may contain soy or sesame. (pdf p48) The breakfast hanger has béarnaise and an egg; the dinner hanger has chilli crisp and no egg.

**Ask the chef.** What is in the house Creole spice; soy or sesame in the chilli crisp.

### Cherries Jubilee, $14 at both meals (pdf p34 to p35)

**One breath.** "Older than Bananas Foster: Escoffier is credited with cherries jubilee for Queen Victoria's jubilee in the 1890s. Ours is fresh tart black cherries in a warm Luxardo cherry sauce, flamed at the table over our vanilla ice cream. Brighter and fruitier than the Foster; for the table, have both."

**The story.** (expert) Credited to Escoffier for one of Victoria's jubilees; the Diamond Jubilee of 1897, when he ran the Savoy kitchens, fits better than the Golden Jubilee of 1887. Escoffier's Guide Culinaire (1903) recipe is stoned cherries poached in syrup, the syrup bound with arrowroot, served in small timbales with a tablespoon of warmed kirsch lit at the table: kirsch, and no ice cream. The ice cream is a later, largely American habit, and Brennan's Luxardo sauce is a cherry-on-cherry update. (expert, correcting the workflow's draft) Luxardo was founded in 1821 in Zara, then Austrian Dalmatia and now Zadar in Croatia, by Girolamo Luxardo, building on the rosolio maraschino his wife Maria Canevari made at home; the Zara distillery was destroyed in the Second World War and Giorgio Luxardo re-founded the firm at Torreglia in the Veneto in 1947; the company grows its own marasca cherries there. The house makes both the liqueur and the candied marasca cherries in syrup, and the menu's "Luxardo sauce" does not say which. (expert) Luxardo Maraschino is bottled at 32 per cent and lights more gently than kirsch or brandy at 40; lower proof also means more alcohol can survive in the sauce, which matches the guide's own warning.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p35) Same safety steps as Foster; cherries may contain pits, confirm; dairy; alcohol not fully burned off; no minimum printed, confirm. (pdf p48) Foster versus Jubilee: rich and caramel versus tart and fruity.

**Ask the chef.** Which Luxardo product is in the sauce (Maraschino liqueur, Sangue Morlacco, or the cherry syrup) and which spirit is lit; can it be made for one; "black" cherries are usually sweet and "tart" ones usually red, so which variety.

### New Orleans Bread Pudding, $14 at both meals (pdf p35)

**One breath.** "New Orleans' comfort dessert: bread soaked in sweet custard and baked soft inside and golden on top, with a whiskey caramel, buttermilk ice cream for a little tang, and pralines, PRAH-leens, the New Orleans candy of pecans, butter and brown sugar."

**The story.** (expert, correcting the workflow's draft) Bread pudding is a medieval European thrift dish for stale bread that became a Louisiana Creole staple. The Picayune's Creole Cook Book (1901) gives bread pudding as its own baked recipe; its pain perdu is a separate French toast dish and should not be cited for bread pudding. The warm whiskey sauce is a 20th-century New Orleans restaurant signature, and Brennan's version uses a whiskey caramel, a caramel loosened with cream and whiskey, which is more stable in service than the classic butter, sugar, egg and bourbon emulsion. New Orleans bread pudding traditionally uses the airy local French loaf, which drinks custard without turning to paste. (expert) The praline takes its name from the Maréchal du Plessis-Praslin, whose 17th-century cook (named Clément Lassagne in English sources, Jaluzot in some French ones) made sugar-coated almonds; the Ursuline nuns, who reached New Orleans in 1727, are credited by tradition with bringing it; Louisiana cooks swapped pecans for almonds and made a soft, creamy patty; in the 19th century it was sold on the street by Black women vendors, the pralinières. In New Orleans the word is PRAH-leen; PRAY-leen marks you as from elsewhere; the guide's PRAW-leen is the same local vowel.

**Confirmed.** (pdf p35) Gluten, eggs, dairy, tree nuts (pecans), whiskey in the caramel. (pdf p47) Watch the whiskey for guests who avoid alcohol. (search, brennansneworleans.com) $14.

**Ask the chef.** Which whiskey; which bread; raisins or not.

## 10. The sauces in one place (expert unless marked)

- **Hollandaise.** Warm emulsion of yolk and clarified butter sharpened with lemon or reduced vinegar; the mother. On Hussarde and Owen.
- **Béarnaise.** Hollandaise built on a tarragon, shallot and vinegar reduction; Collinet, 1836, Saint-Germain-en-Laye. On the breakfast hanger.
- **Choron.** Béarnaise plus reduced tomato; Alexandre Étienne Choron, Voisin, Paris. On the Sardou. (pdf p50) "tangy and herbal".
- **Foyot, or Valois.** Béarnaise plus glace de viande; Restaurant Foyot, rue de Tournon, Paris. On the Chateaubriand. (pdf p50) "béarnaise made even richer with a reduced beef glaze".
- **Marchand de vin.** Classical: shallots reduced in red wine with demi-glace. New Orleans, per Brennan's own cookbook: a roux-based brown sauce with ham, mushroom, onion, garlic, stock and red wine. Say "a savoury red wine and mushroom sauce" (pdf p2); drop "reduction" (pdf p17).

## 12. Flambé: the chef's tableside procedure and safety

(pdf p34) The guide's seven steps are the house's own and are learnt word for word: cart placement, measure the rum into a small vessel first and never pour from the bottle near a flame, pull the pan off the flame to add rum then tilt the far edge towards the flame to ignite, never add alcohol to a lit pan, keep a lid in reach, let the flame burn out before plating, turn the burner off before you leave the cart.

(expert) What the steps assume and do not say:

- The flame burns the alcohol vapour above the pan, not the liquid. An 80 proof spirit flashes at about 26 C, so a spirit straight from a cool bar may not catch; a few seconds' warming lets it light at once. If it boils, vapour comes off in a rush and the pan flares high. If the pan is far too hot the alcohol boils off before you can light it. Overproof rum has no place at a guest's table.
- In a bright room an ethanol flame is nearly invisible. This matters most at breakfast, when the Foster closes the $80 tasting (pdf p49). Treat the pan as lit until it has clearly died down. A lid is the emergency stop; never water.
- Mise en place on the guéridon, gay-ree-DOHN, the cart: burner, flambé pan, butter, brown sugar, cinnamon in a shaker, liqueur and rum pre-measured in jiggers, bananas cut, ice cream scooped at the last moment into chilled bowls, long spoon and fork, lid or damp cloth. Sequence: butter, sugar to a bubbling blond caramel, cinnamon, liqueur off the flame, bananas cut side down and turned once, pan off the burner, measured rum, back on and tilted to catch, let it burn down, plate bananas over ice cream, then sauce.
- Dusting cinnamon into the flame throws a shower of sparks; it is long-standing Foster showmanship, and the guide never mentions it. Confirm the house practice at training rather than improvise.
- Alcohol: flaming removes only part of it. The USDA nutrient-retention table gives about 75 per cent retained for flamed dishes. The guide's p34 note ("flambéing doesn't remove all the alcohol") is right; its p47 script ("most of it burns off, but not all of it") is too generous. Better: "Only a small part burns off, most of the rum stays in the sauce, so I would steer you to the Lemon Tart or the Pineapple Tarte Tatin."
- Burns: cool running water for twenty minutes, no ice, report to the manager.

## 16. For Lizzy, the maître d' agent: what generalises

(expert) Four habits of this guide that any house can copy: hold a dated must-know sheet separate from the menu, because the menu changes with the season and the history does not; keep every dish and every history line in three lengths, one breath, one minute, and the long version (pdf p2); mark every fact with its source and date so a server never says "the website says" when the website has moved on; keep the short list of questions guests actually ask (the age of the house, the family, the dessert, the closure, the turtles, is the turtle real) and drill those.

## For the app

### What should become structured records

Five record types carry everything above. Each carries the same provenance block, so Lizzy can build the same thing for any restaurant from a menu plus a server guide, and so the Bartender's Ledger and the Sommelier's Codex can share the spirits and wine rows.

- `label`: one of `pdf`, `search`, `expert`
- `source`: the page for pdf, the site for search, `professional knowledge` for expert

### Rules the app should enforce

- A fact with `status: corrected` stores only the corrected text; the refuted wording is never shown to a server.
- A fact with `status: unconfirmed` or `disputed` renders with a visible marker and is excluded from any drilled "say it" line.
- A price renders with its `read_on` date and never without one.
- A dish whose `disagreements` array is non-empty shows a "confirm on shift" badge on the pairing or component in question.
- An open question that `blocks_line` hides that line until `answered` is true.
- The maître d' agent (Lizzy) builds a new restaurant by filling the same five record types from that house's menu and guide, with `label: pdf` for the guide, `label: search` for anything found live, and `label: expert` for professional background, never presenting an expert line as a fact about that restaurant.

## For the app
Five record types, all carrying one provenance block (label: pdf | search | expert; source; source_date; status: holds | disputed | unconfirmed | corrected; confidence: high | medium | low). A refuted claim is stored only as its correction with status corrected; unconfirmed and disputed facts render with a visible marker and are excluded from drilled lines; every price carries its read_on date (the guide's menus: 2026-09-26).

1. Dish record (one per menu item and tasting course): id, restaurant_id, name, name_as_printed, menus [{menu: breakfast_lunch | dinner | breakfast_tasting | dinner_tasting | bubbles, course_position, price {amount, currency, as_printed, read_on}}], menu_line_as_printed, components [], main_ingredient, technique, sauce, key_flavours, accompaniments, on_the_palate, lines {ten_seconds (the one-breath line), twenty_seconds, forty_five_seconds}, story {classical_foundation, origin, house_variation, cook_notes} each provenance-tagged, pronunciations [{term_id, say_it}], service_notes_pdf {allergen_flags [], alcohol, temperature_ask, tableside, minimum_order, other} copied from the guide only, pairings [{wine_id, pour_oz, context: a_la_carte | tasting, glass_price, disagreement}], sourcing_names [producer_id], facts [{text + provenance}], disagreements [{topic, pdf_says, search_says, resolution}], ask_the_chef [], ask_the_sommelier [], season_note, last_verified. Example: Eggs Hussarde, breakfast_lunch $27 (read 2026-09-26) and breakfast_tasting course 3; menu line as printed; lines in three lengths; origin fact labelled search (Garden & Gun, TasteAtlas); the marchand de vin wording fact labelled expert; a disagreement row for the tasting Champagne (pdf: Charles Lafitte Brut 4 oz; search: Brennan's Essential by Piper-Heidsieck; resolution: confirm on shift); ask_the_chef entries for ham and mushroom in the sauce and for permission to say 'created here'.
