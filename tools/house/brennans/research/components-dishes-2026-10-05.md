# Components, the kitchen: research log for the dish components, 5 October 2026

A top-level research file for tools/house/brennans/components/dishes.json, the master chef's breakdown of every
dish on the Brennan's menus into its ingredients, techniques and stories, with a comparison for each dish. Sixty
web searches were run (the cap). Each entry below gives the query and what the results showed, with the URLs the
results named. Where a search did not settle a fact, the component leaves it out or makes it a question for the
chef. Facts already held in research/dossier-the-kitchen.md, research/master-review-chef-2026-10-04.md and
research/summary-keyfacts.md are cited there and not repeated as searches. (expert) in a component's sources
means classical professional knowledge, never a statement about the Brennan's recipe.

## The searches

1. Brennan's history, Owen Brennan, 1946, Bourbon Street, 417 Royal Street, 1956.
   Showed: founded 1946 by Owen Brennan as the Vieux Carré restaurant on Bourbon Street across from the Old
   Absinthe House; in 1954 a lease dispute led Owen to plan the move to Royal Street; Owen died in 1955; Ella
   Brennan and her siblings completed the move and the restaurant opened at 417 Royal Street in 1956.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brennan%27s ; https://neworleanshistorical.org/items/show/1502 ;
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Brennan_(restaurateur)

2. Bananas Foster, 1951, Richard Foster, crime commission, Ella Brennan, Paul Blangé.
   Showed: in 1951 Ella Brennan and chef Paul Blangé adapted a dish of caramelised bananas Ella's mother made at
   home; named for Richard Foster, chairman of the New Orleans Crime Commission and Owen Brennan's friend;
   created while the restaurant was still Owen Brennan's Vieux Carré; New Orleans was the largest port of entry
   for bananas from Central and South America; the sauce is butter, brown sugar, cinnamon, dark rum and banana
   liqueur. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bananas_Foster ; https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2016/09/30/493157144 ;
   https://www.frenchquarter.com/bananas-foster-in-the-french-quarter/

3. Eggs Hussarde origin, Brennan's, marchand de vin, Holland rusk.
   Showed: popularised at Brennan's from about 1950, but a "Poached eggs a la Hussarde" recipe was printed in
   Boston in 1895, credited to chef Adolphe Gallier of the Hotel Brunswick in New York, and a version is in
   Escoffier's Le Guide Culinaire (1903). Classic build: Holland rusks (or English muffins), Canadian bacon,
   marchand de vin, poached eggs, hollandaise; hussarde from hussar, the Hungarian light cavalry.
   https://www.barrypopik.com/index.php/new_york_city/entry/eggs_hussarde ; https://www.tasteatlas.com/eggs-hussarde
   Conclusion: never say "invented here"; say "the Brennan's signature, popularised here".

4. Eggs Sardou, Antoine's, Victorien Sardou.
   Showed: created at Antoine's and named for the French playwright Victorien Sardou; one account dates his
   visit to 1892 (the dossier gives 1908 as the usual date, so no date is printed); the original was poached
   eggs, artichoke hearts, ham, anchovies, truffle and hollandaise; most later recipes swap these for creamed
   spinach. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eggs_Sardou ; https://www.tastingtable.com/997200/

5. 417 Royal Street, 1795, Banque de la Louisiane, Paul Morphy, LB monogram.
   Showed: built 1795 for Don José Faurie; later the Banque de la Louisiane, the first bank in Louisiana; the
   Morphy family owned it 1841 to 1891 and Paul Morphy, unofficial world chess champion, lived there until his
   death in 1884; the bank's initials LB survive in the centre of the balcony railing.
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brennan%27s ; https://neworleanshistorical.org/items/show/800

6. Brennan's closure 2013, reopening November 2014, Ralph Brennan, Terry White.
   Showed: closed June 2013; Ralph Brennan, a cousin of the former operators, and his partner Terry White
   bought the building at a foreclosure auction and later the name; reopened November 2014 after a restoration
   (the house press release says 16 months; the dossier says 17; no month count is printed); interiors by
   Richard Keith Langham. https://www.brennansneworleans.com/brennans-news/restored-reborn-and-reopening/

7. Marchand de vin sauce, New Orleans.
   Showed: French for wine merchant; classically shallots reduced in red wine (older versions with marrow); in
   New Orleans the shallot becomes onion and garlic, ham often joins, with beef broth; Antoine's made it famous
   in the city. http://www.jfolse.com/recipe_week/recipe0209/WAFB_021009beef.html ; https://www.lostdishes.com/riddles/marchanddevin.htm

8. Sauce Choron, Alexandre Choron, Voisin.
   Showed: Alexandre Étienne Choron (Caen, 1837 to 1924), chef de cuisine at Voisin on the rue Saint-Honoré,
   created Choron sauce, béarnaise enriched with tomato; also remembered for the Christmas 1870 menu during the
   Siege of Paris. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_%C3%89tienne_Choron

9. Sauce Foyot, Valois, Restaurant Foyot.
   Showed: béarnaise with meat glaze, also called sauce Valois; Restaurant Foyot at 33 rue de Tournon, Paris,
   named for Nicolas Foyot, chef to King Louis-Philippe, who bought it after 1848; closed 1937 (the dossier says
   1938, so no closing year is printed); Léopold Mourier, from 1891, created many dishes bearing its name.
   https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauce_Foyot ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restaurant_Foyot

10. Béarnaise origin, 1836, Collinet, Pavillon Henri IV, Saint-Germain-en-Laye.
    Showed: credited to Jean-Louis-François Collinet, reportedly for the 1836 opening of the Pavillon Henri IV
    at Saint-Germain-en-Laye; hollandaise built with white wine vinegar, shallot, chervil and tarragon; named
    for Béarn, the home region of King Henri IV. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A9arnaise_sauce

11. Hollandaise history, mother sauce.
    Showed: an emulsion of egg yolk, melted butter, water and lemon juice or a vinegar reduction; lecithin in the
    yolk binds it; the name means Dutch sauce with no clear Dutch link; Escoffier listed it among the five mother
    sauces in the 1907 English edition of Le Guide Culinaire. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollandaise_sauce ;
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_mother_sauces

12. Beurre blanc, Clémence Lefeuvre, Nantes.
    Showed: credited to Clémence Lefeuvre at a riverside inn at Saint-Julien-de-Concelles, east of Nantes on the
    Loire, in the later nineteenth century (dates differ, so none is printed); the legend says it was a béarnaise
    made without yolks or tarragon; served with river fish. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beurre_blanc ;
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cl%C3%A9mence_Lefeuvre

13. Sole Véronique, Escoffier, Carlton, Messager.
    Showed: credited to Auguste Escoffier at the Carlton in London, named for André Messager's comic opera
    Véronique; sole with a creamy sauce and peeled green muscat grapes. Dates differ (1898 versus the London run),
    so none is printed. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%A9ronique_(operetta)

14. Cherries jubilee, Escoffier, Queen Victoria, 1897.
    Showed: generally credited to Escoffier for one of Queen Victoria's jubilees, most often the Diamond Jubilee
    of 1897; his recipe lit a spoonful of warmed kirsch in each timbale; no ice cream in the original.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherries_jubilee

15. Luxardo, 1821, Zara, Torreglia, 1947.
    Showed: Girolamo Luxardo founded the distillery in Zara on the Dalmatian coast in 1821, built on the marasca
    cherry; after the Second World War Giorgio Luxardo re-founded the firm at Torreglia, Italy, in 1947.
    https://imbibemagazine.com/luxardo-maraschino-liqueur/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maraschino

16. Chateaubriand origin, Montmireil, 1822.
    Showed: thought to have been created by a chef called Montmireil around 1822 for the writer and diplomat
    François-René, Vicomte de Chateaubriand; the centre cut of the tenderloin, traditionally cooked between two
    lesser cuts that were thrown away; about 2 pounds and 3 to 4 inches thick in the American trade.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chateaubriand_(dish)

17. New Orleans turtle soup history.
    Showed: a Creole soup from the French colonial era; snapping turtle, dark roux, beef stock, trinity, tomato,
    Worcestershire, finished with sherry, lemon and hard-boiled egg; many soups are stretched with veal or beef;
    real turtle today comes from licensed farms. https://louisianacookin.com/creole-turtle-soup/ ;
    https://www.foxnews.com/food-drink/brennans-turtle-soup.print

18. Creole tomato, Plaquemines, St. Bernard, LSU AgCenter.
    Showed: farmers in St. Bernard and Plaquemines parishes marketed vine-ripened tomatoes from rich alluvial soil
    as Creole; LSU AgCenter also bred a variety named Creole (1970 in one article; the dossier says 1969, so no
    year is printed); today "Creole" is a marketing term for Louisiana-grown tomatoes; the season depends on the
    weather. https://www.lsuagcenter.com/profiles/lbenedict/articles/page1469026887857 ;
    https://countryroadsmagazine.com/cuisine/Louisiana-foodways/the-truth-about-creole-tomatoes/

19. Leidenheimer Baking Company, 1896.
    Showed: started 1896 by George Leidenheimer from Deidesheim, Germany, first on Dryades Street, on Simon
    Bolivar Avenue from 1904; began with dense German loaves, then a light, crackly French bread around 1905;
    the city's best-known maker of po-boy bread. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leidenheimer_Baking_Company

20. Andouille, Louisiana, LaPlace.
    Showed: a coarse-grained smoked pork sausage, French in origin, shaped in Louisiana by German and French
    butchering traditions along the River Parishes; LaPlace calls itself the Andouille Capital of the World;
    Cajun andouille is smoked over pecan wood and sugar cane. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andouille ;
    https://www.eatbayou.com/the-history-of-andouille-in-louisiana/

21. Tasso.
    Showed: a smoked, spiced, cured pork from south Louisiana, made from the shoulder, not the leg; used diced as a
    seasoning meat in jambalaya, gumbo, red beans and sauces. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasso_ham

22. Holy trinity, Paul Prudhomme, mirepoix.
    Showed: onion, celery and green bell pepper, the Cajun and Creole version of mirepoix with bell pepper for
    carrot; the phrase was most likely popularised by Paul Prudhomme; the name nods to the Catholic faith of
    early settlers. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_trinity_(cooking) ; https://www.lsuagcenter.com/articles/page1621432197656

23. Gumbo etymology.
    Showed: most likely from a West African word for okra (ki ngombo, gombo); okra from West and Central Africa,
    filé from the Choctaw, roux from France (darker in Louisiana). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gumbo ;
    https://www.southernfoodways.org/interview/a-short-history-of-gumbo/

24. Louisiana popcorn rice, 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline.
    Showed: the popcorn-like aroma of Louisiana aromatic rice is 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline, the same compound as in
    basmati and jasmine, at high levels. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1990.tb03961.x

25. Rohan duck, D'Artagnan.
    Showed: a proprietary hybrid including the heritage Mallard and the Pekin, raised for D'Artagnan on a farm in
    New York State (the Catskills), to about 11 weeks, without antibiotics; rosy, tender, mild meat; named for the
    Rohan family of Brittany. https://www.tastingtable.com/685236/dartagnans-rohan-ducks-hit-the-market/

26. Duck confit, Gascony.
    Showed: a preserving technique of south-western France (Gascony): duck salted with garlic and herbs up to 36
    hours, poached slowly in its fat, stored under the fat; confit from confire, to preserve.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duck_confit

27. Blackened redfish, Paul Prudhomme, redfish ban.
    Showed: blackened redfish appeared at K-Paul's around 1980 (sources give 1980 and 1981); spices and a
    very hot cast-iron pan stood in for a grill; the craze led Louisiana to ban commercial redfish fishing in the
    late 1980s. https://www.foodrepublic.com/1631780/origin-why-redfish-banned/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Prudhomme

28. Pompano en papillote, Antoine's, Santos-Dumont.
    Showed: Jules Alciatore at Antoine's created pompano en papillote for a banquet honouring the Brazilian
    balloonist Alberto Santos-Dumont; the puffed parchment suggests a balloon.
    https://www.kitchenproject.com/history/Fish/Fish-En-Pappilote/index.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine%27s

29. Barbecue shrimp, Pascal's Manale, 1954.
    Showed: barbecue shrimp is credited to Pascal's Manale in 1954 per the family cookbook; a customer described a
    buttery shrimp dish from Chicago; head-on shrimp in butter, black pepper, Worcestershire, lemon and garlic,
    never barbecued. https://www.whereyat.com/new-orleans-barbecue-shrimp ; https://www.rouses.com/about/blog/barbecue-shrimp/

30. 'Nduja, Spilinga.
    Showed: a spicy, spreadable pork sausage from around Spilinga in Calabria: trimmings and fatback with
    sun-dried Calabrian chilli; soft as a pâté at room temperature; melts into sauces. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%27Nduja

31. Cane syrup, Steen's, Abbeville, 1910.
    Showed: Steen's has made cane syrup in Abbeville since 1910; cane juice cooked down in open kettles with
    nothing removed, so sweeter than molasses, from which sugar has been taken out.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steen%27s_cane_syrup

32. Grand Isle Jewels.
    Showed: a brand launched in April 2025 by JEDCO, the Jefferson Parish Economic Development Commission, with
    the Grand Isle off-bottom oyster farmers; oysters raised in cages above the seafloor, cleaner and gentler on a
    storm-damaged coast. https://louisianaradionetwork.com/2025/04/23/grand-isle-oyster-farmers-launch-grand-isle-jewels/ ;
    https://jedco.org/jedco-reveals-grand-isle-jewels-off-bottom-oyster-brand/

33. Fresno chilli, 1952, Clarence Hamlin.
    Showed: released by Clarence Brown Hamlin in 1952 and named for Fresno, California; 2,500 to 10,000 Scoville,
    the jalapeño's band. Nothing on mignonette. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresno_chile

34. Shrimp and grits, Charleston, Bill Neal, Crook's Corner.
    Showed: a Lowcountry fishermen's breakfast; recipes in Charleston print by the 1930 book Two Hundred Years of
    Charleston Cooking; Bill Neal's version at Crook's Corner (Chapel Hill) was published by Craig Claiborne in
    The New York Times in 1985 and went national. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crook%27s_Corner_(restaurant) ;
    https://charlestonmag.com/features/shrimp_grits

35. Debris, Mother's Restaurant, 1938.
    Showed: debris (DAY-bree) is the roast beef that falls into the gravy as it roasts and is carved; Mother's on
    Poydras Street, opened 1938 by Simon and Mary Landry, made the debris po-boy famous.
    https://www.eatbayou.com/what-is-debris-style-roast-beef/ ; https://mothersrestaurant.net/

36. Creekstone Farms, hanger steak.
    Showed: founded 1995 by John and Carol Stewart as a Black Angus farm in Kentucky; processing plant in Arkansas
    City, Kansas, since 2003; 100% Black Angus; hanger is also called butcher's steak, hanging tender, onglet.
    "One per animal" was not shown by this search (it is professional knowledge, marked expert).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creekstone_Farms_Premium_Beef

37. Vital Farms, 108 square feet.
    Showed: started 2007 near Austin, Texas, with 20 hens; pasture-raised means at least 108 square feet of outdoor
    space per hen; works with hundreds of family farms. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vital_Farms ;
    https://vitalfarms.com/learn/faqs/are-vital-farms-eggs-still-pasture-raised/

38. Two Brooks Farm, Sumner, Mississippi.
    Showed: a rice farm in Sumner, Mississippi, started by Mike Wagner in 1992; children Abbey and Lawrence in key
    roles; 14 products including rice grits and rice flour; ducks and geese winter on the flooded fields.
    https://www.farmprogress.com/conservation-and-sustainability/sustainability-plays-crucial-role-at-two-brooks-rice-farm

39. Mimolette.
    Showed: a cheese of Lille and the north of France, said to have been made at Louis XIV's request under
    Colbert's policies to replace Dutch Edam; orange from annatto; cheese mites on the rind help it age.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimolette

40. Abita Amber.
    Showed: Abita was founded in 1986 in Abita Springs, Louisiana, by Jim Patton and Rush Cumming; Amber was its
    first beer and is the flagship, a Munich-style lager of pale and caramel malts with German Perle hops.
    https://abita.com/brew/amber/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abita_Brewing_Company

41. Nueske's, Wittenberg, 1933.
    Showed: founded 1933 by R.C. Nueske in Wittenberg, Wisconsin; family recipes from before the family came to
    America; applewood smoked for at least 24 hours. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nueske%27s_Applewood_Smoked_Meats

42. Succotash, Narragansett.
    Showed: from the Narragansett word msickquatash, boiled corn kernels; a Native American dish of corn and beans
    eaten long before colonists arrived. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Succotash

43. Brabant potatoes.
    Showed: cubed potatoes blanched, fried and tossed in butter, garlic and parsley; called Louisiana fries; on
    the menus of traditional Creole restaurants such as Galatoire's. https://beyondgumbo.com/2023/08/13/brabant-potatoes-a-new-orleans-classic/

44. Rice dressing versus dirty rice.
    Showed: both carry the trinity; the line between them is drawn differently across Louisiana, one account
    giving ground beef or pork to dressing and livers and gizzards to dirty rice, another the reverse; "may
    depend on your area code". https://www.theadvocate.com/acadiana/entertainment_life/food/dirty-rice-or-rice-dressing-in-louisiana/

45. Pralines.
    Showed: named for the Maréchal du Plessis-Praslin, whose cook sugared almonds; the Ursuline nuns, in New
    Orleans from 1727, are credited by tradition with bringing it; pecans replaced almonds; the pralinières were
    women, many free women of colour, who sold them on French Quarter streets in the nineteenth century.
    https://leahspralines.com/pages/history-of-pralines ; https://lsupress.org/recipe-pralines/

46. Bread pudding and whiskey sauce.
    Showed: a European thrift dish for stale bread; New Orleans cookbooks carry it from at least 1885; the city's
    French bread stales fast and soaks well; boozy sauces took off with a restaurant revival from the 1970s.
    https://64parishes.org/entry/bread-pudding

47. Tarte Tatin.
    Showed: named for Stéphanie and Caroline Tatin of the Hôtel Tatin at Lamotte-Beuvron; the accident story is a
    legend and the tart may be an improved tarte solognote; Maxim's in Paris made it famous.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarte_Tatin

48. Financier.
    Showed: brown butter, egg white, flour and ground almond; attributed to a pâtissier named Lasne near the Paris
    Bourse around 1890, shaped like a gold ingot for stock traders; descended from the nuns' visitandine.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financier_(cake)

49. Café brûlot diabolique, Antoine's.
    Showed: created at Antoine's by Jules Alciatore in the late nineteenth century; coffee, brandy, orange and
    lemon peel, cinnamon and clove, flamed and ladled at the table. https://www.neworleans.com/drink/cocktails/cafe-brulot-diabolique/

50. Chicory coffee, New Orleans.
    Showed: roasted chicory root stretched coffee in French practice during the Napoleonic blockades and in New
    Orleans, famously during the Civil War blockade; kept for its mellow, roasty taste.
    https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/chicory-coffee-mix-new-orleans-made-own-comes-180949950/

51. Crawfish pie, Natchitoches meat pie.
    Showed: the Natchitoches meat pie is a fried hand pie of seasoned pork and beef, documented by 1826 and made an
    official state food in 2003; crawfish pies are made as hand pies or full pies.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natchitoches_meat_pie ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crawfish_pie

52. Crystal hot sauce.
    Showed: made by the family-owned Baumer Foods in New Orleans since 1923 from aged red cayenne peppers,
    distilled vinegar and salt. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_Hot_Sauce

53. Castelvetrano olives.
    Showed: the Nocellara del Belice variety from the Valle del Belice in Sicily; bright green, buttery and mild;
    lye-cured to remove bitterness. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nocellara_del_Belice

54. Marcona almond.
    Showed: a Spanish variety, shorter, rounder, softer and sweeter than Californian almonds; usually blanched,
    fried in oil and salted. https://despanabrandfoods.com/products/marcona-fried-almonds

55. "Maggie's Mushrooms" Brennan's.
    Showed: a menu listing of Maggie's Mushrooms as a $12 side at Brennan's; Mushroom Maggie's Farm in St
    Francisville, Louisiana, run by Maggie Long and Cyrus Lester, grows lion's mane, oyster, pink trumpet,
    shiitake and chestnut mushrooms for restaurants; the link between the two is a search summary's inference and
    stays a question for the chef. https://mushroommaggiesfarm.com/ ; https://www.wafb.com/2020/01/23/showcasing-louisiana-mushroom-maggies-farm/

56. Tien Dat tofu.
    Showed: a Vietnamese co-op making extra-firm tofu sold at the Crescent City farmers markets; Tien Dat Tofu LLC
    registered in Louisiana with an address in Marrero, on the West Bank across the river from the city.
    https://www.marketumbrella.org/news/529/88/March-22-2021 ; https://opengovus.com/louisiana-corporation/40269041K

57. Rémoulade, New Orleans versus French.
    Showed: French rémoulade is white, mayonnaise with mustard, pickles, capers and herbs; the New Orleans Creole
    version is spicier and pinker, built on Creole mustard with paprika and hot sauce, with or without mayonnaise.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remoulade

58. Caesar salad, Cardini, Tijuana, 1924.
    Showed: Caesar Cardini's restaurant in Tijuana, 1924; romaine, raw egg yolk, olive oil, Parmesan,
    Worcestershire, lime, toasted bread; Cardini relied on the anchovy in the Worcestershire rather than whole
    anchovies. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar_salad

59. Pimento cheese.
    Showed: began in New York around the 1870s as Neufchâtel with Spanish pimientos; the South made it with
    cheddar and mayonnaise and made it its own. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pimento_cheese

60. Poussin and the Rock Cornish game hen.
    Showed: a poussin is a chicken under 28 days, about a pound; the USDA defines the Rock Cornish game hen as an
    immature chicken under five weeks and not over 2 pounds, so it is the nearest American equivalent, larger
    than a British poussin. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poussin_(chicken)

## Corrections the searches forced on earlier drafts

- Eggs Hussarde: printed in the 1890s and in Escoffier, so Brennan's popularised it; the components never say
  the dish was invented here.
- Eggs Sardou: the date of Sardou's visit is given as 1892 by one source and 1908 by the dossier; no date is used.
- Beurre blanc, Sole Véronique, Foyot's closing, the Creole tomato cultivar and the reopening's month count each
  carry conflicting dates; none of those dates is printed.
- Blackened redfish: 1980 and 1981 both appear; the component says "around 1980".

## Repairs after the refuter (4 Oct, late)

- Roux: research/dossier-the-kitchen.md summarises Brennan's published turtle soup recipe as a dusting of flour cooked five minutes, then beef stock, which is not a dark roux, and leaves the build to the chef. The roux component and the Turtle Soup comparison now give the dark roux as the Creole tradition and send ours to the chef. For the gumbo, the same dossier quotes the Gambit relaunch review, "thick with dark roux", and the comparison attributes that to the reviewer.
- Bananas Foster: created by Brennan's in 1951 on Bourbon Street (bananas-foster-story), not at 417 Royal.
- Petite Filet: the sear, gentle finish and rest are the classic method; no house source prints the kitchen's.
- Breakfast sides card: now a factual question on the printed prices (guide pdf p29 to p31).
- Eater video: the title says Brennan's invented Eggs Hussarde; its why now says the title overstates (the 1895 recipe in hussarde-story).
