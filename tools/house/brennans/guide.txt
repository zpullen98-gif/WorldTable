 1. The server formula
 Every description in this guide is built from the same five parts, always in the same order. Learn the order once and you
 can describe any dish on any menu.

     1 Main ingredient                         2 Technique         3 Sauce & key flavors            4 Accompaniments

     5 How it tastes


 1. Main ingredient                                What the guest is eating, named plainly and with its origin when the menu gives one —
                                                   “Gulf redfish,” “100% turtle meat,” “coffee-cured Canadian bacon.”

 2. Technique                                      How it’s cooked — seared, poached, braised, flambéed, slow-cooked. Only what the
                                                   menu says or clearly implies; never an invented brine time or temperature.

 3. Sauce & key flavors                            The sauce or the flavor that defines the plate — hollandaise and marchand de vin, brown-
                                                   butter vinaigrette, aged Sherry. The sauce usually matters more than the protein, for the
                                                   guest and for the wine.

 4. Accompaniments                                 What comes with it on the plate — the garnish, the side, the bread.

 5. How it tastes                                  Texture and flavor in plain words — rich, bright, crisp outside and tender inside, smoky,
                                                   sweet-tart. This is the line that makes a guest want it.


 Three lengths, one formula
 10 seconds · ≤ 25 words                           For the order or a quick question. The dish name, the main ingredient and one hook (the
                                                   sauce or the signature detail). Parts 1 and 3, sometimes 5.

 20 seconds · ≤ 50 words                           What you say as the plate lands, or when a guest asks “what’s that?” Parts 1 to 4 in one or
                                                   two sentences, ending on the flavor.

 45 seconds · ≤ 110 words                          The “tell me about this dish” answer. All five parts: what it is, how it’s made, what each
                                                   component does, why it’s special at Brennan’s (the history or the house detail), and a
                                                   gentle recommendation.


 The voice rules
        Speak as yourself to the guest: warm, confident, plain English, first person (“I,” “we,” “our”), present tense.
        Explain any French or Creole term right after you use it — “marchand de vin, a red wine and mushroom sauce.”
        Describe what the menu says plus what is true of the technique in general. Don’t invent house details, farm names or
        recipes.
        Lead with the dish, end with the taste. Recommend gently — “if you like…, you’ll love…” — never push.
        Allergens, rare cooking, bones, heat, alcohol in a dish and tableside fire go in your head every time (see each dish’s
        service note).

 2. The pairing formula
 Each wine was chosen from Brennan’s own by-the-glass list by checking the dish against these principles, in roughly this
 order.

 Acid                                              The wine should be at least as tart as the dish — lemon, tomato, vinaigrette and
                                                   mignonette all need a high-acid wine.

 Fat                                               Butter, cream, hollandaise and fried food want acidity or bubbles to cut through and
                                                   refresh.

 Weight                                            Light dishes with light wines, rich dishes with fuller wines — neither should bury the
                                                   other.

 Sweetness                                         The wine must be at least as sweet as the dish, or it tastes thin and sour. Desserts need
                                                   dessert wines.

 Heat                                              Spice wants low alcohol, low tannin and a touch of sweetness; high alcohol makes heat
                                                   burn.

 Tannin                                            Tannic reds love protein and fat (steak); keep them away from oily fish and heavy spice.

 Salt & umami                                      Salt loves acid, bubbles and sweetness. Umami softens with fruit and clashes with big
                                                   tannin.

 Bridge                                            A shared flavor — herbs, citrus, smoke, mushroom, butter, toast — ties the dish and the
                                                   wine together.

 Method & region                                   Grilled and smoked dishes take more body than poached ones; French-Creole food often
                                                   loves French wine.


 How each pairing is written
 Why                                               One sentence: the principle that makes the match work.

 Say it                                            The suggestion as you’d make it at the table — one or two sentences, never pushy.

 When asked “why this wine?”                       The sommelier’s explanation in plain words.

 On the palate                                     What actually happens when the guest tastes them together — for your understanding.

 Second choice                                     A different style for a guest who wants something else.

 Serve / avoid                                     How to pour it, and the style that would clash.
